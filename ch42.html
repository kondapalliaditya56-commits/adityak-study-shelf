Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • Federation never started: 50% of Princes had to sign the IoA (Section 6); threshold never met
  • Dyarchy at the Centre, not in provinces: 4 Reserved subjects (Defence, External Affairs, Ecclesiastical, Tribal); Finance and Home were Transferred
  • Provincial autonomy from 1937; Federal Court 1937; RBI (RBI Act 1934, operational 1 April 1935) is NOT a GOI 1935 provision
  • FA 1/3 Prince nominees and indirect; CoS direct and permanent; 10% voters; women's derived vote and reserved seats
  • 1937: INC majority in 6, ministries in 8 of 11; Punjab = Unionist Party (Sikandar Hayat Khan); Constituent Assembly idea: M.N. Roy 1934, Swaraj Party May 1934

Mains hooks

  • Why the British passed GOI 1935: pressure from below and legal consultations above (250-word FYQ on the board)
  • How the Act was a trap: strong Centre, powerful Governor, Divide and Rule inside INC
  • How GOI 1935 helped the Constituent Assembly (Mains PYQ): lists, Federal Court, Governor, responsible government

Federation and the Centre stayed on paper; only the provincial half ran from 1937.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: The British did not give GOI 1935 out of generosity. Linlithgow36-43 called it the best way to maintain control, and it grew out of pressure from below (revolutionaries, Anti-Simon, Civil Disobedience, the Great Depression) and consultations above (Simon, three Round Tables). Its federal Centre needed 50% of the Princes to sign, so it never started and the 1919 machinery kept ruling the Centre. Only the provincial half worked from 1937: dyarchy ended and Congress took 8 of 11 provinces for 28 months, which gave it experience, energised the Princely States and tested the British plan to split INC.

  1. A candid Viceroy. Linlithgow36-43 implemented the Act in 1937 and admitted it was the best way to maintain control over India. It runs to 321 sections. Nothing in it was a gift of self-government.
    → But why did Britain bother at all?
  2. Pressure from below. Revolutionaries (HRA1924, HSRA1928, Chittagong Armory Raid 1930-33), the masses (Anti-Simon 1928, Civil Disobedience 1930-34) and the Great Depression of 1929 kept Britain nervous. The board calls reform a safety valve on a pressure cooker of Indians.
    → So Britain also needed a legal route.
  3. Consultations above. The Simon Commission (1927, no Indian member) reported in June 1930: provincial autonomy, nothing at the Centre, more franchise, separate electorates, Burma out. Three Round Table Conferences followed (1930, 1931, 1932).
    → But Indian demands were rejected, so the Act was against self-government.
  4. The Act of August 1935. The Act has two halves. The Centre half proposed a Federation of British India and Princely States with dyarchy at the Centre. The provincial half proposed autonomy with a still-powerful Governor.
    → So what stopped the Centre half?
  5. The Princes say no. The Federation needed at least 50% of Princes to sign the Instrument of Accession (Section 6). They feared two bosses, losing financial autonomy, low representation and democracy. The threshold was never met.
    → Now the Centre stayed on the old Act.
  6. The Centre stays on paper. The Act of 1919 kept governing the Centre. The Federal Court was set up in 1937, but the Federal Assembly, Council of States and dyarchy at the Centre never began. The Viceroy kept 80% of the Budget non-votable.
    → But the provinces were different.
  7. Provinces go live in 1937. Dyarchy ended and all provincial subjects became transferred. A Premier and Council of Ministers became responsible to the Assembly. Orissa, Bihar and Sindh became provinces (1936) and Burma was separated (1937).
    → But the Governor was still powerful.
  8. A strong Governor. Forty percent of the provincial Budget was non-votable. The Governor could summon the Assembly, veto bills, issue ordinances, run tribal areas, protect minorities and British interests, and take over the government indefinitely.
    → Now who benefits from this design?
  9. The British plan. Hold the Centre, hand over provinces and keep INC busy governing. Provincial leaders would become autonomous power centres, so Divide and Rule would be applied to INC itself. INC rejected the Act and demanded a Constituent Assembly by Universal Adult Franchise.
    → So INC still decided to contest.
  10. Congress ministries 1937-39. INC contested for the same reason as the Pro-Changers of 1923: occupy the space and do Constructive Work, now with executive power. It won a majority in 6 provinces and formed ministries in 8 of 11. Punjab went to the Unionists and Sindh to the Sind United Party.
    → So what did 28 months change?
  11. The impact. Morale of cadre, peasants and workers rose, INC gained governance experience, and Praja Mandals or State People's Congresses spread in Princely States. The Chamber of Princes (1921) was the British answer from above; the ministries energised the answer from below.

In simple words#

  • Why the Act: the British wanted to keep control, not share it. Linlithgow36-43 said so openly.
  • Two halves: the Centre half (Federation, dyarchy) never started; the provincial half ran from 1937.
  • The Federation trap: it needed 50% of the Princes to sign, and they refused, so the old 1919 Centre continued.
  • Provinces: dyarchy ended, ministers answered to the Assembly, but the Governor kept big powers and 40% of the Budget was non-votable.
  • Voting: only about 10% could vote; women's vote came through the husband; communal seats grew to new groups through quotas within quotas.
  • INC's answer: reject the Act, demand a Constituent Assembly, yet contest 1937 and rule 8 of 11 provinces for 28 months.
  • Legacy: three lists, Federal Court, Governor's take-over and tribal areas show up later in the Constitution of 1950.

0 · The chapter on one board#

Every constitutional reform had a cause, and GOI 1935 is the last big one before Independence. Ask: who pushed the British, and who did the Act really serve?

1 · Why Was the GOI Act 1935 Passed?#

Story link

Because Britain wanted to keep control while easing pressure, the Act was a manoeuvre, not a gift. Ask: what exactly was the pressure?

  • 1.1 GOI Act 1935 did not come from any genuine British desire to give Indians self-government.
  • Linlithgow36-43 (Viceroy 1936 to 1943, who implemented the Act in 1937) was candid: the Act was the best way to maintain control over India.
  • That is the honest summary of a 321-section Act.
  • 1.2 The circumstances were two kinds of pressure: pressure from below and pressure from legal consultations above.
  • 1.3 Pressure from below came from every direction:
    • Revolutionaries: HRA1924, HSRA1928 and the Chittagong Armory Raid of 1930-33 kept the British nervous
    • Masses: Anti-Simon agitations 1928 and Civil Disobedience Movement 1930-34
    • Great Depression 1929: mass economic suffering and deeper anger against British rule
  • Every section of society (youth, peasants, workers, capitalists) and even World History (GD1929) fed the pressure that produced GOI 1935.
  • 1.4 Legal basis was built through the stages that follow (Section 2).
Teacher's board
  • Pressure from below = CDM and consultations above = RTC (board under 1.2).
  • The board joins GOI 1919 with GOI 1935 as two reforms born from pressure on Britain.
Handout alert
  • Handout alert (Prelims 2018): GOI Act 1935 proposed Provincial Autonomy and a Federal structure. The PYQ listed with this alert in the companion is on residuary powers (answer: Governor General); see the PYQ table.

2 · Legal Basis#

Story link

Because Britain needed a legal route that looked consultative, it sent a commission and then called conferences. Ask: did any of them listen to Indians?

2.1 Simon Commission Report (June 1930)

  • The seven-member Simon Commission had no Indian member. It recommended:
    • i) Provincial Autonomy: abolish dyarchy (GOI 1919) and give full responsible government in provinces, with some emergency powers kept by the Governor
    • ii) No change at the Centre: the Centre stayed under British despotism
    • iii) Expansion of franchise: wider electorate, but far short of Universal Adult Franchise
    • iv) Retention of separate electorates: the communal electorate system continues
    • v) NWFP: keep it a Chief Commissioner's Province with a limited representative legislature and no responsible government
    • vi) Separation of Burma from India
Teacher's board
  • All seven Commission members were British MPs.
  • NWFP: the Round Table process recommended upgrading it to a Governor's Province; implemented in April 1932 with the extension of dyarchy under GOI 1919. The board reads this as a full province with dyarchy, so the governing Act is GOI 1919.
  • Sind: the Simon Commission wanted detailed inquiry first. The board says this is the same as the MLN Report 1928.
  • Boundaries Commission: the board compares it with the 1953 State Re-organisation Commission and notes 1912: Bihar & Orissa.
  • Burma: the board adds the Third Anglo-Burmese War (Dufferin 84-88) beside the two wars in the handout.
  • NWFP detail: the RTC process upgraded it to a Governor's Province, implemented April 1932, together with the extension of dyarchy under the GOI Act 1919.
  • Sind: the Commission expressed sympathy with separation from Bombay but deferred a final decision pending inquiry into financial consequences. After the First RTC the Sind Financial Enquiry Committee (the Miles Irving Committee, 1931) estimated an initial annual deficit of about Rs 1.10 crore.
  • Broader reorganisation: a neutral Boundaries Commission to examine reorganisation of provinces, with Sind and Orissa as prominent cases.
  • Burma: the 1st Burma War (1824-26) and 2nd Burma War (1852-53) led to annexation; it was finally separated from India in 1937.

Teacher's board
  • 1st RTC: the board writes the Princes' demand as made under the influence of some powerful pro-united India Princes.
  • For Prelims 2009 the board adds 1932 and White Paper contributes: the 3rd RTC fed the White Paper that fed the Act.

2.3 Thus GOI Act 1935 Was Against the Principle of Self-Government

  • i) No Indian in the 7-member Simon Commission (1927).
  • ii) INC boycotted the 1st and 3rd RTCs; the 2nd RTC was a failure.
  • iii) The Nehru Report of August 1928, merely a wish of the All Party Conference, stayed unfulfilled.

3 · Territorial and Administrative Reorganisation#

Story link

Because the Act gave provinces real power, their borders had to be redrawn first. Ask: which new provinces appear and when?

  • 3.1 Full province status to Orissa (1936) and Bihar (1936), separating them from the combined Bihar & Orissa province created in 1912.
  • Sindh separated from Bombay and made a full province (1936).
  • 3.2 NWFP: its full-province status (created 1932, raised from Chief Commissioner's Province to Governor's Province) was reaffirmed.
    • In 1932 it got only a limited legislative council of about 40 members with dyarchy-type arrangements; the Governor kept strong reserve and veto powers and the executive was not responsible to the council
    • Full responsible government came only with GOI 1935 from 1937: dyarchy abolished, a proper Legislative Assembly, and a Premier with a Council of Ministers responsible to the PLA
  • 3.3 Burma was separated from India in 1937. United Provinces as a name was formalised in 1937.
Teacher's board
  • Orissa, Bihar, Sindh and Burma all change under GOI 1935.
  • NWFP: a full province, but now no dyarchy (GOI 1935).
  • The board writes undivided Awadh 1801 and 1856 and then 1877, 1902, 1937 beside the United Provinces name: check your notebook for what each year marks.

4 · Features at the Centre: The Part That Never Happened#

Story link

Because the Princes would not sign, the Centre half of the Act never started. Ask: what did the Act plan, and why did the Princes say no?

Teacher's board
  • The board splits the Act into Sec 4: provisions at the Centre (not applied, except some) and Sec 5: provisions at Provinces (applied 1937). Provinces here do not include Princely States (PS).
  • Everything at the Centre under GOI 1935 stayed on paper. The central federal provisions never became operational and the GOI Act 1919 continued to govern the Centre. Hold on to this.

4.1 A Federation of British India and the Princely States

  • India was to become a Union of British India and Princely States with federal relations between the two (this mirrored the Nehru Report of August 1928).
  • This required at least 50% of Princes to sign the Instrument of Accession (IoA) as defined in Section 6 of GOI 1935. That threshold was never met.
  • The Butler Committee of 1927 had promised no change in the status of Princely States without the Princes' consent, and the Princes used the promise as cover to stay out.
  • After signing the IoA a Princely State would transfer only Defence, Foreign Affairs and Communications to the Union. This is structurally like Jammu & Kashmir signing only an IoA in 1947 and not a full merger; Art. 370 is that legacy.
Teacher's board
  • CoI creates a State for India. GOI 1935 proposes a Central legislature with seats for 565 Princely States as well.
  • Pre-condition for the Act to come into effect = creation of a United India. Only then could the Centre make laws for the whole of India, drawing members from British India and the 565 PS. This United India was never created.
  • All sections that mention the whole of India or the 565 PS = null and void; sections about provinces = valid.
  • Rules and People: Rules (T) of 1935 cover the whole of India (British India plus 565 PS). From 1861 to 1919 the rules were for British India only.
  • Condition for the whole of India: at least 50% of Princes must agree to join by signing the IoA (Sec 6). The reason, on the board: the promise of the Butler report. An example of DnR: Princes happy, British India sad.
  • Never done: British India: Centre stays with GOI 1919 and Provinces move to GOI 1935.
  • On signing the IoA = Art. 370-like status. Imagine 565 Art. 370s. Like the MLN Report of Aug 1928: only D, C, FA with the Centre; the rest, including domestic affairs, with the Prince.
  • IoA decides the distribution of subjects between the Centre and a Princely State (board, Democracy in GOI 1935).
Handout alert
  • Handout alert: the handout says the Butler Committee of 1927; the board writes Butler Report 1929 for the same promise. Use the handout for the Committee (1927); the board's 1929 is the report date, so check your notebook.
  • Reserved list on the board: the teacher writes D, C, FA / Tribal Affairs / Church beside the Reserved subjects. D, C, FA is the IoA trio (Defence, Communications, Foreign Affairs). The typed four Reserved subjects are Defence, External Affairs, Ecclesiastical, Tribal; use the typed four.
  • Why the Princes refused to sign:
    • i) Paramountcy was not abolished by GOI 1935; Princes faced two bosses, the Union Government and the British Crown, and wanted neither
    • ii) Financial autonomy: once inside the Union, the GOI could compel Princes to contribute to the Defence Budget (for example, force them to fund the army)
    • iii) Low representation: smaller Princely States felt they would get little say in the proposed legislatures
    • iv) Fear of democracy: once the States joined the Union, INC could launch movements inside those States too; as one country the rules of political activity would change entirely

Teacher's board
  • Financial autonomy for a Prince: with only paramountcy and no IoA, the subsidy (the defence contribution) follows that State's own arrangement. Under GOI Act 1935, defence follows India's needs, and nobody knows how much each State would be made to share.
  • Fear of democracy: the board notes INC can support the AI-SPC (1927), which works better if India is one.

4.2 A Bicameral Legislature at the Centre

  • The proposed Central legislature: Federal Assembly (FA) and Council of States (CoS). Under GOI 1919 the equivalents (CLA and CoS) were only for British India, not the Princely States.
  • i) One-third of FA members were Nominees of the Princes, not elected. This practically ended the chance of an INC majority in the more powerful lower house.
  • ii) Elections to the CoS were direct (for better representation of regions and communities); elections to the FA were indirect, specifically to limit INC strength in the more powerful house.
  • Logic: regional parties do better than all-India parties when elections are indirect, so INC would win fewer FA seats.
  • iii) CoS: permanent, one-third retiring every third year (COI1950: one-third every 2 years). FA: 5-year life (CLA under GOI 1919: 3 years).
Teacher's board
  • Democracy in GOI 1935 (synonym: responsible government): Centre = partial responsible government (dyarchy), only for British India; Provinces of British India = 100% fully responsible government; 565 PS = no responsible government.
  • FA pie: the Princely States held one-third of FA seats, set against their much smaller population share (figures on the board: check your notebook). In a democracy seats are distributed as per population.
  • Voter behaviour: in state (MLA) elections regional parties do better (voters choose a CM); in Lok Sabha elections all-India parties do better (voters choose a PM).
  • Today: Lok Sabha is directly elected and more powerful; Rajya Sabha is chosen by MLAs. 1935 was the reverse: FA indirect and CoS direct.
  • 1967: elections at the Centre and the provinces together (one nation, one election); INC lost heavily for the first time and the era of regional parties began.

4.3 Right to Vote: Expanded but Restricted

  • Franchise extended, but income and education criteria meant only 10% of the population qualified. Intent: deny the vote to INC's base of poor peasants and workers.
  • Women's franchise was derived, not independent:
    • A woman could vote on her husband's income or property, with a lower financial threshold than the one for men
    • A widow could vote on property or income left by the dead husband
    • Qualifications were defined in the Sixth Schedule and varied by province; in many provinces literacy alone was enough for a woman, while men without property often needed middle school or matriculation
Teacher's board
  • R2V (right to vote) ladder: LP1916: franchise as broad as possible. GOI 1919: R2V linked with income; first time a woman's vote is linked to her income.
  • MLN Report Aug 1928: first time UAF (Universal Adult Franchise) demanded, by the All Party Conference.
  • GOI 1935: R2V linked with income and/or education; women's vote linked to the husband's income. Logic: patriarchal society: less women's employment and inheritance not going to daughters.

4.4 Representation: Continuation and Expansion of Communal Politics

  • i) Separate electorates for all minorities continued as under GOI 1919 (and as per the Communal Award, August 1932).
  • ii) Scheduled Castes got reservation in elected seats in the FA, CoS and PLAs. Under GOI 1919 reservation for depressed classes was only in nominated seats, so this was a step forward.
  • iii) Reservation for women and labour/workers was also provided.
Teacher's board
  • First time: the phrase Scheduled Castes (1935) replaces Depressed Classes (1919) (Dalits). PLC was technically renamed PLA.
  • Women and labour: first time. Women's seats are a quota within quota; labour seats are separately reserved.
  • Elected majority since 1919 at the Centre and provinces.
  • Poona Pact, September 1932 (starred): about 147 seats in PLC and 18% in CLA. The board adds that the share of elected seats now far exceeds the share of nominated seats, so check your notebook for the exact figures.

4.5 First Federal Distribution of Subjects

  • For the first time a proper federal distribution of subjects into three lists: Federal, Concurrent, Provincial (ancestor of the 7th Schedule of COI1950).
  • Under GOI 1919 the Centre outsourced some subjects to provinces but constitutional ownership stayed with the Centre. Under GOI 1935 provinces received constitutional ownership of their subjects, so 1935 marks the peak of federalism in British India; the journey began in 1861 and peaked in 1935.
  • For Princely States that signed the IoA only three subjects (Defence, Communications, Foreign Affairs) went to the Centre, structurally like Art. 370. The Motilal Nehru Report of August 1928 had a similar federal distribution.
Teacher's board
  • The three lists cover British India, not 565 PS.

4.7 Dyarchy at the Centre

  • Ironically, dyarchy applied in provinces under GOI 1919 was now proposed at the Centre. Federal subjects had two legal categories: Reserved and Transferred.
  • Reserved subjects (strictly 4): Defence, External Affairs, Ecclesiastical Affairs (Church), Tribal Areas. Under the absolute legislative and executive control of the Viceroy, with up to 3 Counsellors not responsible to the legislature. Link: 5th Schedule of COI1950 for tribal areas.
  • Transferred subjects: all other Federal List subjects, including Finance and Home, run by the Viceroy on the aid and advice of a Council of Ministers responsible to the FA and CoS. This made only a partially responsible government at the Centre.

4.8 Fiscal Autonomy to GOI from the Secretary of State

  • Financial control over British India moved from the Secretary of State for India (SoS) in London to the Viceroy in India.
  • Since GOI 1858 the SoS was the top authority on Indian finances; that is why Morley made his famous Budget speech in 1906.
  • Nehru called this the only major change in the whole Act.

4.9 Federal Court

  • The Act provided for a Federal Court, the first court with jurisdiction over disputes between provinces and between provinces and the Centre. It was set up in 1937, ancestor of the Supreme Court under COI1950.

4.10 Reserve Bank of India

  • Not a provision of GOI 1935. The RBI was established under the RBI Act 1934 and became operational on 1 April 1935, timed with the wider constitutional and financial restructuring of the mid-1930s.
  • It was set up as the central banking authority to manage currency and credit, a prerequisite for the fiscal federalism GOI 1935 envisaged.

5 · Features at the Provinces: The Part That Did Work (from 1937)#

Story link

Because the Centre stalled, the real change fell on the provinces. Ask: how democratic was provincial autonomy, really?

  • 5.1 Provincial Autonomy: the provincial provisions came into force in 1937. The federal distribution of subjects and the end of dyarchy made all provincial subjects transferred, under PLAs and popular ministers.
  • The Governor was expected to act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. The Governor is the Centre's agent, so 1919 dyarchy was anti-democratic and also anti-federal. Federalism began in 1861 and peaked in 1935.
  • 5.2 Fully Responsible Government: dyarchy ended; a Council of Ministers headed by a Premier (Chief Minister) became responsible to the PLA, genuine responsible government at the provincial level for the first time.
    • Direct elections and separate electorates continued as under GOI 1919
    • Some provinces got an Upper House (a representation mechanism for one administrative level below the legislature)

6 · The Evolution of Voting Rights and Communal Representation (1909-1935)#

Story link

Because Britain wanted to add new groups without upsetting fixed communal ratios, it built compartments inside compartments. Ask: what is the difference between a separate electorate and a reserved seat?

6.1 The Core Definitions

ConceptMeaning
Why it confusesOne of the most misunderstood areas of modern Indian constitutional history: textbooks blur reserved seats and separate electorates
Separate ElectorateThe voter pool is strictly segregated by identity: only voters of a community vote for that community's candidates (only Muslims voting for a Muslim candidate)
Reserved SeatA seat is guaranteed for a group, but who votes for it can vary: the candidate pool is restricted, the voter pool might not be

6.2 Phase 1: The 1909 Act (Birth of Separate Electorates and the Double Vote)

  • Policy: the Indian Councils Act of 1909 formally introduced Separate Electorates for Muslims.
  • Reality, the Double Vote: rolls were not mutually exclusive. Qualified Muslims voted in the new separate Muslim constituencies and also in the standard General constituencies.

6.3 Phase 2: The 1919 Act (Watertight Compartments)

  • Policy: GOI 1919 adopted the Hindu-Muslim compromise of the 1916 Lucknow Pact. Muslims surrendered the Double Vote in exchange for a guaranteed quota of seats and acceptance of separate electorates.
  • Reality: legally watertight rolls: a special minority roll (like the Muslim roll) or the General roll (everyone else, predominantly Hindus).
  • Rule of exclusion: cross-voting was banned. A Muslim voter had no right to vote for a General seat, and a General voter had none for a Muslim seat.

6.4 Phase 3: The 1935 Act (Expansion and Internal Carve-Outs)

  • The Act took the watertight compartments of 1919 and expanded them to new groups using combinations of reservation and separate electorates.
  • i) Women (communal segregation through internal carve-outs): reserved seats for women were given without altering any religious community's share.
    • Step 1, the quota: the British first fixed the absolute number of seats per community to keep the communal balance (for example, Muslims get exactly 30% of seats in a province)
    • Step 2, the carve-out: inside that quota, they reserved some seats for women. If the Muslim quota was 30 seats, 2 could be reserved for Muslim women, leaving 28 standard Muslim seats and 2 Muslim Women's seats. The same rule applied to the General (Hindu) quota
    • Result: the community's total representatives stayed untouched; the only losers were the men of those communities, who had slightly fewer seats to contest
    • Voting clarification: these seats were not a gender-exclusive electorate. Men and women both voted, but the watertight rule applied: only Muslim men and women vote for the Muslim Women's seat, and only General men and women for the General Women's seat
  • ii) Labour (special constituencies): true separate electorates; seats allocated to specific registered Trade Unions or factory groups; only qualified workers in those groups vote.
  • iii) Differential franchise: to raise the number of women voters without universal adult suffrage, the bar was lowered for women. In many provinces literacy alone qualified a woman, while propertyless men typically needed middle school or matriculation.
Teacher's board
  • ICA 1909: separate electorates to Muslims.
  • GOI 1919: separate electorates to Muslims, Sikhs, Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans in India.
  • GOI 1935: same as 1919 but new quotas of seats. For women: quota within quota. The board's example: a circle of Muslim (M), Sikh (S) and Christian (Chr) seats; the General half has its own women's seats voted by the unreserved category, while the seat reserved for Muslim women sits inside the Muslim quota and its voters are Muslim men and women.

6.5 Summary Takeaway

  • From 1909 to 1935 the system moved from overlapping voting rights to a rigidly divided society where religion or class decided which box a citizen was locked into.
  • General did not mean open to everyone; it meant those left over after all separate electorates were carved out.
  • This was the British way to grant special representation to new groups without upsetting the fiercely negotiated communal ratios between Hindus, Muslims and Sikhs.
