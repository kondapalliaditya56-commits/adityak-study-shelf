1 · Introduction and the problem of defining corruption#

Corruption is as old as civilisation. Kautilya's Arthashastra lists many ways in which a civil servant can be corrupt and gives specific measures to contain it. It is part of governance everywhere, differing only in degree, and has touched almost every field of human effort (it contributed to the decline of empires and to reforms in religion).

Is a gift a bribe?

The handout gives six factors to weigh:

  • Intent of the donor.
  • Expectation of the recipient.
  • Timing of the gift.
  • Value of the gift.
  • The country's legal framework.
  • Social acceptability of the gift.
Gift or bribe
IETVLS

Intent, Expectation, Timing, Value, Legal framework, Social acceptability: "Is Every Token Valued Like a Sale?"

Cultural differences

What counts as corruption varies by culture.

Only public bodies, or the private sector too?

Include both sectors

  • Many argue corruption covers public and private sectors, but the counterargument is that if the net is cast too wide the focus is diluted and the definition becomes unmanageable

Public sector first

  • If the government is incorruptible, private-sector corruption will come down naturally

Transparency International's answer

Because interpretations vary, Transparency International (TI) defines corruption as "private abuse of public office". It also advocates the Elephant Test: corruption may be hard to define or describe, but when a civil servant behaves unethically, society knows it. It is easier to point out than to define.

Elephant Test in one line

You may not be able to define an elephant, but you know one when you see it: society recognises corrupt behaviour on sight.

Five pairs or triplets of types appear in the handout.

3 · Behaviour that harms the public interest#

Corruption in the wider sense goes beyond giving and receiving bribes. It includes any behaviour that harms public interest:

  • Lethargy in discharge of assigned duties.
  • Dereliction or negligence of duty.
  • Repeated carelessness.
  • Non-punctuality.
  • Subjective interpretation of public interest.
  • Favouritism and nepotism.
  • Communalism, casteism, lynching and regional bias.
Wider list
LDRNSFC

Lethargy, Dereliction, Repeated carelessness, Non-punctuality, Subjective public interest, Favouritism, Communalism etc.: "Lazy Duty Runs Never Services Friends' Causes".

4 · Implications of corruption#

Social

  • Divisions: vertical (the rich or well-connected get their due, the poor do not) and horizontal (favouritism to one caste or class group, neglect of others).
  • Insecurity: people do not report crimes or cooperate with police, which boosts criminals and criminalises society.
  • Danger to life: building collapses and fire disasters from substandard materials, false certificates, through collusive corruption.
  • Erodes the social fabric: general distrust in society.

Economic

  • Less aid to poor countries: in African countries most aid went into the pockets of dictators, warlords, officials and cronies, so donors revised their stance.
  • Less investment: investors are wary. Economist Paulo Mauro: investment differential between a less and a more corrupt country can be as high as 12%.
  • Acts as an additional tax on investment (bribe or extortion on businesses).
  • Lower government revenues (officials collude or extort while collecting).
  • Public expenditure diverted to non-priority sectors to ease corrupt activity.
  • Efficiency of government in development work is compromised.
  • Distorts fair competition (collusive corruption, crony capitalism).
  • Brain drain and flight of capital.
  • Trouble joining international clubs: Eastern European countries, more corrupt than Western Europe, had difficulty getting into the European Union.
  • Environmental damage through collusion with officials leading to flagrant violations.

Political and legal

  • Enhances power of public servants: in a fair system discretion is reduced, in a corrupt one the official uses discretion against public interest.
  • Increases the role of money power.
  • Pork-barrelling: a project is launched as serving public interest, but deeper examination shows it is not needed and serves the private interests of a corporate house, officials and politicians.
  • Citizen despair makes extremist politicians attractive (xenophobia, radical solutions, radical populism).
  • Mass unrest, as in the India Against Corruption campaign and unrest worldwide.
  • Radical tendencies if unabated: the left extremism problem and the insurgency in the North-East are fuelled by rampant corruption leading to poor development and a vicious cycle of violence.

Security implications

  • Corruption within security forces aids money laundering, drug and human trafficking and arms smuggling.
  • Corruption in defence procurement compromises the quality of defence contracts.

International implications

5 · Revisionism in corruption studies#

A school of thought with few takers. It says some corruption in civil services is not bad. Its hypothesis: civil servants are by and large lethargic, less motivated, prone to red tape and low paid, which lowers efficiency, so the presence of corruption increases the bureaucracy's efficiency in doing public work.

Exam handle

Revisionism = "corruption as grease". The handout stresses that it has few takers, so never present it as the accepted view.

6 · Factors behind corruption#

Five families of causes. Memory frame: {{P E L A P}}.

Five families
PELAP

Psycho-social, Economic, Legal, Administrative, Political: "Poor Elephants Lift Awkward Pots".

Psycho-social factors

Human behaviour is the combination of the person's own attitudes or predispositions and the environment around the person.

  • Need for THYMOS (Greek: respect or recognition): high corruption correlates with low self-worth. People turn to materialism to raise self-worth. Low self-esteem puts the locus of control outside the person, who derives self-worth from how society values him.
  • Societal attitude: society sees government as highly corrupt. Persistent labelling and shaming of government employees disincentivises even honest employees, who are seen through a corrupt lens.
  • Squirrel Nuts Syndrome: bureaucrats near retirement become more corrupt to continue the benefits they enjoyed in service.
  • Opportunity Theory: most civil servants do not seek opportunities, only a few do, but when opportunities come their way they commit corruption. So a major way to curb corruption is to reduce opportunities.
  • Religious context: in European studies, areas with more Protestants had less corruption, since Protestant Christianity was born as a reaction to corruption in the Catholic Church.
  • History: India has a long history of corruption (lower bureaucracy was corrupt since ancient times, as in Kautilya's works), making society more tolerant.
  • Popular sanction: there is some degree of societal sanction of corruption and no popular sanction against it; honest people are victimised.
  • Social evils: excessive expenditure on marriages, dowry etc. provoke public servants to be corrupt.
Handout alert

Under "Popular sanction" the handout first says there is "some degree of societal sanction regarding corruption" and then "there is no popular sanction against corruption". The point it makes: society lacks real popular disapproval (no popular sanction against corruption) and honest people are victimised. Use that reading.

Psycho-social eight
TSSORHPS

Thymos, Societal attitude, Squirrel Nuts, Opportunity, Religion, History, Popular sanction, Social evils: "Tall Sailors Seldom Overlook Rough Harbours, Pirates Sail".

Squirrel Nuts

A squirrel hoards nuts before winter, and a bureaucrat near retirement hoards benefits before retirement. Thymos = hunger for respect.

Economic factors

  • Poor salary at upper levels of bureaucracy is blamed, but simply raising salaries may not make a significant dent.
  • Culture of consumerism feeds low self-esteem and so corruption.
  • Licence Permit Quota Raj to Clearance Raj: now so many clearances and NOCs are needed to start a venture; the red tape gives incentives to be corrupt.
  • Willingness and ability to corrupt: large MNCs have the financial clout to bribe their way to favourable policy.
  • Lack of cooperation from business houses: if all refused to pay bribes, corruption would fall significantly, but they try to outbid each other.
Economic five
PCCWL

Poor salary, Consumerism, Clearance Raj, Willingness and ability, Lack of business cooperation: "Pay Can't Cure Willing Lions".

Legal factors

The handout's central claim: there is excessive legal protection for public servants against prosecution for corruption.

Protective provisions
ProvisionWhat it saysEffect
Article 311Reasonable opportunity to the public servant before any action against himMakes it very difficult to dismiss corrupt servants; excessive protection
Section 19 of PCA, 1988Sanction of the appointing authority is required before prosecuting a public servantProtects honest servants from spurious allegations but misused to protect the corrupt
Section 197 of CrPCFor any offence (apart from corruption) related to discharge of public duties, permission of appointing authority is requiredSame protective logic and same misuse
Section 6A of DSPE Act, 1946Prior sanction for investigation of officers of joint secretary rank and aboveStruck down (see flow below)

Section 6A chain

Preliminary inquiry
The first step a body takes; then a full-fledged investigation. Prior sanction is needed for investigation but not for preliminary inquiry
Single Directive
Required sanction of the appointing authority for both preliminary inquiry and investigation against officers of joint secretary rank and above
1997
Supreme Court struck it down in the Vineet Narain Case
Section 6A
DSPE Act amended and the Single Directive made law as Sec 6A
2014
Supreme Court struck down Sec 6A: officers of joint secretary and above had special protection, violating equality
2018
PCA 1988 amended, Section 17A added: permission needed for preliminary inquiry for all public servants, except where the public servant is caught red-handed

Other legal factors:

  • Inadequacy of the judicial process, with delay in prosecution.
  • Absence of effective whistle-blowers' laws to protect honest officials.
  • Inadequate autonomy of anti-graft bodies such as CBI and CVC, which have become politicised.
Protective shields
ASSS

Article 311, Section 19 PCA, Section 197 CrPC, Section 6A DSPE: "All Shields Stay Strong".

Administrative factors

  • Procedural complexity creates the need for middlemen, conduits for corrupt money.
  • Red-tapism.
  • Weak internal grievance redressal.
  • Peer pressure and indoctrination make honest officials afraid to pursue anti-graft action.
  • Bureaucratic resistance to administrative reforms.
  • Politicisation of civil service management through transfers, key and plum postings.
  • General culture of secrecy in administration.

Political factors

  • Absence of political will: multiple attempts to pass the Lokpal bill and the delay in appointing the Lokpal.
  • Criminalisation of politics: criminals become part of the system and protect themselves.
  • No encouragement of neutrality: public servants take sides when a particular dispensation is in power.
  • Lack of voter maturity: voters re-elect parties earlier ousted for misdeeds, because of the TINA factor (There Is No Alternative).
  • Money power in elections incentivises corruption; weak disclosure norms on functioning and funding of political parties.
Political five
ACNVM

Absent will, Criminalisation, Neutrality lacking, Voter immaturity (TINA), Money power: "Any Candidate Needs Votes and Money".

7 · Measuring corruption#

Four methods appear in the handout.

Methods of measurement
MethodDetailsLimits or notes
Official statisticsBased on prosecution and investigation recordsIf prosecution and investigation are ineffective, the statistics do not give a comprehensive idea
Perceptual and attitudinal surveysTransparency International's Corruption Perception Index (CPI), annual since 1995; 0 = most corrupt, 100 = least corruptBased on questionnaires to country experts and business leaders (not the common man); based on perception, not objective assessment; sources change each year; rank comparison over years is not appropriate since the number of countries varies
Experiential surveysGlobal Corruption Barometer (TI), based on actual experienceNot annual, done regionally. In the Asia region Japan was least corrupt and India most corrupt among 17 countries surveyed
Tracking surveysWorld Bank's PETS: Public Expenditure Tracking Survey; the World Bank demands transparency in tracking funds and their flow at different levels when donor-agency funds are spentTracks the flow of funds
