In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • The Competition Commission of India (CCI) guards fair market practices and consumer welfare. It works under the Competition Act, 2002 (amended 2007, in force 2009) and replaced the old MRTP regime after the 1991 reforms.
  • The old MRTP Act (1969), rooted in Arts. 38 and 39, tried to stop big business from growing too large. The new law instead regulates market conduct.
  • The CCI is a statutory, quasi-judicial body under the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, with a Chairperson and up to six Members who serve 5 years or until age 65.
  • It acts against cartels and anti-competitive agreements, abuse of dominance, and harmful mergers and acquisitions (combinations). Penalty is up to 10% of average turnover
  • Appeals go to the NCLAT
  • Recent focus (2020-25) is the digital economy: Google's Android case (2022, Rs 1,338 crore), the 2023 Amendment (deal value threshold, global-turnover penalty, settlements) and the Digital Markets and Data Unit (2024).

Key terms

Cartel
Secret agreement among firms (price-fixing, bid-rigging) that distorts competition; looked at under Section 3
Abuse of dominant position
Misusing market power through unfair pricing, discrimination or denying market access
Combination
A merger, amalgamation or takeover; examined under Sections 5 and 6
AAEC
Appreciable adverse effect on competition; the test for harmful agreements and combinations
Director General (DG)
Investigates when the CCI directs; has search, seizure and examination powers like a civil court
NCLAT
National Company Law Appellate Tribunal; hears appeals against CCI orders
Deal value threshold
2023 Amendment idea that brings big acquisitions under CCI scrutiny even if the target's turnover is low
DMDU
Digital Markets and Data Unit (2024); watches data concentration, algorithmic pricing and platform gatekeeping

1 · Introduction and the MRTP background#

  • The CCI is the guardian of fair market practices and consumer welfare in India's liberalised economy. It was established under the provisions of the amendment of the Competition Act, 2002 in 2007 and replaced the outdated MRTP regime, marking the shift from a control-oriented to a market-driven system after the 1991 reforms.
  • Statutory duties:
    • Eliminate practices having adverse effect on competition
    • Promote and sustain competition
    • Protect consumer interests
    • And ensure freedom of trade carried on by other participants in markets in India
  • Vision:
    • To promote and sustain an enabling competition culture through engagement and enforcement that would inspire businesses to be fair, competitive and innovative
    • Enhance consumer welfare
    • And support economic growth

MRTP regime

MRTP Act at a glance

ComponentDescription
LawMonopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices (MRTP) Act, 1969, India's first major competition law
Constitutional rootDirective Principles, Articles 38 and 39: prevent "concentration of wealth to the common detriment"
Core objective 1Prevent monopoly: controlled large industrial houses so they did not dominate the economy
Core objective 2Regulate trade: targeted restrictive (limiting competition) and unfair (misleading or fraudulent) trade practices
Core objective 3Public interest: an economic system working for the common good, not just elite corporations
Regulatory bodyMRTP Commission (enquiry and investigative power)
Key shift (1991)Liberalisation moved focus from "restricting company size" to "regulating market conduct"
Current statusRepealed; replaced by the Competition Act, 2002
  • Memory aid (MRTP objectives): Monopoly prevention, Regulate trade practices, Public interest: "Make Rules for the Public".

2 · Objectives of the Competition Act, 2002#

  • The Act promotes a vibrant, competitive market and replaces the restrictive, outdated provisions of the MRTP Act, 1969. Its objectives balance economic efficiency and consumer welfare.

Seven objectives

ObjectiveMeaning
Prevent anti-competitive agreementsProhibit collusive arrangements such as cartels, price-fixing or bid-rigging
Prohibit abuse of dominant positionNo misuse of market power through unfair pricing, discriminatory practices or denial of market access
Regulate combinations (mergers and acquisitions)Examine mergers, amalgamations or takeovers that may have an "appreciable adverse effect" on competition within India
Promote and sustain market competitionAn ecosystem where many players compete freely and fairly, leading to innovation and efficiency
Protect consumer interestsConsumer choice, with quality goods and services at reasonable prices
Ensure freedom of tradeOpen market where big and small enterprises operate without unfair barriers or monopolistic restrictions
Encourage economic efficiencyBetter productivity, technological progress and national competitiveness
  • Memory aid (Seven objectives): Agreements (anti-competitive), Abuse of dominance, Combinations, Promote competition, Protect consumers, Freedom of trade, Efficiency: "All Agents Compete, People Profit Fairly, Everywhere".
  • Shortcut: Three prohibitions. Agreements, Abuse, Combinations: the three things the Act polices. The other four objectives are promotion and protection goals.

4 · Powers and functions#

  • The CCI is both regulator and adjudicator. Its powers come from the Competition Act, 2002 and cover preventive, corrective and advisory roles.

Seven groups of powers

PowerWhat it does
1. Regulatory (preventive)Examines agreements such as cartels, price-fixing, bid-rigging or exclusive supply under Section 3. Prohibits conduct causing or likely to cause "appreciable adverse effect on competition" (AAEC). Example: in 2022 the CCI fined Google for abusing dominance in Android licensing
2. Adjudicatory (corrective)Quasi-judicial: issues cease-and-desist orders, imposes monetary penalties, directs modification of anti-competitive agreements; can order division of enterprises to prevent abuse of dominance (Section 28); orders appealable to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT)
3. InvestigativeCan direct the Director General (DG) to investigate; the DG has search, seizure and examination powers akin to a civil court; investigations form the evidentiary base for adjudication
4. Advisory and advocacyOpinions on competition policy when sought by the Centre or States; market studies (telecom, e-commerce, digital advertising); outreach and academic collaboration
5. Combinations (M&A)Under Sections 5 and 6 examines mergers and acquisitions; can approve, reject or suggest modifications. Example: in 2023 the CCI approved the Sony-Zee merger with conditional safeguards against content monopolisation
6. Civil court powersSummon witnesses, require documents, take evidence on oath, conduct inquiries
7. PenalUp to 10% of the average turnover of the enterprise; in cartel cases, up to three times the profit or 10% of turnover, whichever is higher
  • Memory aid (Powers): Regulatory, Adjudicatory, Investigative, Advisory, Combinations, Civil court powers, Penal: "Right Actions Impose Accountability Clearly, Court-Proof".
  • Shortcut: Section map. Section 3 = Agreements. Sections 5 and 6 = Combinations (mergers). Section 28 = Division of enterprises. Remember A-M-D in number order 3, 5-6, 28.

Case path under the Act

  • Information of alleged contravention: Matter comes to the CCI
  • CCI directs the Director General: DG investigates with civil-court-like search and seizure
  • Evidence supports adjudication: Investigation forms the evidentiary foundation
  • CCI order: Cease-and-desist, penalty, modification, or division of enterprise (Sec. 28)
  • Appeal: To the NCLAT

5 · Recent reforms and developments (2020-2025)#

  • The CCI has widened its focus from traditional sectors to the digital economy, acting against abuse of dominance by multinationals in digital platforms, e-commerce and technology.

Strengthening the framework

  • Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023: new concept of the deal value threshold, bringing big-tech acquisitions (for example start-up buyouts by global firms) under CCI scrutiny even if the target has low turnover.
  • Empowered the CCI to impose penalty guidelines linked to global turnover for deterrence against multinational violators.
  • Introduced settlement and commitment mechanisms so companies can voluntarily fix anti-competitive behaviour before formal adjudication, speeding up disposal.

Digital market oversight

  • The CCI launched a Digital Markets and Data Unit (DMDU) in 2024 to monitor data concentration, algorithmic pricing and platform gatekeeping.
  • Aligns India with the EU's Digital Markets Act (DMA) and the UK's Competition and Markets Authority (CMA).

Major cases against dominant global firms

CaseYearWhat happened
Google, Android ecosystem2022Rs 1,338 crore fine for abusing dominance in Android's mobile OS market by restricting app developers and mandating pre-installation of Google services. Behavioural remedies: users may uninstall preloaded apps; third-party app stores permitted. Seen as parallel to the EU's action against Google
Apple, App Store policies2023Investigation into the 30% commission on in-app purchases, viewed as restrictive and discriminatory toward small developers; echoed US and EU proceedings
Amazon and Flipkart, preferential treatment2021Exclusive deals and preferential listings for select sellers distorted price competition. Detailed scrutiny of marketplace algorithms; commitments sought for fair-play compliance
  • Memory aid (Big-tech cases): Google (2022, Rs 1,338 crore), Apple (2023, 30% commission), Amazon-Flipkart (2021, preferential listings): "Good Appetite Always".

Market studies and advocacy

  • Market studies: telecom (2021), pharmaceuticals (2022), digital advertising (2023).
  • Advocated interoperability and data portability against "walled garden" ecosystems of big tech.
  • Competition literacy campaign "Fair Competition for a Vibrant Economy" (2024).

Institutional strengthening and judicial developments

  • Coordination with SEBI, RBI and MeitY on fintech and digital platforms.
  • AI-based analytics tools for early detection of cartelisation and predatory pricing.
  • Regional offices in Bengaluru and Mumbai expanded to decentralise enforcement and reach digital start-ups.
  • The NCLAT and Supreme Court upheld CCI orders in multiple cases (for example Google v. CCI, 2023), affirming investigative autonomy
  • The judiciary also stressed natural justice and procedural safeguards
  • Memory aid (2023 Amendment): Deal value threshold, Global-turnover penalty guidelines, Settlement and commitments: "Deals Get Scrutinised".

6 · Key challenges#

Seven challenges

ChallengeDetail
1. Digital market complexityGoogle, Amazon and Meta use algorithmic pricing, data ecosystems and opaque platform models; hard to prove dominance or abuse without proprietary data or algorithmic transparency
2. Limited deterrence for multinationals10% of turnover cap, but earlier fines were based only on Indian revenue, inadequate to deter global firms; the 2023 amendment partially addressed this, but consistent implementation remains a concern
3. Procedural delays and backlogComplex probes, shortage of economic experts, lengthy hearings. Over 350 cases pending as of 2024
4. Overlapping jurisdictionsOverlap with TRAI (telecom), SEBI (financial markets) and MeitY (digital data) causes friction and forum shopping
5. Global coordination gapsNo bilateral cooperation with global antitrust regulators limits coordinated enforcement against cross-border MNC practices
6. Resource and expertise deficitToo few digital economists, data scientists and legal analysts for algorithmic collusion and AI-driven pricing
7. Compliance and advocacyWeak compliance among smaller firms; limited competition awareness among consumers and start-ups outside metros
  • Memory aid (Challenges): Digital complexity, Limited deterrence, Procedural delays, Overlapping jurisdictions, Global coordination gaps, Resource deficit, Compliance and advocacy: "Dear Leaders, Please Open Good Reforms, Clearly".

Also in the handout

  • The CCI often faces difficulty in proving dominance or abuse due to lack of access to proprietary data and algorithmic transparency.

7 · Way forward and conclusion#

Seven suggestions

Way forwardDetail
1. Digital market law (DMA-style)A Digital Competition Act as recommended by the 2024 Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance: regulate gatekeeper platforms, ensure interoperability, prevent self-preferencing
2. Enhanced global cooperationPartnerships with the European Commission, US FTC and OECD for data exchange and harmonised probes
3. Institutional capacityRecruit data analysts, digital economists and AI experts; a DMDU with advanced forensic capabilities; continuous staff training
4. Binding penalty frameworkOperationalise penalties on global turnover for MNCs, in line with EU and US models
5. Expedited resolutionRobust use of settlement and commitment mechanisms (2023 amendment) to free bandwidth for high-impact digital cases
6. Advocacy and awarenessWider competition literacy among small businesses, State regulators and consumers through regional outreach and university collaboration
7. Greater autonomyFinancial and functional independence and transparent appointment of Members and DG
  • Memory aid (Way forward): Digital law, Global cooperation, Institutional capacity, Binding penalties, Expedited resolution, Advocacy, Autonomy: "Don't Give In, Be Ever Alert, Act".
  • Conclusion: The CCI must move from a reactive enforcement agency to a forward-looking market regulator, balancing competition with innovation and consumer welfare and placing India alongside leading global antitrust regimes.

Also in the handout

  • Forward Outlook The CCI’s evolution reflects India’s transition from reactive enforcement to proactive market regulation.
  • ## Conclusion The CCI’s trajectory mirrors India’s ambition to build a fair, transparent, and innovation-friendly economy.

Traps#

Trap 1 · Act year versus working year The Competition Act was passed in 2002; the handout says it was amended in 2007 and the Commission was established and came into force in 2009. The MRTP Act dates from 1969 and is repealed. Rule: Act 2002, amendment 2007, in force 2009; MRTP 1969 repealed.
Trap 2 · MRTP versus CCI MRTP restricted the size of large industrial houses; after 1991 the focus moved to regulating market conduct (cartels, abuse of dominance, combinations). Rule: MRTP = control size; CCI = protect competition and consumers.
Trap 3 · Two different penalty rules A normal penalty is up to 10% of the average turnover of the enterprise. In cartel cases it is up to three times the profit or 10% of turnover, whichever is higher. Rule: general = 10% of turnover; cartel = 3 x profit or 10% turnover, whichever is higher.
Trap 4 · Appeal forum and appointing committee CCI orders are appealed to the NCLAT. Members are appointed by the Centre on the recommendation of a Selection Committee chaired by the Chief Justice of India or a Supreme Court judge nominated by him. Rule: appeal = NCLAT; Selection Committee head = CJI or his nominee judge.
