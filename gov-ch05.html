1 · Regulatory Bodies · WHY THEY ROSE · ROLE · Courts#

Handout only · RB pp. 2-4
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • After 1991 the State stopped running most sectors and started refereeing them. Independent regulators were created to keep that refereeing away from politics. Know why they rose, how far they really are free of politics, what they added, and how courts shaped them.

Three main functions of the State

  • provision of services: many services, most monopolistic (police, defence)
  • regulation:
    • Became important post-1991 (LPG reforms): before 1991 most sectors were nationalised, so little need for regulation (less open competition, few players)
    • After liberalisation and privatisation competition and private players grew, so the need to regulate was felt
  • redistribution and welfare: socio-economic justice through welfare programmes, subsidies and redistribution, particularly for vulnerable sections

Two forms of regulation

  • self-regulation: regulators are from that sector only and directly involved in service provision. E.g. Bar Association of India, ICAI, News Broadcasting Standards Authority
  • external regulation: regulators are outside the sector and not directly involved in service provision

Two types of regulatory bodies

  • sector-specific: power, telecom etc. E.g. IRDA, PFRDA, TRAI, SEBI
  • general: regulate some aspects of all sectors. E.g. Competition Commission of India

WHY Independent Regulators ROSE

Factors behind the rise of Independent Regulatory Bodies

  • insulate key sectors from the ups and downs of politics:
    • Before 1991 the government held a monopoly in most sectors
    • Regulators set up after the reforms insulate key sectors from political interference
  • insulate from pressures of re-distributive justice:
    • Services once given as redistributive justice are now also provided by private sectors
    • Government control let politicians promise services at subsidised rates for gaining votes
  • attract private investment including FDI
  • increasing technicality of regulatory space
  • Protect the interest of consumers
  • Promote fair competition through fair rules
  • government was itself a player in several sectors, so not fair for it to regulate them: hence separate independent regulators
  • experience gap:
    • With nationalisation there were very few private players, so government had no experience of regulating
    • After withdrawal, regulation needed experts of those sectors
  • create conditions conducive to the growth of the sector
Memory aid · GIFT PRICE

Nine factors: Government was a player · Investment (FDI) · Fair competition · Technicality · Politics insulated · Redistributive pressure insulated · Interest of consumers · Conditions for growth · Experience gap. (own mnemonic)

ARE IRCs Really Depoliticised?

Value addition

IRCs were set up to insulate key economic sectors from political interference and ensure independent, transparent and expert-based decision-making. But their success in depoliticisation has been limited.

Reasons for limited depoliticisation

  • political pressure: governments still influence decisions, esp. where tariff increases and market reforms clash with electoral promises and public expectations
  • redistributive justice: regulators must balance commercial viability with social welfare, so economic regulation cannot be cleanly separated from politics
  • limited independence: government control over appointments, funding and regulatory powers
  • regulatory capture: powerful businesses influence decisions through financial resources and technical expertise
Memory aid · CAPE

Why depoliticisation is limited: Capture by business · Autonomy limited · Political pressure · Equity (redistributive justice) duties. (own mnemonic)

SectorWhat happened (handout)
PowerOdisha Electricity Regulatory Commission (1995) set up for independent tariff regulation amid rising losses and distorted tariffs; political pressure to keep subsidised tariffs limited depoliticisation. Even after the Electricity Regulatory Commissions Act, 1998 and Electricity Act, 2003, tariff decisions and subsidies stay politically sensitive
Telecom (TRAI)set up to attract private investment and ensure independent telecom regulation, but faced continuous conflicts with the Department of Telecommunications (DoT), which kept important licensing and policy powers; the 2G spectrum allocation controversy showed how politically influenced decisions can undermine independent regulation
Petroleum (PNGRB)PNGRB Act, 2006: limited powers, upstream activities outside its jurisdiction and important pricing decisions beyond its direct control: government retention of crucial powers limits a regulator even when a separate body exists
Way forward / key point

Overall assessment: IRCs have not completely depoliticised economic regulation, as governments keep control of important policy and regulatory decisions. But they made regulatory processes more transparent, consultative and open to public scrutiny.

Critical assessment

  • increasing regulators with limited powers: more regulators have not always come with real independence or authority
  • rationalising myth: the belief that merely creating regulators will send the right signals and attract investment fails without effective powers and institutional capacity
  • sinecure state: regulators may be used to rehabilitate retired government officers: power, money and prestige but little responsibility or work

Contribution OF Regulatory Bodies

What regulators contributed

  • checking elite capture:
    • Regulators depend on big corporates for information to make rules
    • Corporates may give it in ways that benefit them (elite capture). To counter it, regulators adopt transparent, democratic rule-making
  • public participation:
    • Open hearings and comment-seeking
    • Democratisation of regulatory space: greater democratisation of regulatory decision-making
  • changing political engagement:
    • Earlier mobilisation was on macro issues (free power for farmers)
    • Now also micro issues such as different tariffs for different sectors and the location of power plants
  • greater transparency: an inversion of the original idea, as the regulatory space itself became an arena of political contest, but transparency has increased over time
Memory aid · PEDCT

Contributions: Public participation · Elite capture checked · Democratised space · Changed political engagement · Transparency. (own mnemonic)

Judicial Intervention

Courts in the telecom sector

  • Delhi Science Forum Case (1996): Supreme Court stressed the need for an independent regulatory framework in telecom
  • 2G Spectrum Case: SC examined the legality and transparency of spectrum allocation
  • TRAI and TDSAT: judicial intervention strengthened their regulatory role while subjecting decisions to scrutiny
  • outcome: better overall quality of sectoral governance
Note

Historical data (2009-12 study): effectiveness of sector regulators can be read from appeals against their decisions before appellate bodies. The figures reported: telecom 62% · power 8% (with interstate variations).

2 · Regulators: People · Parent Ministry · Accountability · Conflicts#

Handout only · RB pp. 4-6
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • A regulator is only as independent as its members, staff, funding and links to its ministry. This chapter covers who sits on regulators, how ministry and regulator share work, how regulators are held accountable (India vs USA and UK), and where regulators fight each other.

Human Resource

Two kinds of people in regulators: regulators and general staff

  • regulators need two things: professional expertise and independence
  • historical findings:
    • 90% of members had a government background (partly because officials had expertise)
    • 49% had worked with the nodal or a related ministry before appointment, which can compromise independence
    • Appointment and removal still depend on government discretion
Memory aid · 3 QUALITIES

Seek in a regulator: Expertise · Independence (not detachment) · Ability to engage multiple players (a miniature State).

General staff: four problems

  • understaffing: 15-30% in regulators, including vacancies in senior positions
  • dependence on deputation:
    • Fewer permanent staff, more deputation from PSUs
    • Experience may not be used fully because of a short period of employment
  • lack of motivation:
    • No clear career path and adequate incentives
    • Regulators cannot offer incentives like PSUs
  • weak employment ecosystem: regulatory space has no employment ecosystem with clear career pathways
Memory aid · UDLW

Staff woes: Understaffing 15-30% · Deputation dependence · Low motivation · Weak ecosystem. (own mnemonic)

Note

Variations among regulators: composition, tenure, and appointment and removal processes differ. Need: a comprehensive regulatory framework and less government discretion.

Relationship WITH THE Parent Ministry

Value addition

Policy and regulation are hard to separate: it is not always clear where policymaking ends and regulation begins.

Ministry's role

  • set the broad policy
  • appointment and removal of regulators
  • provide funding and staff
  • provide the parliamentary interface
  • ensure vigilance against corruption and bribery
  • coordination with other regulators and ministries
  • capacity building
Memory aid · PAF PVCC

Ministry duties: Policy · Appointments · Funds and staff · Parliament interface · Vigilance · Coordination · Capacity building. (own mnemonic)

Note

Proposal: a separate Ministry of Regulation over all regulators. Why it may not work: the ministry of a sector understands its functioning and complexities better, and a single ministry may lack expertise to supervise regulators across diverse sectors.

Way forward / key point

UK practice: regulators and parent ministries issue management statements setting annual and long-term objectives and norms for their interactions.

Accountability OF Regulators

CountryAccountability picture (handout)
USAindependent commissions called the fourth, headless branch of government: substantial autonomy from the executive, while congressional oversight may be limited by lack of technical expertise, raising accountability concerns
Indiaregulators work under nodal ministries: a different relation between autonomy and executive accountability. Created by statute; most have an independent appellate mechanism; accounts under CAG audit; subject to public service laws; annual reports placed before Parliament through the executive; Parliamentary Committees examine reports and depositions; no separate parliamentary committee needed, as the committee of the nodal ministry is better placed
UKCorporate Plans · open meetings and consultations · affordable appeals · Regulatory Impact Assessment of regulations on the sector · information on comprehensive websites

Steps to ensure accountability

  • statutory creation: law increases accountability through parliamentary scrutiny and a legally defined framework
  • a clear mandate
  • clear definition of the relationship with Executive, Legislature and Judiciary
  • transparency in appointments, removal and decision-making
  • periodic reports
  • an independent appellate mechanism
Memory aid · RAPID SC

Accountability steps: Relationship defined · Appointment transparency · Periodic reports · Independent appeal · Decision transparency · Statute · Clear mandate. (own mnemonic)

Other Issues AND Inter-regulator Conflicts

Other issues

  • regulation vs development: more focus on regulation than on development of the sector
  • strained stakeholder relations: e.g. Cellular Operators Association of India (COAI) vs TRAI
  • inadequate research:
    • Too few macro and micro studies
    • Such studies help ministries find problems and an agenda for reform
Conflict of jurisdictionIssue (handout)
IRDA vs SEBI (2010)Unit Linked Insurance Plans (ULIPs) combine insurance and investment features; the Central Government intervened to clarify jurisdiction
CCI vs RBIjurisdiction over bank mergers
CERC vs CCIboundary between sector-specific electricity regulation and general competition law
CCI vs PNGRBcompetition proceedings involving public-sector oil companies: sectoral vs general regulators
Key point

FSLRC, 2011 (Justice B.N. Srikrishna) recommended consolidating financial-sector regulation, including the merger of SEBI, FMC, IRDA and PFRDA. Result: the Forward Markets Commission (FMC) was later merged with SEBI; the other regulators remain separate.

Reform requirements

  • clear jurisdiction: clear division between sectoral and general regulators
  • inter-regulatory consultation: fewer conflicting orders, uncertainty and prolonged litigation
  • independent appointments: combine professional expertise, independence and accountability

3 · FOOD Safety AND Standards Authority OF India (fssai)#

Handout only · RB pp. 6-8 · 2026 developments marked verify
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • FSSAI is the food regulator: it sets food standards, licenses food businesses and checks labels, working with the States. Know the Act, the ministry, the functions, the schemes, and the problems of weak enforcement and small vendors.
About FSSAIHandout
Statusstatutory body under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006
Nodal ministryMinistry of Health and Family Welfare
Objectiveavailability of safe and wholesome food for human consumption
Scopefood manufacturing, processing, storage, distribution, sale and import
Implementationworks with State Food Safety Authorities

Functions AND Powers

What FSSAI does

  • food safety standards: science-based standards for food products, additives, contaminants and packaging
  • licensing and registration of Food Business Operators (FBOs)
  • inspection and surveillance: sampling, testing, surveillance and enforcement
  • food labelling: nutritional information, ingredients and mandatory consumer disclosures
  • consumer protection: misleading claims, unsafe food and violations of safety rules
Memory aid · CLIPS

FSSAI functions: Consumer protection · Labelling · Inspection · Licensing (registration) · Standards. (rearranged: no fixed order) (own mnemonic)

Initiatives

InitiativePurpose (handout)
Eat Right India Movementsafe, healthy and sustainable food habits: regulatory, capacity-building and awareness measures
FoSCoS (Food Safety Compliance System)digital platform for licensing, registration, renewal and compliance
FoSTaC (Food Safety Training and Certification)capacity building among food handlers and businesses
Hygiene Rating Schemeencourages food establishments to meet hygiene and sanitation standards
RUCO (Repurpose Used Cooking Oil)responsible collection and disposal or repurposing of used cooking oil
In the news (Saarthi IAS CA; verify dates)

Recent developments, 2026 (verify dates): school food regulations: draft amendment, Aug 2026, on definitions of food high in added fat, sugar or salt, for safe food and balanced diets for children in schools · front-of-pack nutrition labelling: simplified FOP information still a key regulatory issue · pan masala packaging: amendments notified Aug 2026 · food testing: amendments notified Sep 2026 on timelines for lab sample analysis, methods and reporting formats · FoSCoS: revised licensing requirements and compliance processes in 2026.

Significance, Issues, WAY Forward

Significance in governance

  • public health: safety and quality of food
  • consumer rights: transparency through labelling and regulation of misleading claims
  • preventive healthcare: healthy diets, fewer risks from unsafe food
  • market regulation: common standards, fairer markets
  • cooperative governance: works with States, food businesses and consumers

Issues and challenges

  • weak enforcement capacity: shortage of inspectors and testing laboratories
  • uneven State capacity: infrastructure and staffing differ
  • unorganised food sector: street vendors and small businesses face financial and technical barriers
  • misleading labelling: nutrition claims and packaging confuse consumers
  • balance: protect public health without excessive burden on smaller businesses

Way forward

  • strengthen State enforcement: laboratories, inspector capacity, risk-based surveillance
  • improve nutritional labelling: clear, evidence-based, easy to understand
  • support small food businesses: affordable training and compliance help
  • digital monitoring: FoSCoS, food traceability, data-based inspections
  • consumer participation: grievance redressal, awareness, public consultation

4 · Competition Commission OF India (CCI)#

Handout only · RB pp. 8-9 · 2023-24 developments and Meta case
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • CCI is the general regulator of markets: it stops cartels and abuse of dominance, vets big mergers, and now watches digital platforms. Know the Act, the 2023 changes, the Meta-WhatsApp penalty and the digital-market problems.
  1. 2002
    Competition Act, 2002 creates the CCI
  2. 2009
    CCI fully functional
  3. 2023
    Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023: deal value threshold, faster clearance, settlement and commitments, global-turnover penalties
  4. 2024
    CCI regulations on merger review, settlements, commitments and penalties · Meta penalty ₹213.14 crore
About CCIHandout
Statusstatutory body under the Competition Act, 2002; fully functional in 2009
Nodal ministryMinistry of Corporate Affairs
Objectiveprevent practices with adverse effect on competition, promote and sustain competition, protect consumers, ensure freedom of trade
CompositionChairperson and not more than six other members, appointed by the Central Government
Appealsto NCLAT, then the Supreme Court

Functions AND Powers

What CCI does

  • anti-competitive agreements: prohibits cartels, bid rigging, price fixing and agreements causing appreciable adverse effect
  • abuse of dominant position:
    • Unfair pricing, predatory pricing, denial of market access
    • Dominance itself is not prohibited, abuse is
  • regulation of combinations: mergers, acquisitions and amalgamations
  • investigation and enforcement: directs investigation through the Director General, imposes penalties, issues corrective directions
  • competition advocacy: opinions on policies, awareness among businesses and consumers
Way forward / key point

Remember: being dominant is legal; abusing dominance is not.

Competition (amendment) ACT, 2023

ChangeDetail
Deal value thresholdcombinations above ₹2,000 crore may need CCI approval where the target has substantial business operations in India
Faster merger clearancemaximum period cut from 210 days to 150 days
Settlement and commitmentfaster resolution of specified cases on anti-competitive vertical agreements and abuse of dominant position
Penalty frameworkpenalties can consider global turnover
Regulations (2024)CCI regulations and guidelines on merger review, settlements, commitments and monetary penalties
Memory aid · DFSGR

Amendment 2023: Deal value ₹2,000 cr · Faster 150 days · Settlement and commitments · Global turnover penalty · Regulations 2024. (own mnemonic)

Digital Markets AND THE META CASE

Regulating digital markets

  • platforms gain from network effects, large user bases and concentration of data: risk of market dominance
  • self-preferencing, tying of services, restrictions on market access raise concerns
  • the 2023 amendment strengthens control of combinations, esp. high-value digital deals with limited physical assets
In the news (Saarthi IAS CA; verify dates)

Meta-WhatsApp (2024, verify): CCI imposed a penalty of ₹213.14 crore on Meta for anti-competitive practices linked to WhatsApp's 2021 Privacy Policy update. The case links data collection, consumer choice and market dominance.

Issues AND WAY Forward

Issues and challenges

  • digital market dominance: network effects, data concentration and platform ecosystems make anti-competitive conduct hard to spot
  • regulatory overlap: CCI and sectoral regulators: jurisdictional uncertainty
  • institutional capacity: technical, legal and economic expertise for digital cases
  • enforcement delays: appeals and prolonged litigation
  • innovation vs regulation:
    • Too much intervention discourages investment
    • Too little leads to market concentration

Way forward

  • strengthen digital competition regulation: rules for digital gatekeepers while protecting innovation
  • inter-regulatory coordination: consultation with sectoral regulators
  • technical capacity: digital forensics, platform economics, data analytics
  • timely enforcement: better investigation and appellate efficiency
  • promote fair competition: protect market access for MSMEs, start-ups and new entrants
