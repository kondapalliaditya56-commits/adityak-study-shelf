1 · Law Commission: nature and evolution#

The Law Commission of India is a non-statutory, advisory body constituted by a notification of the Government of India, Ministry of Law and Justice, Department of Legal Affairs. Its role is to recommend legal reforms, simplify and modernise existing laws and promote justice in line with constitutional ideals.

Binding or not

Its recommendations are not binding, but they carry substantial persuasive value in shaping legislative and judicial policy.

Vision

Reforming the laws for maximizing justice in society and promoting good governance under the rule of law.

Historical evolution
PhaseDetails
Colonial period (1834-1947)Four Law Commissions under British rule to codify Indian laws. Their work led to the Indian Penal Code (1860), Civil Procedure Code (1908), Indian Contract Act (1872) and Evidence Act (1872)
Post-Independence (1955 onwards)First Law Commission (1955) under M.C. Setalvad, India's first Attorney-General, to reform outdated colonial laws and align them with the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs). Law Commissions have a 3-year tenure and specific terms of reference
Constitutional relevanceArticle 372 retained pre-independence laws until amended or repealed, so a Law Commission was needed for continuous legal reform
Handout alert

The handout says "22 Law Commissions have been constituted" since Independence, yet also says the 23rd Law Commission was constituted for three years from 1 September 2024. It also refers to "the 22nd Commission, 2023". Use: 22nd Commission (2023), 23rd Commission (from 1 Sept 2024).

Colonial codes
ICCE

IPC 1860, CPC 1908, Contract Act 1872, Evidence Act 1872: "Indian Codes Came, Explained".

3 · Role and functions of the Law Commission#

It is the principal advisory body on legal reform, guiding the Government to modernise, simplify and humanise the legal system. Functions flow from the terms of reference notified by the Government.

Eight functions
#FunctionDetail and handout example
1Review and repeal of obsolete lawsIdentifies redundant or outdated laws and periodically submits lists for repeal. The 248th and 251st Reports (2014) recommended repeal of nearly 1,824 obsolete laws, many later removed by the Repealing and Amending Acts of 2015-17
2Examination of laws in light of the ConstitutionChecks alignment with Fundamental Rights and DPSPs. Examples: gender justice, personal laws and the Uniform Civil Code
3Codification and simplificationConsolidates scattered statutes into clear codes. Played a key role in revising the IPC, CrPC and Evidence Act, recently reintroduced as the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (2023), Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (2023) and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (2023)
4Legal policy and advisory roleAdvises the Union Government on drafting new legislation, harmonising Indian laws with international treaties and conventions, and legal implications of reforms. Does comparative legal studies
5Research and reform in justice deliveryStudies judicial delays, pendency and procedural inefficiency. The 230th Report (2009) proposed fast-track courts, e-judiciary mechanisms and more judicial manpower
6Access to justice for the marginalisedFocus on women, children, SCs, STs and economically weaker sections. The 256th Report (2015) stressed pro bono legal services and technology for rural legal aid
7International and comparative legal researchHandles legal-cooperation and research requests from foreign governments or institutions. Reviews India's obligations under CEDAW and UNCAC
8Recommendations on specific legal issuesSpecialised studies on contemporary challenges (see list below)

Recent subjects (function 8)

  • Criminalization of politics (244th Report).
  • Electoral reforms and simultaneous elections (255th Report).
  • Data protection and privacy (276th Report).
  • Sedition and free speech (22nd Law Commission, 2023).

Five landmark outcomes

Dowry (91st), Custodial crimes (152nd), Suicide Sec 309 (210th), Mental Healthcare Act 2017 outcome, Data protection (276th): "Dear Court, See My Draft".

Significance of Law Commission reports

  • Institutional continuity: each report builds on earlier efforts, so law evolves with society.
  • Judicial credibility: Supreme Court and High Courts frequently cite reports as authoritative sources.
  • Legislative efficiency: ready drafts and model laws save parliamentary committees time.
  • Policy integration: many recommendations are absorbed into NITI Aayog, the National Judicial Mission or Ease of Doing Business reforms.

5 · Bar Council of India: introduction and composition#

The BCI is the apex statutory body established under the Advocates Act, 1961 on the recommendation of the All-India Bar Committee. It regulates the legal profession and sets standards for legal education. It functions as an autonomous institution under the Ministry of Law and Justice (Department of Legal Affairs). So it has a dual purpose: professional regulation of advocates and academic oversight of legal education.

6 · Functions of the Bar Council of India#

Functions are derived from Section 7 of the Advocates Act, 1961.

Seven heads of functions
HeadFunctions
1. Regulatory and supervisoryProfessional standards (conduct and etiquette); disciplinary authority (prescribes procedure for disciplinary committees of itself and SBCs and supervises them); supervision over SBCs; safeguarding rights, privileges and interests of advocates, including welfare and protection of professional independence
2. Educational and academicLegal education oversight (standards in consultation with universities and SBCs); recognition of law universities whose degrees qualify for enrolment; inspection and accreditation (visits itself or directs SBCs); Legal Education Committee frames curriculum, academic policies and guidelines
3. Disciplinary and enforcementHears appeals against SBC disciplinary orders; code enforcement (can suspend or remove advocates guilty of misconduct); coordinates with the judiciary on accountability and integrity of the Bar
4. Welfare and legal aidLegal aid and awareness for the economically weaker; welfare funds for indigent, disabled or distressed advocates; public legal literacy through seminars, publications and drives; encourages advocates to volunteer for public causes
5. Law reform and policyPromotes law reform; opinions on legislative drafts and judicial reforms; seminars, conferences and journals
6. Financial and administrativeManages and invests funds for education, welfare and legal aid; may receive grants, donations and benefactions; conducts elections, appoints committees, runs transparent internal administration
7. International and cooperativeMay affiliate with international legal bodies such as the International Bar Association (IBA) or International Legal Aid Association (ILAA); can recognise, on a reciprocal basis, law degrees from foreign universities for enrolment
Seven heads
REDWLFI

Regulatory, Educational, Disciplinary, Welfare, Law reform, Financial, International: "Right Education Develops Wise Legal Folk, India".

7 · Powers of the Bar Council of India#

The BCI has wide powers, both regulatory and quasi-judicial, as the apex supervisory body for advocates and law institutions.

Seven groups of powers
GroupPowers
1. DisciplinaryConstitute one or more Disciplinary Committees to inquire into misconduct; prescribe procedure for BCI and SBC proceedings; appellate authority against SBC decisions (can affirm, reverse or modify); suspend or remove an advocate's name from the roll for serious misconduct
2. SupervisoryGeneral supervision and control over SBCs; directions to SBCs on enrolment, discipline and welfare; may temporarily assume the functions of an SBC that fails its duties; inspect SBC records and operations
3. RegulatoryStandards of professional conduct and etiquette; minimum academic qualifications and course structure for legal education; recognise or derecognise law colleges and universities; regulate foreign lawyers per the 2023 BCI (Foreign Lawyers and Law Firms) Rules; prescribe conditions for enrolment (qualifications, fees, verification of credentials)
4. Financial and administrativeFunds for welfare, legal aid and law libraries; receive grants, donations, benefactions; regulate elections, membership and tenure of office bearers in BCI and SBCs; appoint committees or sub-committees
5. Quasi-judicialCivil-court-like powers in disciplinary or investigative proceedings: summon and enforce attendance of witnesses; require production of documents; receive evidence on affidavits; requisition public records from any office or court. Can start suo motu inquiries. Orders are binding, subject to appeal before the Supreme Court under Section 38 of the Advocates Act, 1961
6. Law reform and advocacyPropose amendments to legal education rules, professional standards or welfare schemes; consultative role with the Law Commission and Parliamentary Committees; represent India's legal community internationally
7. Emergency and residualResidual powers where no specific rule exists; interim orders to preserve integrity of the profession during proceedings; may suspend enrolments or revoke recognitions in urgent situations
Civil-court powers
SPAR

Summon witnesses, Production of documents, Affidavit evidence, Requisition public records: "See Papers And Records".

Power-ladder shortcut

BCI = appeals, supervision, recognition. SBC decides first, BCI hears appeal, and Section 38 takes it to the Supreme Court.

8 · State Bar Councils#

The SBC is a statutory body under the Advocates Act, 1961 that regulates and represents the profession at State level (enrolment, discipline, welfare and supervision of advocates). It is autonomous but under the general supervision of the BCI to keep uniform standards.

Composition of an SBC
AspectProvision
Legal basisAdvocates Act, 1961, Section 3
EstablishmentOne SBC for each State, or a common SBC for two or more States or UTs (e.g. Punjab and Haryana, Assam, Nagaland and Mizoram)
Ex-officio membersAdvocate-General of the State (in a common SBC, all concerned Advocate-Generals). For the Delhi SBC, the Additional Solicitor-General of India is also ex-officio
Chairman and Vice-ChairmanElected by the Council from among its members; tenure and re-election as per SBC rules
Term of officeFive years for elected members (extendable by 6 months by the BCI if elections are delayed)
CommitteesDisciplinary, Legal Aid, Executive, Enrolment and other committees as required
Administrative controlUnder the overall supervision of the BCI but an independent statutory body

Functions of SBCs

  • Enrolment and regulation: admit persons as advocates on the state roll and keep the register; verify qualifications, character and eligibility; ensure compliance with BCI standards of conduct.
  • Disciplinary and supervisory: decide misconduct cases against advocates on its roll; set up Disciplinary Committees; appeals against its decisions go to the BCI for final adjudication; supervise Bar Associations in the State.
  • Welfare and development: safeguard rights and interests of advocates; welfare schemes for indigent, disabled and distressed advocates; manage welfare funds; legal aid and awareness for the poor.
  • Educational and reform: promote state-level law reform; seminars, lectures and workshops; publish journals; visit and inspect universities as directed by the BCI.
  • Administrative and financial: elect Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson and BCI representatives; manage and invest funds; set up law libraries and give grants to local Bar Associations; frame rules, subject to BCI approval.

9 · Types of advocates#

The Advocates Act, 1961 recognises only one class of legal practitioner, the advocate. For functional clarity (under the Advocates Act, 1961 and Supreme Court Rules, 2013) advocates are grouped into three types.

Senior Advocates

  • A lawyer given special distinction by the Supreme Court or a High Court for exceptional standing at the Bar, ability or specialised knowledge.
  • The Chief Justice of India or a High Court Chief Justice, with a collegium of senior judges, may designate; only with the advocate's consent, on merit, integrity and contribution.
  • The Supreme Court's Permanent Committee (2018) headed by the CJI: the CJI (Chairperson), two senior-most SC Judges, the Attorney General of India, and one senior advocate nominated by the Committee.
  • Restrictions: cannot file cases directly or appear without being briefed through another advocate (Rule 2, BCI Rules); cannot draft pleadings or affidavits, but may give legal opinions and argue cases; must keep the highest ethical standards.

Advocate-on-Record (AOR)

  • Authorised under the Supreme Court Rules, 2013 to act and plead for a party before the Supreme Court.
  • Files cases, drafts petitions, communicates with the court registry. Only an AOR can sign and submit pleadings or file vakalatnamas in the Supreme Court.
  • Eligibility: at least four years of legal practice and one year of training under an AOR with ten years' standing; must clear the AOR Examination conducted by the Supreme Court Registry under a Board of Examiners headed by a Supreme Court Judge.
  • No advocate other than an AOR can file or act in a case in the Supreme Court unless the Court permits.

Other Advocates

  • Names entered on the roll of any State Bar Council under the Advocates Act, 1961.
  • May practise in all courts, tribunals and authorities across India, except where special qualifications like AOR status are required.
  • Can appear in High Courts, District Courts and Subordinate Courts, alone or under Senior Advocates; can draft documents, advise and argue before any court or quasi-judicial authority.
  • Cannot appear in the Supreme Court for a party unless instructed by an AOR. Conduct is governed by BCI Rules of Professional Ethics (Chapter II, Part VI).
