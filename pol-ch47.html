In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • The Law Commission of India is a non-statutory, advisory body set up by a Government notification (Ministry of Law and Justice). It recommends law reform
  • Its reports are not binding but carry great persuasive value
  • Four commissions worked under British rule (1834-1947). The First Law Commission of independent India (1955) was headed by M.C. Setalvad. Each commission has a 3-year term
  • The 23rd started on 1 September 2024
  • Its main jobs: repeal obsolete laws, test laws against the Constitution, codify and simplify laws, advise the Government, and research justice delivery and access to justice. It has submitted 277 reports up to the 22nd Commission.
  • The Bar Council of India (BCI) is a statutory body under the Advocates Act, 1961. It regulates the legal profession and sets standards for legal education. State Bar Councils (SBCs) enrol and discipline advocates in each state.
  • Advocates fall into three types: Senior Advocates, Advocates-on-Record (AORs), who alone can file in the Supreme Court, and other advocates on the state rolls.

Key terms

Non-statutory body
Created by a Government order, not by an Act of Parliament; the Law Commission is one
Article 372
Kept pre-independence laws alive until amended or repealed; the reason a Law Commission was needed
Working paper
Draft of a problem and reform ideas that the Commission circulates for public comment
Advocates Act, 1961
Law that created the BCI and the State Bar Councils
AIBE
All India Bar Examination; a mandatory qualifying exam for law graduates to practise in courts
Senior Advocate
Lawyer given special distinction by the SC or a High Court, with consent; cannot file cases directly or draft pleadings
Advocate-on-Record
Advocate authorised under the Supreme Court Rules, 2013 to act and plead for a party in the SC; only an AOR can file there
Section 38, Advocates Act
Appeal to the Supreme Court against BCI disciplinary orders
Remember it as

Law Commission = non-statutory, 3-year term, 1st in 1955 under Setalvad, 277 reports; BCI = statutory, Advocates Act 1961, Chairman 2 years; SBC members 5 years.

1 · Law Commission: nature and evolution#

  • The Law Commission of India is a non-statutory, advisory body constituted by a notification of the Government of India, Ministry of Law and Justice, Department of Legal Affairs. Its role is to recommend legal reforms, simplify and modernise existing laws and promote justice in line with constitutional ideals.
  • Binding or not: Its recommendations are not binding, but they carry substantial persuasive value in shaping legislative and judicial policy.
  • Vision: Reforming the laws for maximizing justice in society and promoting good governance under the rule of law.

Historical evolution

PhaseDetails
Colonial period (1834-1947)Four Law Commissions under British rule to codify Indian laws. Their work led to the Indian Penal Code (1860), Civil Procedure Code (1908), Indian Contract Act (1872) and Evidence Act (1872)
Post-Independence (1955 onwards)First Law Commission (1955) under M.C. Setalvad, India's first Attorney-General, to reform outdated colonial laws and align them with the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs). Law Commissions have a 3-year tenure and specific terms of reference
Constitutional relevanceArticle 372 retained pre-independence laws until amended or repealed, so a Law Commission was needed for continuous legal reform

  • It also undertakes comparative legal studies to align Indian law with global best practices.
  • It recommends legislative measures to eliminate discrimination and improve legal aid.

3 · Role and functions of the Law Commission#

  • It is the principal advisory body on legal reform, guiding the Government to modernise, simplify and humanise the legal system. Functions flow from the terms of reference notified by the Government.

Eight functions

#FunctionDetail and handout example
1Review and repeal of obsolete lawsIdentifies redundant or outdated laws and periodically submits lists for repeal. The 248th and 251st Reports (2014) recommended repeal of nearly 1,824 obsolete laws, many later removed by the Repealing and Amending Acts of 2015-17
2Examination of laws in light of the ConstitutionChecks alignment with Fundamental Rights and DPSPs. Examples: gender justice, personal laws and the Uniform Civil Code
3Codification and simplificationConsolidates scattered statutes into clear codes. Played a key role in revising the IPC, CrPC and Evidence Act, recently reintroduced as the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (2023), Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (2023) and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (2023)
4Legal policy and advisory roleAdvises the Union Government on drafting new legislation, harmonising Indian laws with international treaties and conventions, and legal implications of reforms. Does comparative legal studies
5Research and reform in justice deliveryStudies judicial delays, pendency and procedural inefficiency. The 230th Report (2009) proposed fast-track courts, e-judiciary mechanisms and more judicial manpower
6Access to justice for the marginalisedFocus on women, children, SCs, STs and economically weaker sections. The 256th Report (2015) stressed pro bono legal services and technology for rural legal aid
7International and comparative legal researchHandles legal-cooperation and research requests from foreign governments or institutions. Reviews India's obligations under CEDAW and UNCAC
8Recommendations on specific legal issuesSpecialised studies on contemporary challenges (see list below)

Recent subjects (function 8)

  • Criminalization of politics (244th Report).
  • Electoral reforms and simultaneous elections (255th Report).
  • Data protection and privacy (276th Report).
  • Sedition and free speech (22nd Law Commission, 2023).
  • Memory aid (Eight functions): Repeal obsolete laws, Examine laws against the Constitution, Codify and simplify, Legal policy advice, Research on justice delivery, Access to justice for the marginalised, International research, Specific issues: "Reform Every Clause, Let Rights Advance In Society".

Working procedure

How a report is made

  • Identification of topics: Referred by the Government, Supreme Court or High Court, or taken up suo motu
  • Research and data collection: With law universities, think tanks and ministries
  • Working Paper: Draft circulated for public feedback and expert review
  • Consultations: Stakeholders, judiciary and ministries
  • Final report drafting: Reviewed and finalised through internal deliberations
  • Submission: To the Ministry of Law and Justice for Cabinet consideration and possible introduction in Parliament
How a report is made
  • Memory aid (Report steps): Identify, Research, Working paper, Consult, Final draft, Submit: "I Read Written Cases, File Summaries".

Also in the handout

  • It periodically submits lists of such laws for repeal, ensuring that the statute book remains concise and relevant.
  • This reduces ambiguity, duplication, and contradictions in the legal system, facilitating better access to justice.
  • Enhancing Access to Justice for the Marginalized: The Commission focuses on laws affecting vulnerable groups—women, children, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and economically weaker sections.
  • It also reviews India’s obligations under global conventions such as CEDAW (Convention on the Elimination of Discrimination Against Women) and UNCAC (United Nations Convention Against Corruption).

Significance of Law Commission reports

  • Institutional continuity: each report builds on earlier efforts, so law evolves with society.
  • Judicial credibility: Supreme Court and High Courts frequently cite reports as authoritative sources.
  • Legislative efficiency: ready drafts and model laws save parliamentary committees time.
  • Policy integration: many recommendations are absorbed into NITI Aayog, the National Judicial Mission or Ease of Doing Business reforms.

Also in the handout

  • The Commission suggests reforms to promote justice, equality, and welfare, and may recommend new legislations to fulfill the socio-economic goals envisioned in the Preamble and Part IV of the Constitution.

5 · Bar Council of India: introduction and composition#

  • The BCI is the apex statutory body established under the Advocates Act, 1961 on the recommendation of the All-India Bar Committee. It regulates the legal profession and sets standards for legal education. It functions as an autonomous institution under the Ministry of Law and Justice (Department of Legal Affairs). So it has a dual purpose: professional regulation of advocates and academic oversight of legal education.

6 · Functions of the Bar Council of India#

  • Functions are derived from Section 7 of the Advocates Act, 1961.

Seven heads of functions

HeadFunctions
1. Regulatory and supervisoryProfessional standards (conduct and etiquette); disciplinary authority (prescribes procedure for disciplinary committees of itself and SBCs and supervises them); supervision over SBCs; safeguarding rights, privileges and interests of advocates, including welfare and protection of professional independence
2. Educational and academicLegal education oversight (standards in consultation with universities and SBCs); recognition of law universities whose degrees qualify for enrolment; inspection and accreditation (visits itself or directs SBCs); Legal Education Committee frames curriculum, academic policies and guidelines
3. Disciplinary and enforcementHears appeals against SBC disciplinary orders; code enforcement (can suspend or remove advocates guilty of misconduct); coordinates with the judiciary on accountability and integrity of the Bar
4. Welfare and legal aidLegal aid and awareness for the economically weaker; welfare funds for indigent, disabled or distressed advocates; public legal literacy through seminars, publications and drives; encourages advocates to volunteer for public causes
5. Law reform and policyPromotes law reform; opinions on legislative drafts and judicial reforms; seminars, conferences and journals
6. Financial and administrativeManages and invests funds for education, welfare and legal aid; may receive grants, donations and benefactions; conducts elections, appoints committees, runs transparent internal administration
7. International and cooperativeMay affiliate with international legal bodies such as the International Bar Association (IBA) or International Legal Aid Association (ILAA); can recognise, on a reciprocal basis, law degrees from foreign universities for enrolment

7 · Powers of the Bar Council of India#

  • The BCI has wide powers, both regulatory and quasi-judicial, as the apex supervisory body for advocates and law institutions.

Seven groups of powers

GroupPowers
1. DisciplinaryConstitute one or more Disciplinary Committees to inquire into misconduct; prescribe procedure for BCI and SBC proceedings; appellate authority against SBC decisions (can affirm, reverse or modify); suspend or remove an advocate's name from the roll for serious misconduct
2. SupervisoryGeneral supervision and control over SBCs; directions to SBCs on enrolment, discipline and welfare; may temporarily assume the functions of an SBC that fails its duties; inspect SBC records and operations
3. RegulatoryStandards of professional conduct and etiquette; minimum academic qualifications and course structure for legal education; recognise or derecognise law colleges and universities; regulate foreign lawyers per the 2023 BCI (Foreign Lawyers and Law Firms) Rules; prescribe conditions for enrolment (qualifications, fees, verification of credentials)
4. Financial and administrativeFunds for welfare, legal aid and law libraries; receive grants, donations, benefactions; regulate elections, membership and tenure of office bearers in BCI and SBCs; appoint committees or sub-committees
5. Quasi-judicialCivil-court-like powers in disciplinary or investigative proceedings: summon and enforce attendance of witnesses; require production of documents; receive evidence on affidavits; requisition public records from any office or court. Can start suo motu inquiries. Orders are binding, subject to appeal before the Supreme Court under Section 38 of the Advocates Act, 1961
6. Law reform and advocacyPropose amendments to legal education rules, professional standards or welfare schemes; consultative role with the Law Commission and Parliamentary Committees; represent India's legal community internationally
7. Emergency and residualResidual powers where no specific rule exists; interim orders to preserve integrity of the profession during proceedings; may suspend enrolments or revoke recognitions in urgent situations
  • Memory aid (Civil-court powers): Summon witnesses, Production of documents, Affidavit evidence, Requisition public records: "See Papers And Records".
  • Shortcut: Power-ladder shortcut. BCI = appeals, supervision, recognition. SBC decides first, BCI hears appeal, and Section 38 takes it to the Supreme Court.

8 · State Bar Councils#

  • The SBC is a statutory body under the Advocates Act, 1961 that regulates and represents the profession at State level (enrolment, discipline, welfare and supervision of advocates). It is autonomous but under the general supervision of the BCI to keep uniform standards.

9 · Types of advocates#

  • The Advocates Act, 1961 recognises only one class of legal practitioner, the advocate. For functional clarity (under the Advocates Act, 1961 and Supreme Court Rules, 2013) advocates are grouped into three types.

Senior Advocates

  • A lawyer given special distinction by the Supreme Court or a High Court for exceptional standing at the Bar, ability or specialised knowledge.
  • The Chief Justice of India or a High Court Chief Justice, with a collegium of senior judges, may designate
  • Only with the advocate's consent, on merit, integrity and contribution
  • The Supreme Court's Permanent Committee (2018) headed by the CJI: the CJI (Chairperson), two senior-most SC Judges, the Attorney General of India, and one senior advocate nominated by the Committee.
  • Restrictions:
    • Cannot file cases directly or appear without being briefed through another advocate (Rule 2, BCI Rules)
    • Cannot draft pleadings or affidavits, but may give legal opinions and argue cases
    • Must keep the highest ethical standards
Permanent Committee for Senior Advocates (2018)

  • AAttorney General of India

  • TTwo senior-most Supreme Court judges

The Committee decides who is CAST as a Senior Advocate: its four members. The order is rearranged for memory. (Memory aid, not from the handout.)

Advocate-on-Record (AOR)

  • Authorised under the Supreme Court Rules, 2013 to act and plead for a party before the Supreme Court.
  • Files cases, drafts petitions, communicates with the court registry. Only an AOR can sign and submit pleadings or file vakalatnamas in the Supreme Court.
  • Eligibility:
    • At least four years of legal practice and one year of training under an AOR with ten years' standing
    • Must clear the AOR Examination conducted by the Supreme Court Registry under a Board of Examiners headed by a Supreme Court Judge
  • No advocate other than an AOR can file or act in a case in the Supreme Court unless the Court permits.

Other Advocates

  • Names entered on the roll of any State Bar Council under the Advocates Act, 1961.
  • May practise in all courts, tribunals and authorities across India, except where special qualifications like AOR status are required.
  • Can appear in High Courts, District Courts and Subordinate Courts, alone or under Senior Advocates
  • Can draft documents, advise and argue before any court or quasi-judicial authority
  • Cannot appear in the Supreme Court for a party unless instructed by an AOR. Conduct is governed by BCI Rules of Professional Ethics (Chapter II, Part VI).

Recall sheet#

Cover the right column and answer. Same questions as the flashcards.

What is the legal nature of the Law Commission?
Non-statutory, advisory body constituted by a Government notification (Ministry of Law and Justice, Department of Legal Affairs).
Are Law Commission recommendations binding?
No, but they carry substantial persuasive value.
Who headed the first post-Independence Law Commission and when?
M.C. Setalvad, India's first Attorney-General, in 1955.
Tenure of a Law Commission?
Generally three years, as per its terms of reference.
Which Article retained pre-independence laws?
Article 372.
How many reports had the Law Commission submitted by the 22nd Commission (2023)?
277 reports.
Which Reports recommended repeal of about 1,824 obsolete laws?
The 248th and 251st Reports (2014).
Which Report was on dowry deaths and what was the outcome?
The 91st Report (1983); Section 304-B IPC and 113-B Evidence Act via the 1986 Amendment.
Which Report was on custodial crimes?
The 152nd (1994); reflected in DK Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997) guidelines.
Which Report was on the death penalty?
The 262nd (2015): abolish it for all crimes except terrorism-related offences.
What did the 22nd Law Commission (2023) advise on sedition?
Retain Section 124A with safeguards and narrow its definition.
What are the steps in making a Law Commission report?
Identify topics, research, working paper, consultations, final report drafting, submission to the Law Ministry.
Under which Act is the BCI established?
The Advocates Act, 1961, on the recommendation of the All-India Bar Committee.
Who are ex-officio BCI members?
Attorney General of India and Solicitor General of India.
Term of BCI Chairperson and Vice-Chairperson?
2 years.
Which Section lists the BCI's functions?
Section 7 of the Advocates Act, 1961.
Which Section allows appeal to the Supreme Court against BCI disciplinary orders?
Section 38.
What power can the BCI use if an SBC fails its duties?
It may temporarily assume that SBC's functions.
Name BCI committees.
Disciplinary, Legal Aid, Executive and Legal Education Committees, plus others as needed.
Which Section of the Advocates Act governs SBCs?
Section 3.
Term of elected SBC members?
Five years, extendable by 6 months by the BCI if elections are delayed.
Who is the ex-officio member of an SBC?
The Advocate-General of the State; the Delhi SBC also has the Additional Solicitor-General of India.
Which committee is found in SBCs but not listed for the BCI?
The Enrolment Committee.
Who designates Senior Advocates?
The CJI or a High Court Chief Justice with a collegium of senior judges, with the advocate's consent.
Who sits on the Supreme Court's 2018 Permanent Committee for Senior Advocates?
CJI (Chair), two senior-most SC Judges, Attorney General, and one senior advocate nominated by the Committee.
Eligibility to be an AOR?
Four years of practice, one year of training under an AOR of ten years' standing, and the AOR Examination.
Can a Senior Advocate file or draft directly?
No; cannot file cases directly or draft pleadings or affidavits, but may give opinions and argue.
Who can appear for a party in the Supreme Court?
Only an AOR files and acts; other advocates need an AOR's instructions.
What did the 2023 BCI Foreign Lawyers Rules allow?
Foreign lawyers and firms may do non-litigious work with reciprocity and annual registration; litigation stays with Indian advocates.
When was the BCI Online Enrolment Portal launched?
2021.

Traps#

Trap 1 · Non-statutory versus statutory The Law Commission is created by a Government notification and is non-statutory. The BCI and State Bar Councils are created by the Advocates Act, 1961 and are statutory. Rule: Law Commission = executive order; BCI and SBC = Act of Parliament.
Trap 2 · Two different terms The BCI Chairman and Vice-Chairman serve 2 years. Elected members of a State Bar Council serve 5 years (extendable by 6 months by the BCI). Rule: BCI office-bearers = 2 years; SBC elected members = 5 years.
Trap 3 · Who may file in the Supreme Court Senior Advocates cannot file cases directly or appear without being briefed through another advocate. Other advocates also cannot act in the SC unless instructed by an AOR. Only an AOR can file and act. Rule: file in the SC = AOR only.
Trap 4 · Number of reports and report subjects The handout gives 277 reports and ties the 276th to data protection and privacy and the 277th to wrongful prosecution. Rule: 277 = wrongful prosecution; 276 = data protection (handout version); 262 = death penalty; 210 = attempt to suicide.
