Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • Fort William College (1800, Wellesley 98-05) was founded to train British civilians, not at the Board's request, not to revive oriental learning, not for Carey's job (Prelims 2020)
  • Sleeman = Thugs (Prelims 1997); Prendergast = Upper Burma 1885-86, Burnes = Afghan frontier killed in the 1st Anglo-Afghan War 1841, Pemberton = Assam/Burma frontier
  • Lytton 76-80 = Forward Policy (Prelims 1999), Ripon 80-84 reversed it; Dufferin 84-88 = Upper Burma, January 1886, the last major extension (Prelims 2000, with the slide's Dalhousie 48-56 nuance)
  • Imperial Preference = tariff privileges for British imports in India, formalised at Ottawa 1932 (Prelims 1999); the five antiquarians Rea, Longhurst, Sewell, Burgess, Elliot = archaeological excavations (Prelims 2023)
  • Champion pairs and the four Orientalist institutions (1781, 1784, 1791, 1800); the Charter Act 1833 did NOT make an Indian the law member of the GG's council (slide 104)

Mains hooks

  • Mains 2014 (10 marks, 150 words): the major political, economic and social developments in the world which motivated the anti-colonial struggle in India: political (American, French, 1830 and 1848, Irish), economic (deindustrialisation, Drain Theory), social (Russia 1917, Wilson 1918, anti-racism), and the carriers Gandhi and Nehru
  • Why ideology came before policy: each one was both a sincere belief and a convenient narrative; always show both layers
  • The arc Orientalism to Authoritarianism to Liberal-Utilitarian-Evangelical to Racism, and the post-1857 six-front shift

Every ideology was a different answer to one question, how exactly should we civilise India, and every one served British power.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: The British told themselves that ruling India was moral (the White Man's Burden), and each ideology under that roof rose or fell with the EIC's confidence: Orientalism while it was unsure, Authoritarianism under Cornwallis 86-93, the three reform ideologies after 1813, and Racism once 1857 had shocked them.

  1. A nation sure of itself. Britain had reasons for pride: the first modern democracy, the Industrial Revolution (c. 1750-1850), victory in the Seven Years' Global War (1756-63), Napoleon beaten (Leipzig 1813, Waterloo 1815) and the rise of capitalism and science. Within 150 years it could convince itself it was destined to lead, and that it owed the world its civilisation. This is the White Man's Burden.
    → So what should the British do in India?
  2. Scared, so Orientalist. Before 1813 the EIC was not militarily confident, and one badly handled insult could trigger mass resistance. Scholars like William Jones found Sanskrit older and more sophisticated than Greek and Latin. So Orientalism said: rule India the India way, with Hindu law, Islamic law, Indian languages and an Indian syllabus. Warren Hastings 73-85 was its champion.
    → Institutions followed the idea.
  3. Four Orientalist institutions. The Calcutta Madrasa came in 1781 (Warren Hastings 73-85), the Asiatic Society of Bengal in 1784 (Sir William Jones), the Sanskrit College at Banaras in 1791 (Jonathan Duncan) and Fort William College in 1800 (Wellesley 98-05) to train British civilians. The Aryan Invasion Theory and kinship through language added a rhetoric of love: we are cousins who have come home.
    → But Orientalism had critics from the start.
  4. My way or highway. The critics said the study of India showed a glorious past and a degenerate present, so restoring the past was pointless. Cornwallis 86-93 ruled India the English way: all-British senior posts, rule of law with the Collector no longer also Judge (Charter Act 1793), the Permanent Settlement 1793 and an Aloof Elite. He had lost America and did not want settlers putting down roots.
    → Between the two, a hybrid appeared.
  5. Indian coat, British hand. Thomas Munro, Governor of Madras 1820-27, governed through Indian forms but kept all power with the EIC: Orientalist in form, Authoritarian in substance. His Ryotwari Settlement copied the ancient State-peasant model but all collection was by EIC officials. Elphinstone, Malcolm and Metcalfe followed him.
    → Then, by 1813, the EIC stopped being cautious.
  6. 1813: confidence, then three ideas. Indian powers had been beaten except Holkar, Bhonsle and the Sikh State, the army was huge, Napoleon was beaten and the Industrial Revolution was in full swing, with a market for British goods to win. Liberalism (Macaulay: Law Commission 1834, Minute on Education 1835, Brown Sahibs), Utilitarianism (Bentham and James Mill: good laws, a strong State, the schoolmaster) and Evangelicalism (Charles Grant, Charter Act 1813, Lex Loci Act 1850) filled the space.
    → Bentinck 28-35's years were the high-water mark.
  7. The Liberal window, 1828-35. From Bentinck 28-35 through the Charter Act 1833 to Macaulay's Minute 1835, Liberal confidence peaked: the Sati Abolition Act 1829, Thuggee suppression under Captain Sleeman (three tools: informants, approvers, swift commissions; over 3,000 Thugs convicted 1831-41), English replacing Persian in higher instruction, and Cornwallis 86-93's Code revised into uniform criminal procedure.
    → Then 1857 broke the story.
  8. The shock of 1857. The Revolt shook the British psychologically and broke the paternalistic narrative. Six fronts shifted: reform retreated, race was read by physical features, the Aryan theory was twisted, Dogs and Indians not allowed, Authoritarianism rose and social reforms slowed after Queen Victoria's Proclamation of 1858. Lytton 76-80 (Vernacular Press Act and Arms Act 1878) and Curzon 99-05 (Partition of Bengal 1905) were its architects.
    → The same hard mood showed on the frontiers.
  9. The Forward Policy and the Great Game. Fearing Tsarist Russia, the British pushed the frontier into Afghanistan: Auckland 36-42's 1st Anglo-Afghan War 1839-42, Ellenborough 42-44's punitive return to Kabul, Lytton 76-80's 2nd war 1878-80 and the Treaty of Gandamak 1879, then Ripon 80-84's reversal. The defensive twin was the Ring Fence, and the Persian flank ended with the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907.
    → And the land-grab ended in Burma.
  10. Upper Burma and Imperial Preference. Dufferin 84-88 annexed Upper Burma in January 1886, the last major extension, deposing King Thibaw. Trade was also bent to the empire: Imperial Preference, formalised at Ottawa in 1932, let British goods enter Indian ports at lower tariffs than German, Japanese or American goods.
    → But the empire's own claims came back to haunt it.
  11. The ideas come home. The White Man's Burden produced its opposite: the American Revolution, the French Revolution 1789, the European revolutions of 1830 and 1848 and the Irish struggle. Later shocks added deindustrialisation and the Drain Theory, the Russian Revolution 1917, Wilson's self-determination and anti-racism. They travelled through people: Gandhi from South Africa (1893-1915) and Nehru from Cambridge.

In simple words#

  • The White Man's Burden is the roof: Western Europe was superior, so the British had a duty to civilise the world. The ideas under it changed with the EIC's confidence.
  • Orientalism (1765-1813): rule India the India way (Hindu law, Islamic law, languages, syllabus). Cornwallis 86-93 then said my way or highway: Indians out of senior posts, Permanent Settlement 1793, an Aloof Elite.
  • After 1813 came three: Liberalism (best ideas, but best = west; Macaulay), Utilitarianism (good laws, strong State; Mill) and Evangelicalism (Christianize to modernize; Grant).
  • 1857 was a shock:
    • reform retreated, race was read by physical features, Dogs and Indians not allowed
    • Lytton 76-80 and Curzon 99-05 were its architects
  • Beside the ideas ran Sleeman and the Thugs, the Forward Policy (Auckland 36-42 to Lytton 76-80, reversed by Ripon 80-84), Upper Burma 1886, Imperial Preference and the foreign currents that fed Indian nationalism.

1 · Why ideology comes before policy#

Story link

Because every ideology was both belief and cover story, policy followed ideas. Ask: what are the two layers, and why show both in Mains?

  • British Imperial Ideologies are the ideas that shaped colonial policy.
  • They were not one fixed set. They changed over time and sometimes competed.
  • They shared one function: they gave the British a way to tell themselves and the world that what they were doing in India was not just profitable but morally right.
  • Every ideology was both a genuine belief held by some Englishmen and a convenient narrative to make imperialism look ethical. Both layers operate at once.
Key takeaway
  • For Mains: always show both layers. Sincere belief and convenient cover story together.
  • Many Englishmen were sincere (Section 5.5), and the ideas still served British power.

2.1 The roots of British civilisational confidence

  • The British had genuine reasons to believe in their superiority:
  • 1. World's first modern democracy: the English Civil War + Glorious Revolution (17th century).
  • 2. Birthplace of the Industrial Revolution (c. 1750-1850).
  • 3. Won the Seven Years' Global War (1756-63): the top global power.
  • 4. Financially backed the coalition that defeated Napoleon at Leipzig (1813)
  • led the allied armies to final victory with Prussia at Waterloo (1815).
  • 5. Capitalism and science transforming British society at an unprecedented pace.
  • A nation that achieves all this within 150 years can convince itself that it is destined to lead the world.
  • Every major imperial power had its own version of WMB.

2.2 WMB as roof, and the counter-ideologies as reaction

  • WMB was the roof over everything else.
  • The specific ideologies below were different answers to the same question: how exactly should we civilise India?
  • WMB also generated its opposite: powerful anti-colonial counter-ideologies worldwide:
  • 1. American Revolution: a political movement from 1765, War of Independence 1776-83.
  • 2. French Revolution (1789): liberty, equality, fraternity.
  • 3. European revolutions of 1830 and 1848.
  • 4. Irish Home Rule movement (1870-1914) and the Irish armed struggle for independence (1916-21).
  • These challenged imperial-superiority assumptions and inspired colonised peoples, including Indian nationalists.
  • (Section 12 develops this: Mains 2014.)

3 · Orientalism (1765-1813): Rule India, the India Way#

Story link

Because the EIC was scared and some wanted to spare India, it chose to rule the India way. Ask: which four things, which four institutions, and which one is the Prelims 2020 answer?

3.1 Orientalism defined

  • In the 18th century the British used language and literature as the measure of civilisation.
  • Scholars like William Jones studied Sanskrit and found it older and grammatically more sophisticated than ancient Greek and Latin.
  • The conclusion: India had been a great ancient civilisation, and European and Indian languages belonged to the same family (today's Indo-European family: genuine scholarship, not propaganda).
  • So Orientalism said: India's ancient past was glorious even if its present was degenerate, so the British should study Indian tradition and rule India by its own customs and laws.
  • In short: Rule India, the India Way.

Teacher's board

  • These four bullets are the practical content of Orientalism.

3.2 Why the EIC adopted Orientalism

  • Two practical reasons:
  • 1. The EIC was militarily not yet confident before 1813.
  • Victory in the Carnatic, Mysore and Maratha Wars was never guaranteed, and one badly handled cultural insult could trigger mass resistance.
  • Ruling through Indian customs reduced that risk.
  • 2. Some Englishmen believed India was not ready for British modernity.
  • Imposing alien systems would do more damage than good.
  • Warren Hastings 73-85 was Orientalism's most prominent champion.
  • He argued that the more the British understood Indian traditions, the lighter the chains of colonialism would feel.

3.3 Orientalism's political angle

  • The British supported the Aryan Invasion Theory: ancient India's cultural greatness was actually brought by Europeans who migrated to India in ancient times.
  • Even while praising India, the British claimed credit.
  • The argument of kinship: Europeans and Indians were separated kin connected through language.
  • It was used to reduce Indian opposition: "we are not foreigners, we are cousins who have come home."
  • This was a rhetoric of love to disguise the reality of exploitation.

4 · Authoritarianism: Cornwallis 86-93 and Conservatism#

Story link

Because critics said the past was dead, Cornwallis 86-93 imposed the English way. Ask: which four steps, and what did Munro's formula add?

4.1 The Cornwallis 86-93 shift

  • Orientalism had critics from the beginning. The study of ancient India had only confirmed that India's past was glorious and its present degenerate.
  • That made restoring the past pointless and called for imposing the British system instead.
  • Cornwallis 86-93 was the first Governor-General to implement this vision systematically.
  • He ruled India, the English Way.
  • Four specific measures:
  • 1. All-British senior administration. He replaced all Indians in senior administrative positions with British officials.
  • His argument:
    • Indian administrators were corrupt and incompetent
    • only British authority could deliver rule of law.
  • (Deeper argument: only by removing Indians from power can the British civilise India.
  • This "corrupt-native-needs-the-honest-foreigner" story was used through 1947 to justify exclusion.)
  • 2. Rule of Law through codification. The Charter Act 1793, influenced by Cornwallis 86-93, established civil law and protection of individual rights and private property.
  • It reintroduced separation of powers at the district level: the District Collector would no longer also be the District Judge.
  • 3. Permanent Settlement (1793) in Bengal, converting zamindars into large landlords on the English model.
  • (Full treatment in the Land Revenue Systems chapter, Chapter 16.)
  • 4. Aloof Elite. EIC officials were discouraged from mixing with Indians. Indian wives were discouraged.
  • Eurasians were barred from EIC service.
  • Officials having Indian wives were barred from purchasing land in India.

About the Permanent Settlement he wrote: "PS 1793 based on English way of agriculture = OUTLIER to Orientalism." That is the reasoning: Orientalism meant ruling the Indian way, but the Permanent Settlement copied the English landlord model, so it breaks the Orientalist pattern and belongs with Authoritarianism.

4.2 The Aloof-Elite doctrine

  • Cornwallis 86-93 had personally commanded the British army's defeat in the American War of Independence (1776-83).
  • He had watched British settlers in the 13 American colonies develop deep roots (family, property, identity) and then fight for independence as their homeland.
  • He was determined not to let the same happen in India.
  • The rule: keep the rulers separate from the ruled. Never let India become home.
  • Wellesley 98-05 shared this authoritarian instinct. Both administrations were control-oriented, centralised and mistrustful of Indian agency.

4.3 Conservatism: Munro, Malcolm, Elphinstone, Metcalfe

  • Thomas Munro (Governor of Madras 1820-27) was a hybrid of Orientalism and Authoritarianism.
  • India should be governed through its own traditions, but the British would govern, with no leadership role for Indians.
  • Total power centralised in EIC hands, but exercised through Indian forms (Indian laws and customs).
  • His Ryotwari Settlement (1801-07, 1820+) in the Madras Presidency was inspired by the ancient Indian model of the State collecting revenue directly from the peasant, without intermediaries.
  • But all collection was done by EIC officials, with no Indians in senior roles.
  • The formula: Orientalist in form, Authoritarian in substance.
  • Mountstuart Elphinstone, John Malcolm and Charles Metcalfe followed the same school.
  • Elphinstone, a disciple of Munro, later implemented the Ryotwari Settlement in Bombay.

5 · Three new ideologies (post 1813): Liberalism, Utilitarianism, Evangelicalism#

Story link

Because the EIC was confident by 1813, three reform ideas took over. Ask: what did each want, and who was the champion of each?

5.1 The post-1813 sharp turn

  • By 1813 the landscape had changed dramatically:
  • 1. The EIC had defeated all major Indian powers
  • only Holkar, Bhonsle and the Sikh State remained as potential rivals
  • 2. The Company's army had grown huge.
  • 3. Britain had defeated Napoleon.
  • 4. The Industrial Revolution was in full swing.
  • The EIC was now confident enough to rule India the British way: openly, aggressively, without apology.
  • There was also a new economic motive: an India with western tastes and western education would become a market for British manufactured goods.
  • Three ideologies filled the space Orientalism had occupied: Liberalism, Utilitarianism, Evangelicalism.

5.2 Liberalism

  • Liberals argued that the EIC had governed India badly under Orientalism.
  • The Great Bengal Famine of 1770 (one crore Indians perished while the EIC doubled land revenue) was cited as proof.
  • India needed to be modernised through the best ideas of the age (best meaning western).
  • Goal: liberate India from feudalism: feudal polity (no democracy), feudal economy (agrarian, closed, unequal), feudal society (birth-based discrimination, no women's rights), and replace it with modern British institutions.
  • Thomas Macaulay, legal member of the GG's council under the Charter Act 1833, was the most prominent Liberal. His three signature interventions:
  • 1. Law Commission (1834): codified western-style laws for British India by 1837.
  • 2. Minute on Indian Education (1835): proposed western education in English to create Brown Sahibs (brown on the outside, British on the inside): a class of Indians as cultural intermediaries between British rulers and the Indian masses.
  • 3. Charter Act 1813 (a Liberal-influenced reform): ended the EIC's monopoly over Indian trade and let non-EIC British businesses enter India freely.
  • This was economic liberalism for British businesses, not for Indian ones.
  • Social reform followed too: Bentinck 28-35 brought the Sati Abolition Act 1829 under Liberal influence (full treatment in Chapter 19).
Handout note
  • A date wrinkle. The slide and the handout list the Charter Act 1813 among Macaulay's "interventions", yet his seat on the GG's council came under the Charter Act 1833.
  • The handout itself calls the 1813 Act a "Liberal-influenced reform", so read it as a Liberal-era measure that opened trade, not as Macaulay's own act.
  • Learn the pair: Law Commission 1834, Minute 1835 are his.

5.3 Utilitarianism

  • Associated with Jeremy Bentham and James Mill.
  • India needed not culture change but good policy and a strong rule of law.
  • The State's job: design laws that maximise benefit for the largest number of people.
  • James Mill's History of British India (1818) declared India's great past a myth and argued India needed an effective schoolmaster: someone to impose good governance from above.
  • Mill joined the Court of Directors of the EIC in London and pushed for the Law Commission in 1834 under Macaulay.
  • Utilitarianism contains an inherent authoritarian streak.
Teacher's board
  • Utilitarianism = "civil servant mind" with four words: effectiveness, max results, implementable, practical.
  • Then: "Bring good laws and a strong State for rule of law." The reasoning: a utilitarian cares about what works, not what is traditional or Christian, so the answer is laws and a strong State.

5.4 Evangelicalism

  • Evangelists argued India's fundamental problem was its religion, which promoted superstition, ignorance, idolatry and tyranny of priests and blocked scientific thinking.
  • Religion shapes values
  • Values shape society. Change the religion, change everything.
  • Their model was Protestant Christianity in western Europe, where Protestant ethics supported the rise of capitalism and modernity.
  • Charles Grant, a member of the Court of Directors and later an MP, influenced the Charter Act 1813 to legalise the entry of Christian missionaries into India.
  • The Lex Loci Act / Removal of Religious-Caste Disabilities Act (1850) allowed a convert to retain inheritance rights in family property, removing one practical obstacle to conversion (full treatment in Chapter 19).
Teacher's board

"Evangelicalism: CHRISTIANIZE India to MODERNIZE India."

Handout alert
  • Charter Act 1813 appears under two ideologies.
  • Liberal side: ended the EIC's trade monopoly (section 5.2).
  • Evangelical side: allowed missionaries (this section). Both are in the handout. Keep the two effects apart.

5.5 Common ground across the three

  • All three pushed for reform of the colonial State and together built the narrative of Benevolent Colonialism: "we are here to help you."
  • Many Englishmen who believed in these ideologies were sincere.
  • And yet all three together contributed to the conditions that produced the Revolt of 1857: cultural, economic and political resistance to a colonial project that had touched almost every aspect of Indian life.

6 · The Liberalism shift 1828-35#

Story link

Because Liberal confidence peaked in 1828-35, four interventions followed. Ask: which four, and what is the Charter Act 1833 trap?

  • The short window from Bentinck 28-35 through the Charter Act 1833 to Macaulay's 1835 Minute is the high-water mark of Liberal confidence in India. Four headline interventions:
  • 1. Sati Abolition Act 1829: criminalised widow-burning (full treatment in Chapter 19).
  • 2. Thuggee Suppression (1830s-40s):
    • Under Captain William Sleeman
    • restored police order on the Grand Trunk roads (Section 8; Prelims 1997)
  • 3. Macaulay's Education Minute 1835: made English the medium of higher instruction, displacing Persian.
  • 4. Cornwallis 86-93 Code progressively revised into a uniform criminal procedure: the foundation Macaulay's 1834 Law Commission would build on.

7 · Racism + Authoritarianism (post 1857)#

Story link

Because 1857 shocked the British, reform retreated and race hardened. Ask: which six fronts, and which two architects?

  • The Revolt of 1857 shook the British not just militarily but psychologically.
  • The scale of Indian violence against British rule (soldiers, civilians, women, children) broke the paternalistic narrative.
  • The British had told themselves they were trustees and reformers. 1857 told them that a very large number of Indians did not see it that way.
Teacher's board

Column 4 of the board: "1857 Revolt = SHOCK for British." "Indians are beyond Reform." "Ideology = DOGS & Indians not allowed." "Up-up Authoritarianism (+) up-up RACISM." (The teacher underlined "Dogs", "Indians" and "not allowed" in green.)

7.1 The six-front sharp shift after 1857

  • 1. Reformist impulse retreated sharply. Liberalism, Utilitarianism and Evangelicalism (Section 5) collapsed.
  • Many concluded that Indians were beyond redemption through reform.
  • 2. Racial sciences shift. European racial classification in the second half of the 19th century shifted from language (the Orientalist criterion) to physical features.
  • Indians were now classed as an inferior and fundamentally different race: not separated kin (Section 3) but a lower order of humanity.
  • 3. Aryan Invasion Theory kept but twisted.
  • Europeans had founded the Vedic civilisation, but by intermarrying with indigenous Indians over millennia they lost their racial superiority and degraded.
  • Even India's ancient greatness was now used as evidence of Indian inferiority.
  • 4. "Dogs and Indians not allowed":
    • Seen on signs at clubs and parks in colonial India
    • It captures the spirit of post-1857 attitudes
  • 5. Authoritarianism increased. The British became distrustful of sharing power with Indians in any meaningful way.
  • 6. Social reforms slowed sharply. Queen Victoria's Proclamation of 1858 explicitly promised religious tolerance and non-interference in Indian customs: a direct acknowledgement that missionary and reformer interference had been a major grievance of 1857.
  • From here, social and political reforms came mainly in response to pressure from Indian leaders, not British initiative.

8 · Law and order paternalism: Sleeman and the Thugs#

Story link

Because the roads were unsafe, a parallel track of law-and-order paternalism ran. Ask: whose department, which three tools, and which distractors in Prelims 1997?

  • Between Orientalism and Liberalism the EIC also ran a parallel track of law-and-order paternalism: reclaiming the roads, the courts and rural policing in the name of the peasant's physical safety.
  • The flagship was the Thuggee and Dacoity Suppression campaign of the 1830s-40s, built around Captain (later Major-General Sir) William Sleeman, under Bentinck 28-35.
  • His Thuggee and Dacoity Department (set up 1835) used three tools:
  • 1. Informant networks infiltrating Thug bands.
  • 2. Approver testimony: captured Thugs gave evidence against their associates in exchange for lighter sentences.
  • 3. Swift judicial commissions: dedicated tribunals delivering rapid trials.
  • Together these dismantled organised highway murder cults that had made long-distance trade on the Grand Trunk Road a life-and-death gamble.
  • Over 3,000 Thugs were convicted between 1831 and 1841.

9 · The Forward Policy in Afghanistan: from Auckland 36-42 to Lytton 76-80#

Story link

Because Russia was feared, the frontier was pushed into Afghanistan. Ask: which four Governors-General, and what did Gandamak 1879 give?

  • The Forward Policy was the militarised face of post-1840s British imperialism on the north-west frontier: Britain should actively push its effective frontier into Afghanistan to pre-empt Tsarist Russian influence, rather than defend a fixed line at the Indus.
  • Its arc ran through multiple Governors-General and is a classic PYQ trap:
  • 1. Auckland 36-42 launched the disastrous 1st Anglo-Afghan War (1839-42) to reinstall Shah Shuja against Dost Mohammad.
  • The retreat from Kabul (January 1842) wiped out almost an entire British-Indian army.
  • 2. Ellenborough 42-44 initiated what later came to be called the Forward Policy: a punitive return to Kabul in 1842 to restore British prestige, followed by a doctrine of pushing the frontier outward.
  • 3. Lytton 76-80 carried forward the Forward Policy and launched the 2nd Anglo-Afghan War (1878-80), forcing the Treaty of Gandamak 1879.
  • The war continued into its 2nd phase after the massacre of British Resident Louis Cavagnari at Kabul.
  • 4. Ripon 80-84 reversed the Forward Policy and withdrew British forces from Afghanistan: the pendulum swing that Liberal governments in London demanded.

9.2 Revision: Russophobia and the Great Game

  • Russophobia: the British fear that Czarist Russia, expanding through Central Asia, would one day reach the north-west land approach to India.
  • The Great Game: this Anglo-Russian rivalry over Central Asia.
  • It shaped British frontier and foreign policy far more than trade.
  • Offensive answer: the Forward Policy, pushing the effective frontier outward through the two Anglo-Afghan Wars (1839-42 and 1878-80).
  • Defensive twin: the Policy of Ring Fence: keeping strong, friendly border States as a buffer around British India, with Afghanistan and Punjab as the buffer in the north-west.
  • The 1838 Tripartite Treaty (Sikh State, EIC and Shah Shuja) was built on this buffer logic (see Chapter 6, Chapter 14).
  • The Persian flank: the same logic produced the Anglo-Persian War 1856-57 and finally the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907, which split Iran into three: a Russian sphere in the north, a neutral buffer in the middle and a British sphere in the south (see Chapter 14).

11 · Imperial Preference#

Story link

Because trade was bent to the empire, tariffs favoured Britain. Ask: what is the key word, and where was it formalised?

  • "Imperial Preference" was a commercial tariff arrangement: the constituent units of the British Empire (Britain, the Dominions and India) granted one another preferential (lower) tariff rates denied to non-Empire competitors.
  • Formalised at the Ottawa Conference of 1932, locking India's trade into an Empire-first structure that subordinated Indian industrial interests to British manufacturing needs.
  • (Full treatment in the Drain Theory chapter, Chapter 27.)
  • Effect on India: British goods entered Indian ports at a lower tariff than German, Japanese or American competitors.
  • India's tariff regime systematically advantaged the very British manufacturing economy that Indian industries were trying to compete with.
Handout alert
  • Prelims 1999 trap: answer (a) special privileges on British imports in India.
  • (b) racial discrimination is a different phenomenon (Section 7).
  • (c) subordination of Indian interest is too vague, a general description of colonialism and not the specific definition.
  • (d) preference to British political agents over Princes is Political Department protocol. The key word is tariff.

12 · Foreign ideologies motivating India's anti-colonial struggle#

Story link

Because the burden produced its opposite, world events fed Indian nationalism. Ask: which three buckets and which two carriers answer Mains 2014?

Handout note
  • Handout numbering glitch. Section 12's sub-points are numbered 13.1 to 13.3 in the handout (and the heading before the summary is "Sec. 13").
  • Here they are called 12.1 to 12.3.
  • WMB (Section 2) produced its opposite: powerful anti-colonial counter-ideologies across the globe that travelled back into India through newspapers, returning students and political exiles.

12.1 Pre-1900 political and ideological currents

  • 1. American Revolution (1765-83): political movement from 1765, War of Independence 1776-83.
  • 2. French Revolution of 1789: liberty, equality, fraternity.
  • 3. European revolutions of 1830 and 1848: democratic uprisings across the continent.
  • 4. Irish Home Rule (1870-1914) and Irish armed struggle (1916-21) against Britain: direct anti-British inspiration.
  • All challenged imperial superiority and inspired colonised peoples worldwide.

12.2 Three 20th-century shocks that radicalised Indian nationalism

  • 1. Industrial Revolution's destructive impact on Indian handloom: deindustrialisation fed the Drain Theory of Dadabhai Naoroji and R.C. Dutt.
  • 2. Russian Revolution of 1917: gave the Indian left a peasant-worker mobilisation model and an anti-imperial vocabulary.
  • 3. Post-WWI principle of self-determination (Wilson's Fourteen Points, 1918) and rising anti-racism: made continued British rule increasingly indefensible in international forums.

12.3 Returning Indian leaders brought these currents home

  • The world-developments travelled through people, not ideas alone:
  • 1. Gandhi from South Africa: anti-colonial training from Indian-diaspora struggles in South Africa (1893-1915).
  • 2. Nehru from Cambridge: Western liberal-democratic political theory absorbed during his Cambridge studies, applied in Indian Congress politics.

Recall sheet#

Read the question, answer in your head, then tap to check.

Why do ideologies come before policy?They gave the British a moral story for what they did in India; each was both a sincere belief and a convenient narrative.
What is the White Man's Burden?The belief that Western European civilisation was superior, so the British had a duty to civilise the rest of the world; the roof over every other ideology.
Five roots of British confidence?First modern democracy (Civil War, Glorious Revolution); Industrial Revolution c. 1750-1850; Seven Years' War won 1756-63; Napoleon beaten (Leipzig 1813, Waterloo 1815); capitalism and science.
Four counter-currents WMB produced?American Revolution (1765; war 1776-83); French Revolution 1789; European revolutions 1830 and 1848; Irish Home Rule 1870-1914 and armed struggle 1916-21.
Orientalism in the teacher's words?"Rule India the India Way": Hindu law, Islamic law, Indian languages, Indian syllabus in schools and colleges.
Why did the EIC adopt Orientalism?Not yet militarily confident before 1813 (one insult could spark mass resistance); some Englishmen thought India not ready for British modernity. Champion: Warren Hastings 73-85, "lighter chains".
Four Orientalist institutions?Calcutta Madrasa 1781 (Warren Hastings 73-85, GG); Asiatic Society 1784 (William Jones, Puisne Judge); Sanskrit College Banaras 1791 (Jonathan Duncan, Resident); Fort William College 1800 (Wellesley 98-05, GG).
Prelims 2020: why Fort William College?To train British civilians (newly recruited EIC civil servants) in Indian languages and administration; not Board orders, not oriental revival, not Carey's employment.
Political angle of Orientalism?Aryan Invasion Theory (Europeans brought India's greatness) and kinship through language ("cousins who have come home"): a rhetoric of love to disguise exploitation.
Cornwallis 86-93's four measures?All-British senior administration; Rule of Law by codification (Charter Act 1793; Collector not Judge); Permanent Settlement 1793; Aloof Elite.
Why the Aloof-Elite doctrine?He had lost the American War of Independence (1776-83) and saw settlers put down roots and fight for their homeland: keep the rulers separate, never let India become home.
Conservatism?Munro, Malcolm, Elphinstone, Metcalfe: Orientalist in form, Authoritarian in substance (Munro's Ryotwari in Madras 1801-07, 1820+; Elphinstone took it to Bombay).
What changed by 1813?All major Indian powers beaten except Holkar, Bhonsle and the Sikh State; huge army; Napoleon beaten; Industrial Revolution; plus a market motive for British goods.
Liberalism's goal and proof?Liberate India from feudalism (polity, economy, society) with British institutions; proof of Orientalist misrule: the 1770 famine, one crore dead while revenue doubled.
Macaulay's three interventions?Law Commission 1834 (codes by 1837); Minute on Education 1835 (English, Brown Sahibs); the Charter Act 1813 (Liberal-influenced end of the EIC trade monopoly).
Utilitarianism in one line?Bentham and James Mill: good policy and strong rule of law, laws for the greatest number; Mill's History of British India 1818 wanted an effective schoolmaster; he pushed the Law Commission.
Evangelicalism in one line?Change India's religion to change everything; Protestant model; Charles Grant's influence on the Charter Act 1813 legalised missionaries; Lex Loci Act 1850 protected a convert's inheritance.
Common ground of the three?Reform of the State and a narrative of Benevolent Colonialism ("we are here to help you"); sincere, yet they helped produce 1857.
Liberalism Shift 1828-35, four items?Sati Abolition 1829; Thuggee suppression; Macaulay's Minute 1835 (English replaces Persian in higher instruction); Cornwallis 86-93 Code revised into uniform criminal procedure.
Six-front shift after 1857?Reform retreated; race by physical features; Aryan theory twisted; "Dogs and Indians not allowed"; Authoritarianism up; social reforms slowed (Queen's Proclamation 1858).
Lytton 76-80 and Curzon 99-05?Lytton 76-80: Vernacular Press Act and Arms Act 1878; Curzon 99-05: Partition of Bengal 1905. Both: prestige, paramountcy, racial hierarchy.
Sleeman's three tools and the numbers?Informant networks, approver testimony, swift judicial commissions; Department set up 1835; over 3,000 Thugs convicted 1831-41.
Prelims 1997 distractors?Prendergast = Upper Burma 1885-86; Burnes = killed in the 1st Anglo-Afghan War 1841; Pemberton = Assam/Burma frontier.
Forward Policy arc?Auckland 36-42 (1st war 1839-42); Ellenborough 42-44 (punitive return to Kabul 1842: handout says initiated, slide 91 says ended); Lytton 76-80 (2nd war 1878-80); Ripon 80-84 reversed.
Gandamak 1879 terms?A mini subsidiary alliance: Resident, Army, foreign policy taken, Khyber Pass and some lands to British, 6 lakh a year to the Amir; war continued after Cavagnari's massacre.
Russophobia and the Great Game?British fear of Czarist Russia reaching India via Central Asia; the Anglo-Russian rivalry; Forward Policy = offence, Ring Fence = defence (Afghanistan and Punjab as buffers; 1838 Tripartite Treaty).
Persian flank?Anglo-Persian War 1856-57; Anglo-Russian Convention 1907 split Iran: Russian north, neutral middle, British south.
Prelims 2000: last major extension?Dufferin 84-88, Upper Burma, January 1886 (3rd Anglo-Burmese War 1885-86, Thibaw to Ratnagiri); slide 108 notes the famous alternative, Dalhousie 48-56.
Imperial Preference?Tariff arrangement among Britain, Dominions and India giving lower rates than to non-Empire rivals; formalised at Ottawa 1932; British goods entered at lower tariff than German, Japanese or American.
Mains 2014 skeleton?Intro: WMB bred counter-ideologies carried by newspapers, students, exiles. Body: political (American, French, 1830 and 1848, Irish); economic (Industrial Revolution deindustrialisation, Drain Theory); social (Russia 1917, Wilson 1918 self-determination, anti-racism); carriers (Gandhi from South Africa 1893-1915, Nehru from Cambridge). Closing: the empire's own moral claim became its weak point.
Five antiquarians (Prelims 2023)?Rea (Amaravati, Bhattiprolu), Longhurst (Nagarjunakonda, Hampi), Sewell (A Forgotten Empire), Burgess (first Director of the Archaeological Survey of Western and Southern India), Elliot (Madras Civil Service; Andhra Buddhist sites and coins): all archaeology.
Which ideologies end in 1857 and which begin there?Liberalism, Utilitarianism and Evangelicalism (1813-57) end; Racism plus Authoritarianism begins (post-1857).
