1 · Why ideology comes before policy#

  • British Imperial Ideologies are the ideas that shaped colonial policy.
  • They were not one fixed set. They changed over time and sometimes competed.
  • They shared one function: they gave the British a way to tell themselves and the world that what they were doing in India was not just profitable but morally right.
  • Every ideology was both a genuine belief held by some Englishmen and a convenient narrative to make imperialism look ethical. Both layers operate at once.
Remember

For Mains: always show both layers. Sincere belief and convenient cover story together. Many Englishmen were sincere (Section 5.5), and the ideas still served British power.

2 · Foundation: the White Man's Burden (WMB)#

Note

No class notes beyond the board. The typed slides 69-71 repeat the handout; the board on slide 65 is the only ink (below).

All other ideologies rested on one belief: Western European civilisation was superior, so the British had a responsibility to civilise the rest of the world. This is the White Man's Burden (WMB).

Teacher's board

WMB = "Civilize the rest of the world" and "We are superior". His bracket gives the reasons for the confidence: the Industrial Revolution 1750-1850: first (Britain was first); the Seven Years' Global War 1756-63: won; the earliest of democracies; and empire. (After "1st" the line trails off, and slide 70 of the handout carries the rest.)

2.1 The roots of British civilisational confidence

The British had genuine reasons to believe in their superiority:

  1. World's first modern democracy: the English Civil War + Glorious Revolution (17th century).
  2. Birthplace of the Industrial Revolution (c. 1750-1850).
  3. Won the Seven Years' Global War (1756-63): the top global power.
  4. Financially backed the coalition that defeated Napoleon at Leipzig (1813); led the allied armies to final victory with Prussia at Waterloo (1815).
  5. Capitalism and science transforming British society at an unprecedented pace.

A nation that achieves all this within 150 years can convince itself that it is destined to lead the world. Every major imperial power had its own version of WMB.

Memory hook

"Democracy, Machines, War, Napoleon, Science": D-M-W-N-S.

Decode: first modern Democracy (Civil War + Glorious Revolution, 17th century); Machines = Industrial Revolution (c. 1750-1850); Won the Seven Years' Global War (1756-63); Napoleon beaten (Leipzig 1813, Waterloo 1815); Science and capitalism.

Why it sticks: five proud steps in time order, the 17th century to 1815.

Memory hook

Leipzig 13, Waterloo 15.

Decode: Britain financed the coalition that won at Leipzig (1813) and led the allied armies with Prussia to final victory at Waterloo (1815).

Why it sticks: the two years are two steps apart, 13 then 15: money first, sword second.

2.2 WMB as roof, and the counter-ideologies as reaction

  • WMB was the roof over everything else. The specific ideologies below were different answers to the same question: how exactly should we civilise India?
  • WMB also generated its opposite: powerful anti-colonial counter-ideologies worldwide:
  • American Revolution: a political movement from 1765, War of Independence 1776-83.
  • French Revolution (1789): liberty, equality, fraternity.
  • European revolutions of 1830 and 1848.
  • Irish Home Rule movement (1870-1914) and the Irish armed struggle for independence (1916-21).
  • These challenged imperial-superiority assumptions and inspired colonised peoples, including Indian nationalists. (Section 12 develops this: Mains 2014.)
Note

Slide 71 shows only two of the four counter-ideologies (the American Revolution and the Irish struggle). The handout lists all four; Section 12 repeats the four. Use the handout's four.

Memory hook

"A-F-E-I": the four counter-currents.

Decode: American Revolution (from 1765; war 1776-83), French Revolution (1789), European revolutions (1830 and 1848), Irish Home Rule (1870-1914; armed struggle 1916-21).

Why it sticks: "AFEI" is simply the order in time.

3 · Orientalism (1765-1813): Rule India, the India Way#

3.1 Orientalism defined

  • In the 18th century the British used language and literature as the measure of civilisation.
  • Scholars like William Jones studied Sanskrit and found it older and grammatically more sophisticated than ancient Greek and Latin.
  • The conclusion: India had been a great ancient civilisation, and European and Indian languages belonged to the same family (today's Indo-European family: genuine scholarship, not propaganda).
  • So Orientalism said: India's ancient past was glorious even if its present was degenerate, so the British should study Indian tradition and rule India by its own customs and laws.
  • In short: Rule India, the India Way.
Handout alert

A date slip on slide 72. The typed slide writes William Jones as "(late 19th c)"; the handout places this scholarship in the 18th century, and the Asiatic Society that Jones founded is dated 1784. Use the handout: 18th century.

Teacher's board

On his board the teacher glosses "Oriental" as "related to east" and writes "Rule India the INDIA WAY" with four bullets: Hindu law, Islamic law, Indian languages, Indian syllabus in schools and colleges. These four bullets are the practical content of Orientalism.

Memory hook

"HILL": the four things Orientalism ruled by.

Decode: Hindu law, Islamic law, Indian Languages, Indian syllabus (Learning in schools and colleges).

Why it sticks: the teacher's four bullets fit the word HILL; Orientalism = "go up the hill of Indian tradition".

3.2 Why the EIC adopted Orientalism

Two practical reasons:

  1. The EIC was militarily not yet confident before 1813. Victory in the Carnatic, Mysore and Maratha Wars was never guaranteed, and one badly handled cultural insult could trigger mass resistance. Ruling through Indian customs reduced that risk.
  2. Some Englishmen believed India was not ready for British modernity. Imposing alien systems would do more damage than good.

Warren Hastings 73-85 was Orientalism's most prominent champion. He argued that the more the British understood Indian traditions, the lighter the chains of colonialism would feel.

Memory hook

"Scared and Spared": two reasons for Orientalism.

Decode: the EIC was scared (not militarily confident before 1813; one insult could spark mass resistance) and wanted to spare India from alien systems (some Englishmen thought India not ready for British modernity). Champion: Warren Hastings 73-85, "lighter chains".

Why it sticks: scared for self, sparing the Indian.

3.3 Orientalism's political angle

  • The British supported the Aryan Invasion Theory: ancient India's cultural greatness was actually brought by Europeans who migrated to India in ancient times. Even while praising India, the British claimed credit.
  • The argument of kinship: Europeans and Indians were separated kin connected through language. It was used to reduce Indian opposition: "we are not foreigners, we are cousins who have come home."
  • This was a rhetoric of love to disguise the reality of exploitation.
Memory hook

Why it sticks: an exploiter who calls himself family.

4 · Authoritarianism: Cornwallis 86-93 and Conservatism#

4.1 The Cornwallis 86-93 shift

  • Orientalism had critics from the beginning. The study of ancient India had only confirmed that India's past was glorious and its present degenerate. That made restoring the past pointless and called for imposing the British system instead.
  • Cornwallis 86-93 was the first Governor-General to implement this vision systematically. He ruled India, the English Way.

Four specific measures:

  1. All-British senior administration. He replaced all Indians in senior administrative positions with British officials. His argument: Indian administrators were corrupt and incompetent; only British authority could deliver rule of law. (Deeper argument: only by removing Indians from power can the British civilise India. This "corrupt-native-needs-the-honest-foreigner" story was used through 1947 to justify exclusion.)
  2. Rule of Law through codification. The Charter Act 1793, influenced by Cornwallis 86-93, established civil law and protection of individual rights and private property. It reintroduced separation of powers at the district level: the District Collector would no longer also be the District Judge.
  3. Permanent Settlement (1793) in Bengal, converting zamindars into large landlords on the English model. (Full treatment in the Land Revenue Systems chapter, Chapter 16.)
  4. Aloof Elite. EIC officials were discouraged from mixing with Indians. Indian wives were discouraged. Eurasians were barred from EIC service. Officials having Indian wives were barred from purchasing land in India.
Teacher's board

Authoritarianism = "MY WAY OR HIGHWAY" and "Removed Indians from all senior positions." About the Permanent Settlement he wrote: "PS 1793 based on English way of agriculture = OUTLIER to Orientalism." That is the reasoning: Orientalism meant ruling the Indian way, but the Permanent Settlement copied the English landlord model, so it breaks the Orientalist pattern and belongs with Authoritarianism.

Memory hook

"A-R-P-A": Cornwallis 86-93's four steps.

Decode: All-British senior posts (Indians removed); Rule of law by codification (Charter Act 1793; Collector is no longer Judge); Permanent Settlement 1793 (zamindars as English-style landlords); Aloof Elite (no mixing, no Indian wives, Eurasians barred, no land purchase for officers with Indian wives).

Why it sticks: "ARPA" like "Arpa", one tidy list of four.

Memory hook

Collector or Judge, not both.

Decode: Charter Act 1793 (Cornwallis 86-93's influence) separated powers at the district level: the District Collector no longer also functions as the District Judge.

Why it sticks: one chair, one job.

4.2 The Aloof-Elite doctrine

  • Cornwallis 86-93 had personally commanded the British army's defeat in the American War of Independence (1776-83).
  • He had watched British settlers in the 13 American colonies develop deep roots (family, property, identity) and then fight for independence as their homeland.
  • He was determined not to let the same happen in India.
  • The rule: keep the rulers separate from the ruled. Never let India become home.
  • Wellesley 98-05 shared this authoritarian instinct. Both administrations were control-oriented, centralised and mistrustful of Indian agency.
Memory hook

"Lost America, so no roots in India."

Decode: Cornwallis 86-93 lost in the American War of Independence (1776-83); British settlers in the 13 colonies grew roots and fought for their homeland; so in India: no mixing, no marriage, no land, never let India become home.

Why it sticks: a lost war turned into a rule.

4.3 Conservatism: Munro, Malcolm, Elphinstone, Metcalfe

  • Thomas Munro (Governor of Madras 1820-27) was a hybrid of Orientalism and Authoritarianism. India should be governed through its own traditions, but the British would govern, with no leadership role for Indians. Total power centralised in EIC hands, but exercised through Indian forms (Indian laws and customs).
  • His Ryotwari Settlement (1801-07, 1820+) in the Madras Presidency was inspired by the ancient Indian model of the State collecting revenue directly from the peasant, without intermediaries. But all collection was done by EIC officials, with no Indians in senior roles.
  • The formula: Orientalist in form, Authoritarian in substance.
  • Mountstuart Elphinstone, John Malcolm and Charles Metcalfe followed the same school. Elphinstone, a disciple of Munro, later implemented the Ryotwari Settlement in Bombay.
Memory hook

"Indian coat, British hand."

Decode: Munro's Conservatism = Orientalist in form (Indian traditions, Ryotwari's direct State-peasant model) and Authoritarian in substance (all collection by EIC officials; no Indians in senior roles). School: Munro, Malcolm, Elphinstone, Metcalfe (Elphinstone took Ryotwari to Bombay).

Why it sticks: the coat is Indian, the hand inside it is British.

5 · Three new ideologies (post 1813): Liberalism, Utilitarianism, Evangelicalism#

5.1 The post-1813 sharp turn

By 1813 the landscape had changed dramatically:

  1. The EIC had defeated all major Indian powers; only Holkar, Bhonsle and the Sikh State remained as potential rivals.
  2. The Company's army had grown huge.
  3. Britain had defeated Napoleon.
  4. The Industrial Revolution was in full swing.

The EIC was now confident enough to rule India the British way: openly, aggressively, without apology. There was also a new economic motive: an India with western tastes and western education would become a market for British manufactured goods. Three ideologies filled the space Orientalism had occupied: Liberalism, Utilitarianism, Evangelicalism.

Memory hook

"Four wins, one market": why 1813 turned.

Decode: four changes (Indian powers beaten except Holkar, Bhonsle, Sikh State; a huge army; Napoleon beaten; Industrial Revolution in full swing) plus one motive: a market for British goods through western tastes and education.

Why it sticks: strength first, then shopkeeping.

Memory hook

Three left standing: H-B-S.

Decode: Holkar, Bhonsle, Sikh State were the only potential rivals left by 1813.

Why it sticks: "HBS" like a station code, three stops left.

5.2 Liberalism

  • Liberals argued that the EIC had governed India badly under Orientalism. The Great Bengal Famine of 1770 (one crore Indians perished while the EIC doubled land revenue) was cited as proof.
  • India needed to be modernised through the best ideas of the age (best meaning western). Goal: liberate India from feudalism: feudal polity (no democracy), feudal economy (agrarian, closed, unequal), feudal society (birth-based discrimination, no women's rights), and replace it with modern British institutions.
  • Thomas Macaulay, legal member of the GG's council under the Charter Act 1833, was the most prominent Liberal. His three signature interventions:
  1. Law Commission (1834): codified western-style laws for British India by 1837.
  2. Minute on Indian Education (1835): proposed western education in English to create Brown Sahibs (brown on the outside, British on the inside): a class of Indians as cultural intermediaries between British rulers and the Indian masses.
  3. Charter Act 1813 (a Liberal-influenced reform): ended the EIC's monopoly over Indian trade and let non-EIC British businesses enter India freely. This was economic liberalism for British businesses, not for Indian ones.

Social reform followed too: Bentinck 28-35 brought the Sati Abolition Act 1829 under Liberal influence (full treatment in Chapter 19).

Note

A date wrinkle. The slide and the handout list the Charter Act 1813 among Macaulay's "interventions", yet his seat on the GG's council came under the Charter Act 1833. The handout itself calls the 1813 Act a "Liberal-influenced reform", so read it as a Liberal-era measure that opened trade, not as Macaulay's own act. 5.3 Utilitarianism

  • Associated with Jeremy Bentham and James Mill. India needed not culture change but good policy and a strong rule of law. The State's job: design laws that maximise benefit for the largest number of people.
  • James Mill's History of British India (1818) declared India's great past a myth and argued India needed an effective schoolmaster: someone to impose good governance from above.
  • Mill joined the Court of Directors of the EIC in London and pushed for the Law Commission in 1834 under Macaulay.
  • Utilitarianism contains an inherent authoritarian streak.

5.4 Evangelicalism

  • Evangelists argued India's fundamental problem was its religion, which promoted superstition, ignorance, idolatry and tyranny of priests and blocked scientific thinking. Religion shapes values; values shape society. Change the religion, change everything.
  • Their model was Protestant Christianity in western Europe, where Protestant ethics supported the rise of capitalism and modernity.
  • Charles Grant, a member of the Court of Directors and later an MP, influenced the Charter Act 1813 to legalise the entry of Christian missionaries into India.
  • The Lex Loci Act / Removal of Religious-Caste Disabilities Act (1850) allowed a convert to retain inheritance rights in family property, removing one practical obstacle to conversion (full treatment in Chapter 19).

5.5 Common ground across the three

6 · The Liberalism shift 1828-35#

7 · Racism + Authoritarianism (post 1857)#

The Revolt of 1857 shook the British not just militarily but psychologically. The scale of Indian violence against British rule (soldiers, civilians, women, children) broke the paternalistic narrative. The British had told themselves they were trustees and reformers. 1857 told them that a very large number of Indians did not see it that way.

Teacher's board

Column 4 of the board: "1857 Revolt = SHOCK for British." "Indians are beyond Reform." "Ideology = DOGS & Indians not allowed." "Up-up Authoritarianism (+) up-up RACISM." (The teacher underlined "Dogs", "Indians" and "not allowed" in green.)

7.1 The six-front sharp shift after 1857

  1. Reformist impulse retreated sharply. Liberalism, Utilitarianism and Evangelicalism (Section 5) collapsed. Many concluded that Indians were beyond redemption through reform.
  2. Racial sciences shift. European racial classification in the second half of the 19th century shifted from language (the Orientalist criterion) to physical features. Indians were now classed as an inferior and fundamentally different race: not separated kin (Section 3) but a lower order of humanity.
  3. Aryan Invasion Theory kept but twisted. Europeans had founded the Vedic civilisation, but by intermarrying with indigenous Indians over millennia they lost their racial superiority and degraded. Even India's ancient greatness was now used as evidence of Indian inferiority.
  4. "Dogs and Indians not allowed": seen on signs at clubs and parks in colonial India; it captures the spirit of post-1857 attitudes.
  5. Authoritarianism increased. The British became distrustful of sharing power with Indians in any meaningful way.
  6. Social reforms slowed sharply. Queen Victoria's Proclamation of 1858 explicitly promised religious tolerance and non-interference in Indian customs: a direct acknowledgement that missionary and reformer interference had been a major grievance of 1857. From here, social and political reforms came mainly in response to pressure from Indian leaders, not British initiative.

8 · Law and order paternalism: Sleeman and the Thugs#

Between Orientalism and Liberalism the EIC also ran a parallel track of law-and-order paternalism: reclaiming the roads, the courts and rural policing in the name of the peasant's physical safety.

The flagship was the Thuggee and Dacoity Suppression campaign of the 1830s-40s, built around Captain (later Major-General Sir) William Sleeman, under Bentinck 28-35. His Thuggee and Dacoity Department (set up 1835) used three tools:

  1. Informant networks infiltrating Thug bands.
  2. Approver testimony: captured Thugs gave evidence against their associates in exchange for lighter sentences.
  3. Swift judicial commissions: dedicated tribunals delivering rapid trials.

Together these dismantled organised highway murder cults that had made long-distance trade on the Grand Trunk Road a life-and-death gamble. Over 3,000 Thugs were convicted between 1831 and 1841.

9 · The Forward Policy in Afghanistan: from Auckland 36-42 to Lytton 76-80#

The Forward Policy was the militarised face of post-1840s British imperialism on the north-west frontier: Britain should actively push its effective frontier into Afghanistan to pre-empt Tsarist Russian influence, rather than defend a fixed line at the Indus. Its arc ran through multiple Governors-General and is a classic PYQ trap:

  1. Auckland 36-42 launched the disastrous 1st Anglo-Afghan War (1839-42) to reinstall Shah Shuja against Dost Mohammad. The retreat from Kabul (January 1842) wiped out almost an entire British-Indian army.
  2. Ellenborough 42-44 initiated what later came to be called the Forward Policy: a punitive return to Kabul in 1842 to restore British prestige, followed by a doctrine of pushing the frontier outward.
  3. Lytton 76-80 carried forward the Forward Policy and launched the 2nd Anglo-Afghan War (1878-80), forcing the Treaty of Gandamak 1879. The war continued into its 2nd phase after the massacre of British Resident Louis Cavagnari at Kabul.
  4. Ripon 80-84 reversed the Forward Policy and withdrew British forces from Afghanistan: the pendulum swing that Liberal governments in London demanded.

9.2 Revision: Russophobia and the Great Game

  • Russophobia: the British fear that Czarist Russia, expanding through Central Asia, would one day reach the north-west land approach to India.
  • The Great Game: this Anglo-Russian rivalry over Central Asia. It shaped British frontier and foreign policy far more than trade.
  • Offensive answer: the Forward Policy, pushing the effective frontier outward through the two Anglo-Afghan Wars (1839-42 and 1878-80).
  • Defensive twin: the Policy of Ring Fence: keeping strong, friendly border States as a buffer around British India, with Afghanistan and Punjab as the buffer in the north-west. The 1838 Tripartite Treaty (Sikh State, EIC and Shah Shuja) was built on this buffer logic (see Chapter 6, Chapter 14).
  • The Persian flank: the same logic produced the Anglo-Persian War 1856-57 and finally the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907, which split Iran into three: a Russian sphere in the north, a neutral buffer in the middle and a British sphere in the south (see Chapter 14).

11 · Imperial Preference#

  • "Imperial Preference" was a commercial tariff arrangement: the constituent units of the British Empire (Britain, the Dominions and India) granted one another preferential (lower) tariff rates denied to non-Empire competitors.
  • Formalised at the Ottawa Conference of 1932, locking India's trade into an Empire-first structure that subordinated Indian industrial interests to British manufacturing needs. (Full treatment in the Drain Theory chapter, Chapter 27.)
  • Effect on India: British goods entered Indian ports at a lower tariff than German, Japanese or American competitors. India's tariff regime systematically advantaged the very British manufacturing economy that Indian industries were trying to compete with.
Handout alert

Prelims 1999 trap: answer (a) special privileges on British imports in India. (b) racial discrimination is a different phenomenon (Section 7). (c) subordination of Indian interest is too vague, a general description of colonialism and not the specific definition. (d) preference to British political agents over Princes is Political Department protocol. The key word is tariff.

Memory hook

Imperial Preference = tariff = British imports first.

Decode: Britain, Dominions and India gave each other lower tariffs than non-Empire rivals; formalised at Ottawa 1932; British goods paid less than German, Japanese or American goods at Indian ports.

Why it sticks: handout hook, copied as written; "Ottawa 32" = a trade fence in the year 1932.

12 · Foreign ideologies motivating India's anti-colonial struggle#

Note

Handout numbering glitch. Section 12's sub-points are numbered 13.1 to 13.3 in the handout (and the heading before the summary is "Sec. 13"). Here they are called 12.1 to 12.3.

WMB (Section 2) produced its opposite: powerful anti-colonial counter-ideologies across the globe that travelled back into India through newspapers, returning students and political exiles.

12.1 Pre-1900 political and ideological currents

  1. American Revolution (1765-83): political movement from 1765, War of Independence 1776-83.
  2. French Revolution of 1789: liberty, equality, fraternity.
  3. European revolutions of 1830 and 1848: democratic uprisings across the continent.
  4. Irish Home Rule (1870-1914) and Irish armed struggle (1916-21) against Britain: direct anti-British inspiration.

All challenged imperial superiority and inspired colonised peoples worldwide.

12.2 Three 20th-century shocks that radicalised Indian nationalism

  1. Industrial Revolution's destructive impact on Indian handloom: deindustrialisation fed the Drain Theory of Dadabhai Naoroji and R.C. Dutt.
  2. Russian Revolution of 1917: gave the Indian left a peasant-worker mobilisation model and an anti-imperial vocabulary.
  3. Post-WWI principle of self-determination (Wilson's Fourteen Points, 1918) and rising anti-racism: made continued British rule increasingly indefensible in international forums.

12.3 Returning Indian leaders brought these currents home

The world-developments travelled through people, not ideas alone:

  1. Gandhi from South Africa: anti-colonial training from Indian-diaspora struggles in South Africa (1893-1915).
  2. Nehru from Cambridge: Western liberal-democratic political theory absorbed during his Cambridge studies, applied in Indian Congress politics.
