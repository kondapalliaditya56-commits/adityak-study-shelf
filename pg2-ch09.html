1 · Right TO Privacy · DPDP · Right TO BE Forgotten · Digital Access#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 8-10. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Privacy became a Fundamental Right in Puttaswamy (2017), overruling the older view. It can be limited only by law, for a legitimate aim, if necessary and proportionate. Know surveillance (s.69, Pegasus), DPDP Act 2023, RTI vs privacy, right to be forgotten, personality rights and digital access.

Judicial Evolution

  1. 1954 / 1962
    M. P. Sharma and Kharak Singh: no general right to privacy; but Kharak Singh struck night visits by police
  2. 1997
    PUCL: phone tapping violates privacy (Art 21) and 19(1)(a); safeguards: lawful authorisation, limited duration, review
  3. 2017
    K. S. Puttaswamy: nine judges, unanimous: privacy is a Fundamental Right under Arts 14, 19, 21

Puttaswamy holdings

  • personal autonomy: body, identity, food, relationships, sexual orientation
  • informational privacy: against unjustified collection, use, disclosure of data
  • dignity and equality: belongs to everyone, not an elitist privilege
  • Overruled earlier denial and the ADM Jabalpur (1976) position
  • Base for data protection, LGBTQ+ rights and limits on surveillance

Restrictions: four tests

  • Legality (basis in law)
  • Legitimate aim
  • Necessity
  • Proportionality
Memory aid · LLNP

Privacy test: Legality · Legitimate aim · Necessity · Proportionality. (own mnemonic)

Contemporary Issues

Surveillance

  • s.69, IT Act 2000: interception, monitoring, decryption on specified grounds with safeguards
  • Pegasus raised surveillance, accountability and chilling-effect worries
  • Surveillance must be lawful, necessary, proportionate, overseen

DPDP Act, 2023

  • Protects digital personal data while allowing lawful processing
  • Consent, data security, individual rights, grievance redressal
  • DPDP Rules, 2025 give phased implementation
  • concerns: broad State exemptions, weak independent oversight
  • RTI link:
    • S.8(1)(j) RTI Act personal-information exemption
    • The DPDP Act amends it: privacy vs transparency

International frame

  • Art 17 ICCPR (no arbitrary interference with privacy)
  • Justice A. P. Shah Committee (2012) on comprehensive privacy protection

Right TO BE Forgotten

Idea and status

  • Seek removal, masking or restricted access to personal information whose public availability is no longer justified
  • A person should not be stigmatised for life by irrelevant past data
  • Several High Courts examined it under informational privacy, dignity, reputation (de-indexing, masking identities)
  • Not absolute
Memory aid · GCPRR

Concerns: Global internet · Censorship risk · Public records · RTI · Rewriting history. (own mnemonic)

Concerns spelt out

  • removed on one platform, available elsewhere
  • can suppress legitimate criticism
  • erodes completeness of public records
  • clashes with right to information
  • May rewrite history
  • Balance privacy and rehabilitation with expression, accountability, preserved records

Personality Rights AND Digital Access

Personality rights

  • Protect name, image, voice, likeness from unauthorised commercial use
  • Tied to privacy, reputation, publicity
  • Anil Kapoor (2023) and Jackie Shroff (2024): Delhi High Court protection
  • AI threats:
    • Deepfakes, voice cloning
    • But over-protection can hurt satire, parody, art (Art 19(1)(a))
  1. 2019
    Faheema Shirin (Kerala HC): internet access linked to education and privacy under Art 21
  2. 2020
    Anuradha Bhasin: expression and trade via the internet protected; restrictions need legality and proportionality; indefinite shutdowns impermissible
  3. 2025
    Pragya Prasun and Amar Jain: digital accessibility part of right to life and dignity, esp. for persons with disabilities
Way forward / key point

Digital governance must promote inclusion, not create new barriers to rights. Digital exclusion can block access to ID, banking, scholarships and welfare.

2 · Personal Autonomy · LIFE Partner · MTP · Lgbtq+ Rights#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 10-12. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Art 21 protects the choice of partner (Hadiya), reproductive autonomy (MTP Act) and sexual and gender identity (NALSA, Navtej). Supriyo (2023) refused a right to same-sex marriage and left it to Parliament. Note the 2026 amendment to the Transgender Act and BNS dropping s.377.

Choice OF Partner

Note

Shafin Jahan v. Asokan K. M. (2018), Hadiya case: right to choose a life partner is protected by Art 21; freedom of conscience and religion protected; neither the State nor patriarchal authority can override an adult's lawful choice.

Related

  • Lata Singh (2006): adults may marry outside caste or religion
  • Shakti Vahini (2018): measures against honour crimes, protect couples

Abortion AND Reproductive Autonomy

Debate

  • Woman's right over her body and reproductive agency vs State duty to protect life including foetal interests
  • When does life begin (biology, morality, religion)

Cases

  • Suchita Srivastava (2009): reproductive choice is part of personal liberty
  • X v. Principal Secretary, Health (2022): unmarried women cannot be excluded from abortion access on marital status
MTP Act 1971 (amended 2021)Rule
up to 20 weekson prescribed grounds, one registered medical practitioner
20 to 24 weeksspecified categories of women, two practitioners
beyond 24 weeksMedical Board for substantial foetal abnormalities, other recognised cases
confidentialityidentity and privacy protected
Value addition

The law regulates when abortion is permitted; it is not an unrestricted right at any stage. Issues: autonomy, safe treatment, foetal interests, unequal rural access.

Lgbtq+ Rights (ARTS 14, 15, 16, 19, 21)

Transgender rights

  • NALSA (2014):
    • Transgender persons are the Third Gender
    • Equality (14), non-discrimination (15, 16), expression, self-identification, dignity (19, 21)
    • Treat as socially and educationally backward with reservation
    • Referred to the Yogyakarta Principles (2006)
  • Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019:
    • Bars discrimination in education, jobs, health, services
    • Identity certificate via District Magistrate
    • Criticised for weak self-identification and low penalties
  • Amendment Act, 2026 (handout; verify):
    • Narrower definition, medical certification, stronger penalty for forced assumption of transgender identity
    • Raises self-identification and NALSA concerns
  1. 2009
    Naz Foundation (Delhi HC): read down s.377 for consensual adult acts
  2. 2013
    Suresh Kumar Koushal: SC reversed it
  3. 2018
    Navtej Singh Johar: SC decriminalised consensual same-sex acts (violated Arts 14, 15, 19, 21); privacy, dignity, constitutional morality over majoritarian morality
  4. 1 Jul 2024
    BNS does not retain s.377: worry about protection against non-consensual acts

Marriage

  • Arun Kumar (Madras HC, 2019): "bride" in the Hindu Marriage Act includes a transgender woman
  • Supriyo v. Union of India (2023):
    • No general Fundamental Right to marry
    • Special Marriage Act not extended to same-sex couples
    • Transgender persons in heterosexual relationships can marry
    • Dignity, autonomy, equality reaffirmed
    • Wider recognition left to the legislature
Memory aid · NNS

LGBTQ+ case chain: NALSA (2014) → Navtej (2018) → Supriyo (2023). (own mnemonic)

3 · Right TO DIE · Suicide · Euthanasia · Living WILL#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 12-14. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Art 21 gives a right to die with dignity in recognised end-of-life situations, not a right to end life at will (Gian Kaur). Attempted suicide is now treated as a mental-health matter. Passive euthanasia and living wills were recognised in Aruna Shanbaug and Common Cause; Harish Rana (2026) covers withdrawing nutrition.

Suicide AND Decriminalisation

Why life is different

  • We can speak or not speak, move or not move
  • But death is irreversible, so the logic does not carry over directly

Legal position

  • IPC s.309 punished an unsuccessful attempt
  • Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, s.115:
    • Presumption of severe stress
    • Care, treatment, rehabilitation
  • BNS s.226:
    • Only an attempt to compel or restrain a public servant
    • The general s.309 offence is not retained

For decriminalisation

  • compassion
  • odd to punish an attempt but not the act
  • encourages treatment
  • failure of social support
  • golden hour: fear of case delays aid
  • global trend to care

Against

  • social consequences for family and society
  • State duty to protect life
Note

Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab (1996): upheld then-s.309; right to life does not include a right to die; distinguished ending life from dignity in the natural process of dying. Law Commission: 42nd Report (1971) and 210th Report (2008) recommended repeal.

Euthanasia

TypeMeaning
Activedirect act, e.g. lethal drug, causes death
Passivewithholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment in legally recognised cases
Voluntarypatient's informed wish
Non-voluntarypatient cannot decide; legal decision process needed
Involuntaryagainst the patient's wishes

For

  • ends suffering
  • informal withdrawals already happen
  • financial burden
  • scarce ICU resources

Against

  • medical ethics (duty to preserve life)
  • abuse by vested interests
  • unexpected recovery
  • less research incentive
  • family convenience over patient interest
  1. 2011
    Aruna Shanbaug: passive euthanasia allowed under strict conditions; High Court oversight and medical opinion
  2. 2016
    Medical Treatment of Terminally Ill Patients Bill: not enacted
  3. 2018
    Common Cause: right to die with dignity part of Art 21; passive euthanasia and Advance Medical Directives (living wills) recognised; no general right to end life
  4. 2023
    Modified Common Cause guidelines: simpler procedure, revised medical boards, no routine High Court approval
  5. 2026
    Harish Rana: Clinically Assisted Nutrition and Hydration (CANH) can be medical treatment capable of withdrawal; stress on best interests, medical opinion, family consultation, safeguards (verify)
Way forward / key point

Living will: a competent adult records wishes on future life-sustaining treatment if they lose capacity. Key distinction: Art 21 protects dying with dignity in recognised end-of-life situations, not an unrestricted right to end life.

4 · Speedy Trial · Legal AID · Undertrials · Custodial Violence · D. K. BASU#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 14-15. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Hussainara Khatoon made speedy trial and free legal aid part of Art 21. About three-quarters of prisoners are undertrials; BNSS s.479 gives early release. Sunil Batra: prisoners keep their rights. D. K. Basu laid down nine arrest safeguards against custodial violence.

Speedy Trial AND Legal AID

Note

Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979): undertrials jailed for years without trial. Speedy trial is part of Art 21; prolonged detention without trial violates liberty; State must give free legal aid to indigent accused; led to release of many undertrials; the process must not become the punishment.

Undertrial prisoners

  • Accused kept in jail during investigation or trial, not convicted
  • About three-fourths of India's prison population
  • reasons:
    • Overworked police
    • "bail is the rule, jail the exception" ignored
    • Judicial pendency
    • Weak prosecution
    • Poverty and poor legal aid
  • BNSS s.479:
    • Release after half of the maximum sentence
    • One-third for eligible first-time offenders, with exceptions
  • Bhim Singh (2014): SC directed effective use of safeguards against long undertrial detention
Memory aid · OBJWP

Undertrial causes: Overworked police · Bail ignored · Judicial pendency · Weak prosecution · Poverty. (own mnemonic)

Note

Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration (1978): prisoners keep their Fundamental Rights; no arbitrary or inhuman treatment, torture or cruel punishment. Imprisonment limits liberty by law; it does not end dignity.

Custodial Violence

Meaning and reasons

  • Physical, mental or sexual abuse of persons in custody
  • Not every custodial death is custodial violence
  • reasons:
    • Overworked police
    • Media and public pressure for quick results (forced confessions)
    • Poor investigation tools
    • Weak training
    • Weak accountability

Safeguards

  • Art 20(3)
  • Art 21
  • Art 22
  • BNSS s.53: medical examination of arrestee to record injuries
  • BNSS s.196: magisterial inquiry into custodial death, disappearance or rape
Note

D. K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997): guidelines against arbitrary arrest and custodial violence.

#D. K. Basu guideline
1police identification and name tags
2arrest memo: date, time, witnessed and countersigned
3inform a relative, friend or nominated person
4police diary entry of arrest and person informed
5injury inspection memo with a copy
6medical examination at intervals
7documents to Magistrate
8arrestee may meet a lawyer during interrogation (not throughout)
9police control room information
