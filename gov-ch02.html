1 · The Problem of Corruption#

Handout only · M2 p.3-8
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: 2016, 2019, 2014. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • TI definition; Elephant Test; CPI since 1995 (0 most corrupt, 100 least)
  • Black, white, grey; grass-eating vs meat-eating; coercive vs collusive; retail vs policy
  • Legal factors: Art 311, PCA Sec 19, CrPC Sec 197, DSPE Sec 6A (Vineet Narain 1997; struck down 2014), PCA Sec 17A (2018)
  • 2020 CPI: New Zealand, Denmark least corrupt; Somalia, South Sudan last; Bhutan 24th, USA 25th, Botswana 35th, China 78th, Pakistan 124th
  • Global Corruption Barometer; PETS (World Bank); Paulo Mauro: investment differential up to 12%

Mains hooks

  • Behaviour that harms public interest (non-performance of duty as corruption)
  • Factors behind corruption: psycho-social, economic, legal, administrative, political
  • Implications: social, economic, political, security, international
  • Why poverty is not the only cause; powerful and affluent also corrupt

Corruption is an elephant: recognised by society even when it cannot be defined, and fed by opportunity and discretion.

In simple words
  • Corruption is old: Kautilya's Arthashastra lists ways civil servants can be corrupt and how to contain it.
  • Defining it is hard: is a gift a bribe? Culture matters (UK school ties, US lobbying, Russia's Blat, China's Guanxi). TI: 'private abuse of public office'; the Elephant Test: hard to define, easy to recognise.
  • Types: black, white and grey; grass eating and meat eating; coercive and collusive; retail (petty) and policy (grand).
  • Costs: social divisions and insecurity, lower investment (Mauro: up to 12%), diverted spending, brain drain, security risks, mass unrest.
  • Causes: psycho-social (THYMOS, Squirrel Nuts Syndrome), economic (poor pay, licence-permit raj), legal (Article 311, PCA Sec 19, CrPC Sec 197), administrative (red tape), political (no will, money power).
  • Measuring it: TI's Corruption Perception Index (0 = most corrupt, 100 = least corrupt, annual since 1995), the Global Corruption Barometer and the World Bank's PETS.

Corruption is hard to define (TI: private abuse of public office; the Elephant Test), comes in many types, and harms society, economy, politics, security and foreign standing. Its causes are psycho-social, economic, legal, administrative and political, and measuring it is imperfect.

  • Corruption is as old as civilisation
  • Kautilya's Arthashastra lists ways a civil servant can be corrupt and the measures to contain it

Defining corruption: issues

  • Is a gift a bribe? Consider intent of the donor, expectation of the recipient, timing, value, the legal framework, and social acceptability.
  • Cultural differences:
    • Old school ties (UK)
    • Regulated lobbying (USA)
    • Blat (Russia, from the Soviet Union)
    • Guanxi (China)
  • Public only, or private sector too? Too wide a net dilutes focus
  • If government is incorruptible, private corruption falls. Transparency International: 'private abuse of public office'
  • The Elephant Test: hard to define, but society knows it when it sees it

  • Psycho-social:
    • Need for THYMOS (respect/recognition) and low self-worth
    • Societal shaming of government employees
    • Squirrel Nuts Syndrome (close to retirement)
    • Opportunity theory (reduce opportunities)
    • Religious context (Protestant areas less corrupt)
    • Long history
    • No popular sanction against it
    • Social evils (dowry, lavish weddings)

Measuring corruption

  • Official statistics (weak where prosecution is weak). Perception surveys: TI's Corruption Perception Index, annual since 1995, 0 = most corrupt, 100 = least
  • 2020 CPI (released 2021): New Zealand and Denmark least corrupt, Somalia and South Sudan last
  • Bhutan 24th, USA 25th, Botswana 35th, China 78th, Pakistan 124th. Critiques: based on experts and business leaders
  • Perception, not objective
  • Sources change
  • Country count varies so ranks are not comparable
  • Experiential:
    • TI's Global Corruption Barometer (not annual, regional)
    • In Asia Japan least corrupt and India most corrupt among 17 countries. Tracking: World Bank's PETS. Content analysis of media (crude)

2 · Tackling Corruption and Social Audit#

Handout only · M2 p.8-11
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: 2021, 2016*. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • Stick/carrot/admin/radical measures with country examples (Botswana, Singapore CPIB, Hong Kong ICAC, South Africa, Georgia, Peru, Romania)
  • CAG covers only 25% of auditable units yearly; procurement 30% of GDP; 2012 bill lapsed
  • International: FCPA 1977, UK Bribery Act 2010, OECD 1997, UNCAC 2003, FATF 1989
  • Social audit: Meghalaya 2017 Act; AP SSAAT; Karnataka rotation after 3 cycles; Chhattisgarh wall writing; Swaraj Abhiyan 2016; MKSS jan sunwai

Mains hooks

  • Stick vs carrot measures; why fragmented agencies fail
  • Features, significance and issues of social audit; how to strengthen
  • Success stories: AP, Jharkhand, MKSS, Bengaluru CRCs, benchmarking

Make demand-side pressure real: audits only work when information flows freely.

In simple words
  • The handout calls corruption a wicked problem: more laws signal inefficiency, and it can only be controlled, not removed.
  • Stick measures: prosecution, stings, tighter laws, asset declaration, effective single agency (Singapore CPIB, Hong Kong ICAC). Carrot: pay rise with enforcement, citizen helplines. Administrative: job rotation, auditing, public procurement law, digitisation, CCTV.
  • Radical ideas: disband departments (Georgia's Traffic Police, 2004), gender balance (Peru), monetising bribes (Romania), amnesty. International: FCPA, UK Bribery Act, OECD Convention, UNCAC, FATF.
  • Social audit: scheme details are shared with beneficiaries on public platforms (Jan Sunwai, Gram Sabha) so people hold officials accountable. It needs a sound access-to-information regime.
  • Success stories: Andhra Pradesh SSAAT (MGNREGA), Jharkhand, Rajasthan MKSS (led to RTI Act 2005), Bengaluru Citizen Report Cards. Strengthen with a national law, trained auditors and rotating teams (Karnataka).

Corruption is a wicked problem: it can be controlled, not eliminated. The toolkit spans stick, carrot, administrative, radical and international measures. Social audit is the participatory tool: public hearings that need a sound RTI regime, with AP's SSAAT as the model.

  • 'Wicked problem':
    • Despite PCA, Lokpal, CVC Act, corruption persists
    • More laws signal systemic inefficiency
    • It can only be controlled

Stick measures

  • Prosecution:
    • Higher conviction deters (Botswana)
    • India's delays and lenient punishment weaken deterrence
    • Harsh penalties (China) unsuitable without rule of law. Stings and entrapment: admissibility unclear
    • Natural stings (citizen recordings) should be admissible
    • Sweden restrictive. Tighten laws: 2018 amendments diluted PCA. Asset declaration with lifestyle monitoring. Effective agencies: India is fragmented (CVC, CBI, Lokpal, ED)
    • Singapore CPIB and Hong Kong ICAC succeed with one agency. Shaming and blacklisting (South Africa)
    • Forfeiture of pensions

Carrot measures

  • Substantial pay increase paired with enforcement
  • Citizen helplines (Delhi anti-corruption hotline) needing whistleblower and witness protection

Administrative and technical

  • Job rotation (Kautilya)
  • Stronger auditing (CAG covers 25% of auditable units yearly)
  • Procurement norms and a Public Procurement Law (procurement 30% of GDP; 2012 bill lapsed)
  • Digitisation
  • CCTV in high-risk zones

Radical approaches

  • Disband departments (Georgia's Traffic Police, 2004)
  • Gender balance (Peru)
  • Monetising bribes (Romania's conscription policy; controversial)
  • General amnesty (50% surrender of black money; debated)

International measures

  • US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (1977), UK Bribery Act (2010)
  • OECD Anti-Bribery Convention (1997)
  • UNCAC (2003)
  • FATF (1989)
  • Interpol IGEC
  • WTO inaction
  • Global financial transparency against banking havens
  • Critique of the global industry:
    • West ignored corruption until the 1990s
    • Anti-corruption became a geopolitical tool
    • NGO vested interests, data over impact
    • Cultural imperialism concerns. Alternatives: spiritual education
    • Integrity Recognition Certificates for honest officers (must be insulated from politicisation)

Social audit

  • Scheme details (financial and non-financial) shared with beneficiaries on a public platform (Jan Sunwai, public hearing, Gram Sabha), so people can hold bureaucracy and Panchayat leaders accountable. Key: a sound access-to-information regime.
  • Features:
    • Multi-perspective
    • Regular
    • Comparative (time and space)
    • Strengthens the demand side and indirectly targets supply-side weakness
  • Significance:
    • Participation
    • Benefits reach the target
    • Social capital
    • Identifies civil servants' HR and training needs
    • Highlights development concerns
    • Transparency and anti-corruption
    • Better future programmes
  • Issues:
    • Not in mainstream policy discourse
    • Statutory in very few programmes (NREGA is one)
    • Lack of trained auditors
    • Opposition from vested interests. Symptom-cause point: audit across cause-effect layers, not surface symptoms alone (e.g. plague awareness drive without fixing sanitation)

3 · CBI and CVC#

Handout only · M2 p.11-15
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: 2021*. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • CBI: SPE 1941; DSPE Act 1946; name 1963; four divisions; conviction rate 67% vs 30-40%; no suo motu jurisdiction
  • Vineet Narain 1997: CVC supervises CBI; Director's min. tenure 2 years; committee (PM, LoP, CJI); single directive struck down; DoPT policy only
  • CVC: 1964 executive; statutory 2003; CVC + max 2 VCs; term 4 years or 65; pay = UPSC Chair/members
  • PJ Thomas 2011: consensus not majority in selection committee
  • CVC Chair heads committee for ED Director and CBI officers SP and above

Mains hooks

  • Issues in CBI functioning and Vineet Narain directions
  • CVC powers, issues and suggestions
  • Institutional framework against corruption

Autonomy of anti-graft agencies is the weak link: independence of selection, tenure, prosecution and staff.

In simple words
  • The CBI began as the Special Police Establishment (1941), got statutory backing from the DSPE Act 1946 and was named CBI in 1963. Issues: political control ('caged parrot'), vacancies, sanction delays, no suo motu jurisdiction.
  • Vineet Narain (1997): the Supreme Court gave the CVC superintendence of CBI in PCA cases, fixed the Director's tenure, set a selection committee (PM, LoP, CJI) and struck down the Single Directive.
  • The CVC was set up in 1964 on the Santhanam Committee and made statutory in 2003: one Central Vigilance Commissioner and up to two Vigilance Commissioners, 4-year term, retirement at 65, no reappointment.
  • P.J. Thomas (2011): selection must follow the consensus of the committee, not a majority.
  • Fix list: independent investigation and prosecution powers, statutory sanction timelines, more staff, binding recommendations and a wider selection panel.

CBI and CVC in date order
Vineet Narain (1997) and CVC powers

The CBI (SPE 1941, DSPE Act 1946, named 1963) is politicised ('caged parrot') despite Vineet Narain (1997). The CVC (Santhanam, 1964; statutory 2003) superintends CBI on PCA cases but depends on it and cannot prosecute.

Legal and institutional framework

  • Sec 203 BNS (Sec 169 IPC):
    • Civil servant illegally acquiring property
    • Sec 316 BNS (Sec 409 IPC): criminal breach of trust by a public servant (as printed). DSPE Act 1946 (CBI)
    • RPA 1951 Sec 123
    • PCA 1988
    • Benami Properties Act 1988
    • PMLA 2003
    • CVC Act
    • RTI 2005
    • Lokayukta Acts
    • Lokpal Act 2013
    • Whistleblower Act 2014
    • Time-bound service delivery laws
  • Institutions: CBI, CVC, state ACBs and vigilance wings, CAG, Lokayuktas, Lokpal, ED (PMLA and FEMA), banking ombudsman, consumer courts, three-tier judiciary.

Central Bureau of Investigation

  • Special Police Establishment 1941
  • Statutory backing by DSPE Act 1946
  • Named CBI in 1963. Divisions: Anti-Corruption
  • Special Crimes
  • Economic Offences
  • Banking and Security Frauds
  • Issues:
    • Politicisation ('caged parrot', a former SC judge)
    • About one-third posts vacant
    • Conviction rate 67% vs 30-40% for other agencies, but low in high-profile cases
    • Sanction delays (Sec 19 PCA, Sec 197 CrPC)
    • No suo motu jurisdiction (cases transferred to it)

Vineet Narain case, 1997

  • CVC to superintend CBI on PCA cases
  • Minimum guaranteed tenure for the Director (2 years under DSPE Act; later extendable up to 5 in yearly steps by ordinance)
  • Director chosen by a committee: PM, Leader of Opposition (or largest opposition party leader), CJI
  • Single Directive struck down
  • DoPT sets only overall policy, not day-to-day investigations
  • Director's transfer by the same committee

Central Vigilance Commission

CBI and CVC compared
  • Set up in 1964 as an executive body on the K. Santhanam Committee (1962)
  • Statutory in 2003 under the CVC Act. Apex vigilance institution free of executive control
  • Advises on planning, reviewing, reforming vigilance. Central Vigilance Commissioner + not more than two Vigilance Commissioners.
  • Selection:
    • From All India or Central Services, and from CPSU officers (not more than two from the two categories, so at least one from each). Committee: PM, Home Minister, Leader of Opposition (government has the final say). P.J. Thomas case (2011): government majority made the LoP's role pointless
    • Selection must follow consensus of all members, not majority (only for this appointment)
  • Term:
    • 4 years for CVC and VCs
    • Retirement age 65
    • No reappointment
    • A VC can become CVC but total stay not above 4 years
    • Barred from UT administrator, diplomatic and other presidential-warrant posts
    • No office of profit. Pay: CVC equals UPSC Chairperson, VCs equal UPSC members
    • Charged to the Consolidated Fund
    • Cannot be altered to disadvantage. Removal: proved misbehaviour or incapacity on SC reference
    • Plus insolvency, moral turpitude, private employment, infirmity, prejudicial financial interest
  • Functions and powers:
    • Superintend CBI on corruption cases (Vineet Narain) and give directions
    • Inquire on Central Government reference or on complaints against Group A, All India Service officers and equivalent CPSU officials
    • Review DSPE investigations and pending sanction applications
    • Tender advice
    • Superintend vigilance in ministries
    • Powers of a civil court
    • Mandatory consultation on vigilance and disciplinary rules
    • CVC chairs the committee recommending the ED Director (and Deputy Director and above)
    • Chairs the committee recommending CBI officers of SP level and above (not Director). Annual report to the President, laid before Parliament
  • Issues:
    • Dependence on a politicised CBI
    • No independent prosecution powers and sanction delays
    • Non-cooperative government
    • Poor engagement with civil society
  • Suggestions:
    • Independent investigative and prosecution powers (2nd ARC: functional autonomy)
    • 3-6 month statutory timeline for sanction (TI India: over 70% of sanctions pending beyond 6 months in 2023)
    • Binding oversight over vigilance administration
    • Manpower (less than 50% of sanctioned staff, over 1,500 organisations per CVC 2023 report)
    • Civil society participation (South Korea ACRC)
    • Binding recommendations
    • Wider selection panel (judiciary or experts)

4 · Lokpal in India#

Handout only · M2 p.15-19
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: 2013. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • 9-member body; 50% judicial; 50% affirmative action; age 45+; 5 years or 70; salary CJI/SC judge
  • Selection committee: PM, Speaker, LoP, CJI/nominee, eminent jurist; search committee of 7
  • PM covered partially (not public order, security, international relations, atomic energy, space; full bench two-thirds)
  • Removal reference signed by 100 MPs; 60 days preliminary inquiry; 7-year limitation
  • Maharashtra first Lokayukta (1971); Odisha 1992, Haryana 1999 abolished

Mains hooks

  • Should the PM be covered? 2nd ARC arguments and counter-arguments
  • Issues with Lokpal; functioning since 2019
  • Lokpal is not a panacea

No single body can cure a hydra: Lokpal works only inside a multi-pronged strategy.

In simple words
  • The ombudsman idea: Sweden 1809, UK 1967. In India the first Bill was introduced in 1968; the present Lokpal Bill was passed in 2013 (becoming the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013); the Lokpal was constituted in 2019 with Justice P.C. Ghose as Chairperson. Maharashtra had the first Lokayukta (1971).
  • State Lokayuktas often became 'paper tigers': recommendatory, misused, and some were abolished (Odisha 1992, Haryana 1999).
  • The Lokpal has nine members including the Chairperson; half judicial, half non-judicial; 50% reserved. Selection committee: PM, Lok Sabha Speaker, Leader of Opposition, CJI or nominee and an eminent jurist.
  • It covers PM (partly), ministers, MPs and Groups A-D, plus bodies funded above Rs 10 lakh. A preliminary inquiry runs within 60 days; a 7-year limit applies to complaints.
  • The PM is covered only partly: no inquiry into decisions on public order, security, international relations, atomic energy and space, and a full-bench two-thirds majority is needed.
  • The record is thin: complaints fell from 1,427 (2019-20) to 110 (2020-21) to 12 till August 2021-22.
TermIn plain words
OmbudsmanThe office for complaints against public servants; Sweden appointed the first in 1809.
LokayuktaThe state-level counterpart; Maharashtra had the first (1971).
Lokpal at a glance

The Ombudsman began in Sweden (1809). India's First ARC proposed a two-tier model; the bill was first introduced in 1968, passed in 2013, and the Lokpal constituted in 2019. States' Lokayuktas show the weaknesses: no standard design, 'paper tigers', abolition by governments.

  • Ombudsman:
    • Sweden 1809
    • UK Parliamentary Commissioner 1967
    • USA in some states (Hawaii, Oregon, Nebraska), not federal. First ARC: two-tier: Lokpal for Secretary-rank and above at Union and State levels
    • Union Lokayukta for lower officials
    • State Lokayuktas in states
  • India:
    • First Bill introduced 1968
    • The present Lokpal Bill passed in 2013, becoming the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013
    • Lokpal constituted 2019 with Justice P.C. Ghose as Chairperson of the nine-member body. Maharashtra first state Lokayukta (1971)

State experience

  • Flooded with complaints
  • Poor effectiveness
  • Inter-state differences (qualifications, tenure, powers, suo motu, no separate agency)
  • Survival depends on state government (Odisha abolished it by ordinance in 1992; Haryana in 1999)
  • Recommendatory 'paper tigers'
  • Corruption within
  • Misused against political opponents

Lokpal Act 2013

  • Composition: nine members including the Chairperson. Chairperson: a present or former CJI or SC judge, or an eminent person with 25 years' experience in vigilance, anti-corruption, law, finance, administration, management. Eight members: half judicial (SC judge or High Court Chief Justice), half non-judicial (eminent, 25 years). 50% affirmative action: SC, ST, OBC, minorities, women.
  • Conditions:
    • Not an MP, MLA, MLC
    • No local body membership
    • Minimum age 45
    • Not removed or dismissed from government service
    • No office of profit, business or profession
    • No party affiliation
  • Appointment by the President on a committee:
    • PM (Chair), Lok Sabha Speaker, Leader of Opposition (LS) (silent on a missing LoP), CJI or nominated SC judge, eminent jurist chosen by the four. A search committee of 7 shortlists, with 50% reservation
    • The selection committee may go beyond its names

Should the Prime Minister be covered?

  • 2nd ARC opposed:
    • Would cripple administration during investigation
    • Foreign powers might exploit it
    • PM already covered by the PCA 1988. Counter: CBI works under DoPT, hence the PM, which would hamper probes
    • PM should lead by example (some Lokayuktas cover Chief Ministers)
    • Article 361 exempts only President and Governors
  • Act covers PM partially:
    • No inquiry into PM decisions on public order, internal and external security, international relations, atomic energy, space
    • The full bench by two-thirds majority must approve initiating an inquiry or investigation

Jurisdiction and functions

  • PM, Union Ministers, MPs, Group A-D officials, and bodies funded by government above Rs 10 lakh a year. Any individual may complain for PCA offences. Preliminary inquiry via the Inquiry Wing, CVC or agencies like CBI
  • Explanation sought from the public servant
  • Complaints against Groups C and D referred to the CVC
  • Preliminary inquiry within 60 days
  • After the report and hearing:
    • Full investigation, departmental proceedings, close the case, or proceed against a false complainant. A bench of at least 3 members decides on sanction. Lokpal replaces the appointing authority in sanctions under CrPC Sec 218 and PCA Sec 19. Powers of search, seizure, attachment, and civil court powers
    • Superintendence over CBI for cases it refers
  • Removal:
    • On grounds of misbehaviour, through a reference to the Supreme Court, but only if the reference is signed by at least 100 MPs
    • On other grounds the President can dismiss on the advice of the Council of Ministers (insolvency, private employment, infirmity of body or mind, prejudicial financial interest). Annual asset declarations
    • Annual report with a memorandum explaining rejected recommendations
    • 7-year limitation for complaints

  • Too wide jurisdiction
  • Convoluted appointment
  • PM only partly covered
  • 7-year limit
  • Scope may intimidate NGOs
  • Delays in appointment
  • Functioning since 2019:
    • Allegations of emasculation by legislation
    • Delayed complaint-form rules, asset disclosure rules, preliminary inquiry and investigation rules
    • Wings not set up by 2020
    • By Sept 2019 disposed of 1,000 of 1,065 complaints
    • Complaints fell from 1,427 (2019-20) to 110 (2020-21) to 12 till August 2021-22
  • 2nd ARC:
    • Give Lokpal and Lokayuktas constitutional status
    • Rashtriya Lok Ayukta for Union Ministers, MPs and State Chief Ministers
    • State Lokayukta for state ministers and legislators
    • CVC for civil servants
    • A Local Bodies Ombudsman
  • Not a panacea:
    • Hydra-headed corruption needs multi-pronged strategy
    • Huge bureaucracy
    • Petty complaints need departmental handling
    • Inadequate judicial process
    • Lacking citizen empowerment and willingness to complain

5 · Whistleblower Protection and the PCA 1988#

Handout only · M2 p.19-20
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: 2015, 2022. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • Competent authority: PM (Union Ministers), CM (state ministers), Presiding Officer (legislators), CVC (civil servants)
  • No anonymous complaints; frivolous complaint penalised with 2-year jail
  • 2015 amendment Bill introduced, then lapsed (never became law): OSA to override the Act
  • SEBI tipping mechanism: reward up to Rs 1 crore
  • PCA 2018: bribe-giving an offence; 7-day complaint defence; jail 3 to 7 years; trial in 2 years, max 4; Sec 17A extends to retired servants

Mains hooks

  • Challenges with the Whistleblower Act and measures to strengthen it
  • 2nd ARC and Law Commission framework
  • Why the 2018 PCA amendment is both strengthening and diluting

A whistleblower law without anonymity protects the disclosure but not the discloser.

In simple words
  • The Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014 lets anyone disclose corruption or wilful misuse of power by a public body. Disclosures go to the competent authority (PM for Union Ministers, CM for state ministers, Presiding Officer for legislators, CVC for civil servants). Anonymous complaints are not entertained; frivolous ones can earn 2 years' jail.
  • Challenges: no anonymity (Satyendra Dubey), weak implementation and awareness, little protection from victimisation. Needs: wider coverage including the private sector; SEBI's tipping reward up to Rs 1 crore.
  • The Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 covers every public servant: government staff, PSU employees, local body functionaries, judges, PSC members, university employees, MPs and MLAs.
  • The 2018 Amendment: giving a bribe is an offence (with a 7-day complaint exemption in coercive cases); Sec 17A extends protection to retired servants; jail raised from 3 to 7 years; trial in 2 years (up to 4); commercial organisations can be prosecuted.
TermIn plain words
PIDPIThe short name the handout gives for the 2014 Whistle Blowers Protection Act.
Sec 17APCA 2018: permission needed for preliminary inquiry, except when caught red-handed.
Remember it as

PCA 2018 cuts both ways: it penalises the bribe-giver and shields the officer.

Whistleblower protection map

The 2014 Act has no anonymity, narrow safeguards and a lapsed 2015 amendment bill that would have let the Official Secrets Act override it. The PCA 1988 covers all public servants; the 2018 amendment criminalised bribe-giving, raised penalties and added Sec 17A.

Whistleblower Protection Act, 2014 (PIDPI)

  • Anyone can be a whistleblower regarding any public body. Disclosure to the competent authority: PM for Union Ministers, Chief Minister for state ministers, Presiding Officer for legislators, CVC for civil servants. No anonymous complaints. CVC can issue directions for protection. Frivolous complaints: 2-year jail.
Where a whistleblower complaint goes
  • 2015 amendment Bill (introduced, then lapsed; never became law): proposed that the Official Secrets Act 1923 would override the Act.

Challenges

  • No anonymity (murder of Satyendra Dubey, who reported corruption in the NHAI)
  • Weak organisational implementation and awareness
  • Inadequate protection against victimisation
  • Yet complaints rise (2018: over 3,500 complaints to NSE-listed companies)

Measures needed

  • 2nd ARC:
    • Stronger law covering public and private sectors. Law Commission framework: confidentiality and anonymity
    • Protection against victimisation and bodily harm
    • Coverage of corporate whistleblowers. SEBI's tipping mechanism: rewards up to Rs 1 crore for actionable information on insider trading, with confidentiality

Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988

  • Covers all public servants:
    • Anyone in government pay or service
    • PSU employees
    • Local body functionaries
    • Judges
    • Office-bearers of government-funded cooperatives
    • PSC members
    • Employees of central and state universities
    • Deemed university employees (SC, 2020)
    • Anyone performing a public duty by virtue of office, including MPs and MLAs

PCA Amendment Act, 2018

  • Giving a bribe is explicitly an offence
  • In coercive corruption, a complaint within 7 days frees the giver from guilt. Sec 17A extended to retired civil servants. Original Sec 13(1)(d) (decision benefiting a commercial organisation against public interest) diluted. Jail from 3 to 7 years. Trial in 2 years, up to 4. Provisions for prosecuting commercial organisations and for attachment or confiscation of property
PCA 2018: both sides of a bribe

Flow charts#

The sanction-to-prosecute chain (Sections 15, 17)

Single Directive: sanction needed even for preliminary inquiry against Joint Secretary and above
1997 Vineet Narain: Supreme Court strikes the Single Directive
DSPE Act amended: Single Directive made law as Sec 6A
2014: Sec 6A struck down (equality)
2018: PCA Sec 17A: permission for preliminary inquiry for all public servants, except if caught red-handed (extends to retired servants)
Meanwhile: PCA Sec 19 and CrPC Sec 197 still need sanction to prosecute; Lokpal replaces the sanctioning authority in its cases

A complaint before the Lokpal (Section 18)

Any individual files a complaint for a PCA 1988 offence
Lokpal orders a preliminary inquiry (Inquiry Wing, CVC or CBI) and seeks the public servant's explanation
Complaints against Group C and D officials go to the CVC
Preliminary inquiry finishes within 60 days
Lokpal bench (min 3) hears the public servant, then: full investigation, departmental proceedings, close the case, or act against a false complainant
If PM is the accused: full bench, two-thirds majority; not public order, security, international relations, atomic energy, space
Prosecution wing and court; time limit for the complaint: 7 years

2022 · 10 marks · Whistle blower, who reports corruption and illegal activities, wrongdoing and misconduct to the concerned auth…

Question. Whistle blower, who reports corruption and illegal activities, wrongdoing and misconduct to the concerned authorities, runs the risk of being exposed to grave danger, physical harm and victimization by the vested interests, accused persons and his team. What policy measures would you suggest to strengthen protection mechanism to safeguard the whistle blower?

The 2014 Act mandates disclosure of identity and offers limited protection; Satyendra Dubey's murder shows the risk.

  • Confidentiality and anonymity (Law Commission framework)
  • Legal protection against victimisation and administrative safeguards against harassment and bodily harm
  • Extend coverage to corporate whistleblowers; 2nd ARC: stronger law for public and private sectors
  • Organisational guidelines and awareness; SEBI-style rewards up to Rs 1 crore; CVC power to direct protection

Close. Protect the person, not just the disclosure.

However, the power of the States to withhold consent to the CBI is not absolute. Explain with special reference to the federal character of India.

  • Vineet Narain 1997: CVC superintends CBI on PCA cases; DoPT sets only policy
  • Issues: politicised 'caged parrot', one-third vacancies
  • Federal angle: not in the modules, so add it separately

Close. State consent protects federalism but cannot be used to block corruption probes. (Partial fit: no content on state consent or federal case law.)

2021 · 10 marks · An independent and empowered social audit mechanism is an absolute must in every sphere of public service, inc…

Question. An independent and empowered social audit mechanism is an absolute must in every sphere of public service, including the judiciary, to ensure performance, accountability and ethical conduct. Elaborate.

Social audit shares scheme details with beneficiaries at public hearings so the people can find loopholes.

  • Independence: AP's SSAAT, Karnataka's rotation of audit teams after three cycles, Meghalaya 2017 Act as model
  • Empowerment: statutory in very few programmes (NREGA); needs trained auditors, Gram Sabha awareness, digital portals
  • Results: crores recovered in AP; Jharkhand SAU exposed ghost beneficiaries; SC in Swaraj Abhiyan (2016)
  • Judiciary: RTI coverage held in 2019, but the notes give no audit model for it

Close. Audit works when information is open and the auditor is independent of the audited.

2019 · 10 (150 words) marks · "Non-performance of duty by a public servant is a form of corruption" Do you agree with this view? Justify you…

Question. "Non-performance of duty by a public servant is a form of corruption" Do you agree with this view? Justify your answer.

Agree, in the wider sense: corruption includes any behaviour that harms public interest.

  • Handout list: lethargy, dereliction or negligence of duty, repeated carelessness, non-punctuality, favouritism and nepotism
  • TI's definition ('private abuse of public office') and the Elephant Test: society recognises it
  • Non-performance harms citizens like speed money does (white corruption tolerated)
  • Caveat: stretching the definition too wide dilutes focus

2016 · 12.5 marks · In the integrity index of Transparency International, India stands very low. Discuss briefly the legal, politi…

Question. In the integrity index of Transparency International, India stands very low. Discuss briefly the legal, political, economic, social and cultural factors that have caused the decline of public morality in India.

TI's index is perception-based, but the handout's factor list explains a low rating.

  • Legal: Art 311 protection, Sec 19 PCA and Sec 197 CrPC sanctions, delayed prosecution, no effective whistleblower law, weak autonomy of CBI and CVC
  • Political: no will on Lokpal, criminalisation of politics, money power, weak party-funding disclosure, TINA
  • Economic: licence-permit-quota to clearance raj, consumerism, poor pay, MNC willingness to bribe
  • Social: shaming of government staff, no popular sanction, social evils like dowry, thymos (low self-worth)
  • Cultural: long history of corruption, tolerance, culture-specific norms

Close. Public morality will recover only when opportunity, impunity and tolerance fall together.

2015 · 12.5 marks · If amendment bill to the Whistleblowers Act, 2011 tabled in the Parliament is passed, there may be no one left…

Question. If amendment bill to the Whistleblowers Act, 2011 tabled in the Parliament is passed, there may be no one left to protect. Critically evaluate.

The 2015 amendment bill proposed that the Official Secrets Act 1923 would override the Act; it lapsed.

  • Protection already weak: no anonymity (Satyendra Dubey murder), limited safeguards against victimisation
  • Competent authority route: PM, CM, Presiding Officer, CVC
  • Frivolous complaints face 2-year jail
  • Fixes: Law Commission framework (confidentiality, anonymity, protection from bodily harm, corporate whistleblowers); SEBI tipping up to Rs 1 crore

Close. A whistleblower law that yields to the Official Secrets Act protects secrecy, not the whistleblower.

2014 · 10 marks · It is often said that poverty leads to corruption. However, there is no dearth of instances where affluent and…

Question. It is often said that poverty leads to corruption. However, there is no dearth of instances where affluent and powerful people indulge in corruption in a big way. What are the basic causes of corruption among people? Support your answer with examples.

Poor pay is blamed, but the handout says raising salaries alone may not dent corruption.

  • Psycho-social: thymos (low self-worth), consumerism, Squirrel Nuts Syndrome, opportunity theory
  • Policy (grand) corruption: crony capitalism and state capture by business houses; MNCs able and willing to bribe
  • Systemic: clearance raj, red tape, middlemen, weak sanctions (Art 311, Sec 19)
  • Examples in the handout: Mundhra scandal, pork-barreling, German car insurance case

Close. Opportunity and impunity, not poverty, are the common thread.

2013 · 10 marks · 'A national Lokpal, however strong it may be, cannot resolve the problems of immorality in public affairs'. Di…

Question. 'A national Lokpal, however strong it may be, cannot resolve the problems of immorality in public affairs'. Discuss.

The handout calls Lokpal 'not a panacea': corruption is a hydra-headed monster.

  • Needs a multi-pronged strategy; huge bureaucracy; petty complaints need departmental handling
  • Judicial process inadequate; no citizen empowerment or willingness to complain
  • Causes of immorality are psycho-social, economic, legal, administrative and political (Sec 15)
  • What Lokpal adds: inquiry and prosecution wings, search, seizure, attachment, sanction replaced

Close. Institutions police conduct; immorality is cured by opportunity reduction, transparency and ethics.

Practice · Should the Prime Minister be brought under the Lokpal? Examine in light of the Lokpal Act 2013.

The Act covers the PM partially.

  • 2nd ARC against: cripples administration, foreign exploitation, PCA already applies
  • For: CBI works under DoPT; lead by example; Art 361 exempts only President and Governors
  • Act: no inquiry on public order, security, international relations, atomic energy, space; full-bench two-thirds majority

Close. Partial coverage balances accountability against continuity, but leaves the main objection unanswered.

Practice · How can the success of Andhra Pradesh's social audit model be scaled up nationally?

AP's SSAAT is an autonomous society that trained village resource persons.

  • Result: crores of wage misappropriation recovered; World Bank global best practice
  • Scale: national law on Meghalaya 2017 lines; independent audit units; PRI capacity
  • Add: trained auditors, rotation of audit teams (Karnataka), wall writing and grievance link (Chhattisgarh), digital portals

Close. Independence of auditors and access to information decide whether audits scale.
