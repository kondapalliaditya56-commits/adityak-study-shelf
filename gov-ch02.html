1 · The Problem of Corruption#

Handout only · M2 p.3-8
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: 2016, 2019, 2014. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • TI definition; Elephant Test; CPI since 1995 (0 most corrupt, 100 least)
  • Black, white, grey; grass-eating vs meat-eating; coercive vs collusive; retail vs policy
  • Legal factors: Art 311, PCA Sec 19, CrPC Sec 197, DSPE Sec 6A (Vineet Narain 1997; struck down 2014), PCA Sec 17A (2018)
  • 2020 CPI: New Zealand, Denmark least corrupt; Somalia, South Sudan last; Bhutan 24th, USA 25th, Botswana 35th, China 78th, Pakistan 124th
  • Global Corruption Barometer; PETS (World Bank); Paulo Mauro: investment differential up to 12%

Mains hooks

  • Behaviour that harms public interest (non-performance of duty as corruption)
  • Factors behind corruption: psycho-social, economic, legal, administrative, political
  • Implications: social, economic, political, security, international
  • Why poverty is not the only cause; powerful and affluent also corrupt

Corruption is an elephant: recognised by society even when it cannot be defined, and fed by opportunity and discretion.

In simple words
  • Corruption is old: Kautilya's Arthashastra lists ways civil servants can be corrupt and how to contain it.
  • Defining it is hard: is a gift a bribe? Culture matters (UK school ties, US lobbying, Russia's Blat, China's Guanxi). TI: 'private abuse of public office'; the Elephant Test: hard to define, easy to recognise.
  • Types: black, white and grey; grass eating and meat eating; coercive and collusive; retail (petty) and policy (grand).
  • Costs: social divisions and insecurity, lower investment (Mauro: up to 12%), diverted spending, brain drain, security risks, mass unrest.
  • Causes: psycho-social (THYMOS, Squirrel Nuts Syndrome), economic (poor pay, licence-permit raj), legal (Article 311, PCA Sec 19, CrPC Sec 197), administrative (red tape), political (no will, money power).
  • Measuring it: TI's Corruption Perception Index (0 = most corrupt, 100 = least corrupt, annual since 1995), the Global Corruption Barometer and the World Bank's PETS.

Corruption is hard to define (TI: private abuse of public office; the Elephant Test), comes in many types, and harms society, economy, politics, security and foreign standing. Its causes are psycho-social, economic, legal, administrative and political, and measuring it is imperfect.

Value addition

As old as civilisation: Kautilya's Arthashastra lists many ways a civil servant can be corrupt and specific measures to contain it. Present everywhere, only in varying degree; almost no human activity is untouched; it has contributed to the decline of empires and to reforms in religion. TI definition: private abuse of public office. Elephant Test: corruption may be hard to define or describe, but when a civil servant does something unethical, society knows it: easier to point out than to define.

Defining issues

  • Is a gift a bribe? consider the donor's intent, the recipient's expectation, timing, value, the country's legal framework, and social acceptability
  • Culture varies:
    • Old school ties (UK: used to leverage favours, culturally accepted)
    • Lobbying and networking (USA: regulated by law, legal, not seen as corrupt)
    • Blat (Russia: Soviet-era system of favours and reciprocal favours, culturally accepted)
    • Guanxi (China: a favour done must be returned)
  • Public bodies only, or private sector too? Many say both, but casting the net too wide dilutes focus and makes corruption unmanageable
  • Counter-view: if government is incorruptible, private-sector corruption will fall naturally

Corruption in the wider sense

  • Goes beyond giving and taking bribes
  • It includes any behaviour that harms public interest: lethargy in assigned duties
  • dereliction or negligence of duty
  • repeated carelessness
  • non-punctuality
  • subjective interpretation of public interest
  • favouritism and nepotism
  • communalism, casteism, lynching, regional bias.

Economic

  • poor salary at upper levels is blamed, but raising pay alone may not dent corruption much
  • consumerism feeds low self-esteem and so corruption
  • licence-permit-quota raj → clearance raj:
    • So many clearances and NOCs before starting a venture
    • Red tape gives incentive to be corrupt
  • willingness and ability: big MNCs have the clout to bribe their way to favourable policy
  • business houses do not cooperate: if all refused to pay bribes corruption would fall sharply, but they try to outbid and outdo each other for government favours

Legal

  • excessive legal protection for public servants against prosecution for corruption
  • Art 311:
    • Reasonable opportunity before action, so it is very hard to dismiss corrupt servants
    • A source of excessive protection
  • PCA 1988, Sec 19: sanction of the appointing authority before prosecution
  • CrPC Sec 197 (as in the handout): permission of the appointing authority to prosecute for any offence other than corruption linked to official duty. Sec 19 and Sec 197 were meant to protect honest servants from spurious allegations but are misused to protect the corrupt
  • delay in prosecution (inadequate judicial process)
  • no effective whistleblower law to protect honest officials
  • inadequate autonomy of CBI and CVC, which have become politicised

THE Sanction Chain

DSPE Act 1946: preliminary inquiry first, then full investigation; prior sanction needed for investigation, not for preliminary inquiry. Single Directive: for Joint Secretary rank and above, sanction needed for both
Vineet Narain 1997: apex court struck the Single Directive down
DSPE Act amended: made law as Sec 6A
2014: SC struck down Sec 6A: violated equality (special protection to JS and above)
PCA amended 2018, Sec 17A: permission needed for preliminary inquiry into all public servants except those caught red-handed
Sanction chain in four steps

Administrative + Political

  • administrative:
    • Procedural complexity breeds middlemen who carry the corrupt money
    • Red tape
    • Weak internal grievance machinery
    • Peer pressure and indoctrination make honest officials afraid to pursue anti-graft action
    • Bureaucratic resistance to reform
    • Politicised management through transfers and plum postings
    • General culture of secrecy
  • political:
    • No political will (repeated attempts to pass the Lokpal Bill, delay in appointing Lokpal)
    • Criminalisation of politics (criminals inside the system protect themselves)
    • System does not encourage neutrality: officials take sides when a dispensation is in power
    • Lack of voter maturity (ousted parties re-elected; TINA: There Is No Alternative)
    • Money power in elections
    • Weak disclosure norms on functioning and funding of parties

Measuring Corruption

Key point

Official statistics: usable, but if prosecution and investigation are ineffective, they give an incomplete picture. Perceptual and attitudinal surveys: especially by international NGOs.

Memory aid · CPI

CPI by Transparency International · annual since 1995 · 0 = most corrupt, 100 = least corrupt. 2020 CPI (released 2021): New Zealand and Denmark least corrupt; Somalia, South Sudan last; South Asia: Bhutan 24th; USA 25th, Botswana 35th, China 78th, Pakistan 124th (as printed).

CPI critique

  • based on questionnaires to country experts and business leaders, not the common man
  • scores rest on perception, not objective assessment
  • sources change year to year
  • number of countries surveyed varies, so comparing ranks over years is inappropriate.

Other tools

  • Experiential surveys: TI's Global Corruption Barometer (actual experience; not annual, regional; in Asia, Japan least corrupt and India most corrupt among 17 countries)
  • Tracking surveys: World Bank PETS (tracks funds and flows at different levels when donor funds are spent)
  • content analysis of media (crude but interesting).

2 · Tackling Corruption and Social Audit#

Handout only · M2 p.8-11
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: 2021, 2016*. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • Stick/carrot/admin/radical measures with country examples (Botswana, Singapore CPIB, Hong Kong ICAC, South Africa, Georgia, Peru, Romania)
  • CAG covers only 25% of auditable units yearly; procurement 30% of GDP; 2012 bill lapsed
  • International: FCPA 1977, UK Bribery Act 2010, OECD 1997, UNCAC 2003, FATF 1989
  • Social audit: Meghalaya 2017 Act; AP SSAAT; Karnataka rotation after 3 cycles; Chhattisgarh wall writing; Swaraj Abhiyan 2016; MKSS jan sunwai

Mains hooks

  • Stick vs carrot measures; why fragmented agencies fail
  • Features, significance and issues of social audit; how to strengthen
  • Success stories: AP, Jharkhand, MKSS, Bengaluru CRCs, benchmarking

Make demand-side pressure real: audits only work when information flows freely.

In simple words
  • The handout calls corruption a wicked problem: more laws signal inefficiency, and it can only be controlled, not removed.
  • Stick measures: prosecution, stings, tighter laws, asset declaration, effective single agency (Singapore CPIB, Hong Kong ICAC). Carrot: pay rise with enforcement, citizen helplines. Administrative: job rotation, auditing, public procurement law, digitisation, CCTV.
  • Radical ideas: disband departments (Georgia's Traffic Police, 2004), gender balance (Peru), monetising bribes (Romania), amnesty. International: FCPA, UK Bribery Act, OECD Convention, UNCAC, FATF.
  • Social audit: scheme details are shared with beneficiaries on public platforms (Jan Sunwai, Gram Sabha) so people hold officials accountable. It needs a sound access-to-information regime.
  • Success stories: Andhra Pradesh SSAAT (MGNREGA), Jharkhand, Rajasthan MKSS (led to RTI Act 2005), Bengaluru Citizen Report Cards. Strengthen with a national law, trained auditors and rotating teams (Karnataka).
TermIn plain words
Social auditPublic verification of scheme work and money with the people it is meant for.

Corruption is a wicked problem: it can be controlled, not eliminated. The toolkit spans stick, carrot, administrative, radical and international measures. Social audit is the participatory tool: public hearings that need a sound RTI regime, with AP's SSAAT as the model.

Value addition

Wicked problem: despite multiple laws (PCA, Lokpal, CVC Act) corruption persists; a proliferation of laws often signals systemic inefficiency. Corruption can only be controlled, not eliminated.

Toolkit

  • TACKLING:
    • Stick: prosecute, one agency
    • Carrot: pay, helplines
    • Admin: rotation, audit, e-gov
    • Radical: disband, amnesty
    • International: FCPA, UNCAC

Stick

  • Legal system and prosecution:
    • Higher conviction rates deter (e.g. Botswana)
    • In India delayed trials and lenient punishment cut deterrence
    • Harsh penalties like China's are unsuitable because of rule-of-law gaps and risk of victimisation
  • Stings and entrapment:
    • Admissibility of sting evidence is ambiguous
    • Natural stings (citizen recordings) should be admissible
    • Legal clarity needed to avoid misuse, as Sweden's restrictive stance shows
  • Tighten anti-corruption laws:
    • 2018 amendments diluted the PCA
    • Political will lacking to plug loopholes in whistleblower law, CVC Act, Lokpal Act
  • Declaration of assets: monitor disproportionate lifestyle indicators and run rigorous audits
  • Effective agencies:
    • India's fragmented set (CVC, CBI, Lokpal, ED) lacks focus
    • Singapore (CPIB) and Hong Kong (ICAC) succeed with a single dedicated agency
  • Public shaming and blacklisting: naming corrupt officials (South Africa), blacklisting firms from public contracts: soft deterrents
  • Penalties on financial security: forfeiting pensions and future benefits creates fear of long-term loss

Carrot

  • substantial pay increase, if paired with strict enforcement, can improve integrity at entry level
  • citizen-centric helplines:
    • Delhi's anti-corruption hotline lets citizens report bribe demands without fear
    • Replication needs effective whistleblower and witness protection laws

Admin/tech

  • job rotation: frequent transfers in sensitive posts break entrenched networks (Kautilya supports)
  • auditing:
    • CAG's limited coverage (25% of auditable units a year) hampers oversight
    • More manpower, IT tools, random audits
  • procurement:
    • Internal bid norms and a comprehensive Public Procurement Law
    • Procurement is 30% of GDP and corruption-prone
    • 2012 bill lapsed, revival urgent
  • digitisation: online delivery cuts physical interface, curbing petty corruption
  • CCTV in high-risk zones (police stations, borders, vulnerable departments), high-risk first

Radical

  • Departmental disbanding (Georgia):
    • Non-reformable departments (Traffic Police, 2004) disbanded
    • Fresh recruitment under stricter norms
  • Gender balance (Peru):
    • More women in bureaucracy showed less corruption
    • Could be piloted in India
  • Monetising bribes (Romania):
    • Allowing official payments to avoid petty bribes (as in its conscription policy)
    • Controversial on equity grounds
  • General amnesty:
    • To disclose black money (with 50% surrender)
    • Debated
    • Stricter enforcement essential after it

International

  • US FCPA 1977 and UK Bribery Act 2010 penalise MNCs' offshore corrupt practices
  • OECD Anti-Bribery Convention 1997 and UNCAC 2003 promote global norms
  • FATF 1989 fights money laundering
  • Interpol IGEC sets corruption standards for police
  • WTO's inaction on corruption as a non-tariff barrier is a missed chance
  • Push for global financial transparency against banking havens. Critique: Western countries ignored corruption until the 1990s
  • Post-globalisation it became a geopolitical tool
  • The "anti-corruption industry" with vested NGO interests often prioritises data production over ground impact
  • Cultural imperialism concerns. Alternatives: spiritual education (ethics and meditation in curricula for internal integrity)
  • Integrity Recognition Certificates (officially recognise honest officers to build peer-pressure networks; must be insulated from politicisation)
Stick, carrot, radical: examples

Social Audit PYQ 2021

Value addition

Definition: details of how a scheme is implemented and its resources, financial and non-financial, are shared with beneficiaries on a public platform (Jan Sunwai, public hearing, Gram Sabha); people can hold the local bureaucracy and Panchayat leaders accountable and judge the real impact. With local people involved, funds, resources, beneficiaries and records are laid bare; people spot loopholes, which checks corruption, raises transparency, fixes accountability and strengthens democracy. Unless public agencies must fully reveal scheme details, social audit is a lost cause: key = sound access to information.

Features

  • multi-perspective: helps beneficiaries and local leaders and civil servants, who can present their side when loopholes are found
  • regular: periodic, so issues are re-evaluated continuously
  • comparative: compares over time and across villages to show weaknesses and good practice
  • strengthens demand side:
    • Builds pressure on local bureaucracy to implement honestly
    • Directly targets demand-side weaknesses, indirectly supply-side ones

Significance

  • more people's participation
  • benefits reach the targeted persons
  • social capital:
    • Community-building
    • People come together to bring change
  • identifies human-resource and training needs of civil servants
  • highlights development concerns
  • promotes transparency, checks corruption
  • helps frame better programmes later

Issues

  • not part of mainstream policy discourse
  • statutory in very few programmes (NREGA is one)
  • lack of trained social auditors
  • opposition from vested interests
  • symptom-cause relationship:
    • Fixing only surface symptoms (awareness drives for plague without tackling poor sanitation, congested housing) gives minimal impact
    • A scheme's failure at a remote symptom level should not justify dropping it unless all causal layers are examined
    • Audits must assess multiple cause-effect layers, so trained auditors are essential

Strengthening

  • Legal and institutional:
    • Model national social audit law inspired by Meghalaya's 2017 Act for all public service schemes (community ownership, accountability)
    • Independent audit units: autonomous, well-resourced, professionally staffed (AP SSAAT)
    • Strengthen CAG oversight and build PRIs' technical and human-resource capacity
  • Stakeholders:
    • Grassroots campaigns for Gram Sabha members
    • Structured auditor training in standards and ethics
    • Partner NGOs and media to amplify findings
    • Recognition for active Gram Sabha participants
  • Standardise and digitise:
    • Online portals with real-time audit reports, scheme spending, PRI records
    • Proactive disclosure of project data
    • Unified audit guidelines and checklists for uniformity and comparability
  • Best practices:
    • Chhattisgarh: link audits with grievance redressal
    • Wall writing for community transparency
    • Karnataka: rotate audit teams after every 3 audit cycles to prevent familiarity and collusion

3 · CBI and CVC#

Handout only · M2 p.11-15
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: 2021*. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • CBI: SPE 1941; DSPE Act 1946; name 1963; four divisions; conviction rate 67% vs 30-40%; no suo motu jurisdiction
  • Vineet Narain 1997: CVC supervises CBI; Director's min. tenure 2 years; committee (PM, LoP, CJI); single directive struck down; DoPT policy only
  • CVC: 1964 executive; statutory 2003; CVC + max 2 VCs; term 4 years or 65; pay = UPSC Chair/members
  • PJ Thomas 2011: consensus not majority in selection committee
  • CVC Chair heads committee for ED Director and CBI officers SP and above

Mains hooks

  • Issues in CBI functioning and Vineet Narain directions
  • CVC powers, issues and suggestions
  • Institutional framework against corruption

Autonomy of anti-graft agencies is the weak link: independence of selection, tenure, prosecution and staff.

In simple words
  • The CBI began as the Special Police Establishment (1941), got statutory backing from the DSPE Act 1946 and was named CBI in 1963. Issues: political control ('caged parrot'), vacancies, sanction delays, no suo motu jurisdiction.
  • Vineet Narain (1997): the Supreme Court gave the CVC superintendence of CBI in PCA cases, fixed the Director's tenure, set a selection committee (PM, LoP, CJI) and struck down the Single Directive.
  • The CVC was set up in 1964 on the Santhanam Committee and made statutory in 2003: one Central Vigilance Commissioner and up to two Vigilance Commissioners, 4-year term, retirement at 65, no reappointment.
  • P.J. Thomas (2011): selection must follow the consensus of the committee, not a majority.
  • Fix list: independent investigation and prosecution powers, statutory sanction timelines, more staff, binding recommendations and a wider selection panel.

Single DirectiveRule requiring sanction before even a preliminary inquiry against senior officers.

Remember it as

CVC: 1964 Santhanam; Act 2003; term 4 years or 65.

The CBI (SPE 1941, DSPE Act 1946, named 1963) is politicised ('caged parrot') despite Vineet Narain (1997). The CVC (Santhanam, 1964; statutory 2003) superintends CBI on PCA cases but depends on it and cannot prosecute.

Legal framework

  • BNS Sec 203 (IPC 169):
    • Civil servants illegally acquiring property is an offence
    • BNS Sec 316 (IPC 409): criminal breach of trust by a public servant
  • DSPE Act 1946 sets up CBI for anti-corruption investigation
  • RPA 1951 Sec 123: corrupt practices in elections
  • PCA 1988: general anti-corruption law
  • Benami Properties Act 1988
  • PMLA 2003: nodal law on money laundering
  • CVC Act
  • RTI 2005 (transparency, accountability)
  • State Lokayuktas Acts
  • Lokpal Act 2013
  • Whistleblower Act 2014
  • Time-bound service delivery laws (to tackle speed money)
  • Institutions: CBI, CVC, state anti-corruption bureaus and vigilance wings, CAG, Lokayuktas, Lokpal, ED (principally PMLA and FEMA), Banking Sector Ombudsman, Consumer Courts, the 3-tier judiciary

Issues

  • politicisation:
    • A former SC judge called CBI a "caged parrot"
    • Every ruling party at the Centre has used such bodies against political opponents, so it is not impartial or autonomous
  • staff shortage: about one-third of posts vacant
  • conviction rate 67% is high vs 30-40% for other agencies, but low in high-profile cases
  • sanction for prosecution:
    • Long delays in disposal (partly staff shortage)
    • After investigation comes prosecution, but PCA Sec 19 and CrPC Sec 197 give civil servants undue protection and sanction must come from the government (state govt, per handout)
    • Delay in sanction means delay in prosecution
  • no suo motu jurisdiction: CBI handles only cases transferred to it

Memory aid · SCATTER

Single Directive struck down · CVC superintends CBI on PCA cases · Appointment of Director by a committee · Tenure minimum 2 years · Transfer by the same committee · Executive (DoPT) sets only the policy framework · Reproach remains: still called a "caged parrot". (own mnemonic)

Directions in detail

  • CVC supervises CBI:
    • CVC deals largely with anti-corruption cases and civil-service discipline violations
    • The CVC Act gives it general superintendence over CBI in PCA cases
  • Minimum guaranteed tenure for the Director and other key CBI posts
  • Under the DSPE Act, 2 years for the Director
  • An ordinance allows extension up to 5 years, after the 2 years, in tranches of 1 year
  • Director's appointment not left to the govt's sole discretion:
    • Committee of the PM, Leader of Opposition in Lok Sabha (or leader of the largest opposition party if there is no recognised LoP) and the CJI
    • The transfer of the Director also needs the same committee
  • Single Directive struck down
  • DoPT: CBI is under its broad direction and control, but only for the overall policy framework, not day-to-day work or investigations

CBI and CVC compared

Selection

  • chosen from two categories:
    • Persons who are or were in All India or Central Services, and officers of CPSUs
    • Selecting from both ensures at least one from each
  • committee:
    • PM, Home Minister, Leader of Opposition (or leader of the largest opposition party)
    • The govt effectively has the final say through its two representatives
  • P.J. Thomas case 2011: SC held that the govt's majority made the LoP's membership pointless, so selection must rest on consensus, not majority (the ruling applied to this appointment, not all committee appointments)

Terms

  • term:
    • 4 years, retirement age 65
    • No reappointment to the same post, but a Vigilance Commissioner can be elevated to CVC if total stay stays within 4 years
  • pay and conditions:
    • CVC = UPSC Chairperson
    • VCs = UPSC members
    • Cannot be altered to their disadvantage
    • Paid from the Consolidated Fund of India
  • Barred from appointment as UT administrator, diplomatic assignments or any post made under the President's warrant and seal
  • No office of profit under Centre or states
  • removal:
    • Proven misbehaviour or incapacity through a reference to the Supreme Court
    • Other grounds: insolvency, moral turpitude, private employment, infirmity of body and mind, prejudicial financial interests

Functions

  • superintend and give directions to CBI in PCA 1988 corruption cases (as directed in Vineet Narain)
  • inquire or cause inquiry on a Central Government reference, or on complaints against Group A officials, AIS officers and equivalent CPSU officials (Sec 8(2) CVC Act 2003)
  • review progress of DSPE investigations under PCA and of pending applications for sanction of prosecution
  • Advice to Central Govt and organisations on referred matters
  • Superintendence over vigilance administrations of ministries and organisations
  • Civil-court powers in inquiries
  • mandatory consultation before the Centre makes rules on vigilance or disciplinary matters of Union services and AIS members
  • CVC is chair (Vigilance Commissioners members) of the committee recommending the Director of Enforcement (and ED officers at Deputy Director level and above, after consulting the Director) and of the committee recommending CBI officers of SP rank and above except the Director, incl. extension or curtailment of their tenure
  • annual report to the President, who places it before Parliament

Issues

  • overwhelming dependence on CBI for investigation, and CBI is politicised and government-controlled
  • No independent prosecution power
  • Sanction needs government permission, often delayed
  • cases of non-cooperative attitude of government
  • poor engagement with civil society, citizens, NGOs in the same field.

Suggestions (handout, with examples)

  • give CVC independent investigative and prosecution powers like Lokpal (2nd ARC Ethics report: functional autonomy for vigilance agencies, less reliance on politicised CBI)
  • statutory 3-6 month timelines for prosecution sanction (Vineet Narain fixed timelines, but TI India 2023: over 70% pending beyond 6 months)
  • make CVC advice a binding oversight over vigilance in ministries and PSUs (Sarkaria)
  • more manpower and technology: dedicated vigilance cadre, digital monitoring (CVC annual report 2023: under 50% of sanctioned staff, over 1500 organisations)
  • civil society participation, public grievance dashboard (2nd ARC; South Korea's ACRC)
  • binding recommendations via amending CVC Act (Punchhi Commission)
  • transparent selection: add judiciary members or eminent anti-corruption experts (P.J. Thomas 2011).
Note

Flag: CVC Selection Committee (PM, HM, LoP) differs from Lokpal (PM, Speaker, LoP, CJI, jurist).

4 · Lokpal in India#

Handout only · M2 p.15-19
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: 2013. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • 9-member body; 50% judicial; 50% affirmative action; age 45+; 5 years or 70; salary CJI/SC judge
  • Selection committee: PM, Speaker, LoP, CJI/nominee, eminent jurist; search committee of 7

Mains hooks

  • Should the PM be covered? 2nd ARC arguments and counter-arguments

  • Lokpal is not a panacea

No single body can cure a hydra: Lokpal works only inside a multi-pronged strategy.

In simple words
  • The ombudsman idea: Sweden 1809, UK 1967. In India the first Bill was introduced in 1968; the present Lokpal Bill was passed in 2013 (becoming the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013); the Lokpal was constituted in 2019 with Justice P.C. Ghose as Chairperson. Maharashtra had the first Lokayukta (1971).
  • State Lokayuktas often became 'paper tigers': recommendatory, misused, and some were abolished (Odisha 1992, Haryana 1999).
  • The Lokpal has nine members including the Chairperson; half judicial, half non-judicial; 50% reserved. Selection committee: PM, Lok Sabha Speaker, Leader of Opposition, CJI or nominee and an eminent jurist.
  • It covers PM (partly), ministers, MPs and Groups A-D, plus bodies funded above Rs 10 lakh. A preliminary inquiry runs within 60 days; a 7-year limit applies to complaints.
  • The PM is covered only partly: no inquiry into decisions on public order, security, international relations, atomic energy and space, and a full-bench two-thirds majority is needed.
  • The record is thin: complaints fell from 1,427 (2019-20) to 110 (2020-21) to 12 till August 2021-22.

Key point

Lokpal = ombudsman or watchdog of administration; first came into existence in Sweden, 1809. UK: designated Parliamentary Commissioner, set up 1967. USA: ombudsman only in some states (Hawaii, Oregon, Nebraska), not federal. First ARC: two-tier set-up: Lokpal to probe officials of Secretary rank and above at Union and State levels; a Union Lokayukta for officials below Secretary rank at the Union level; State Lokayuktas for similar cases in states. India: first Bill introduced 1968; present Bill passed 2013 (became the Act); Lokpal constituted 2019, Justice P.C. Ghose Chairperson of the nine-member body. Maharashtra first state to appoint a Lokayukta (1971).

State experience

  • flooded with complaints: an apex body's efficiency depends on lower departments handling petty complaints well
  • poor effectiveness: powers inadequate to investigate and prosecute properly
  • Inter-state differences in qualifications, tenure, functions, powers, suo motu powers
  • No separate investigation agency
  • survival at the state government's sweet will:
    • Created and abolished by states
    • Odisha abolished it by ordinance in 1992
    • Haryana, 1999
  • paper tigers: mostly recommendatory
  • corruption within Lokayuktas (allegations of extortion)
  • misused to intimidate political opponents

Lokpal ACT 2013 PYQ 2013

9 members · 25 years' experience · min age 45 · term 5 years or 70.

Composition

  • nine-member body including the Chairperson
  • Chairperson: is or has been a CJI or SC judge, or an eminent person with 25 years' experience in vigilance, anti-corruption, law, finance, administration, management
  • 8 members: half judicial (SC judge or High Court Chief Justice), half non-judicial (eminent person with 25 years' experience in the same fields)
  • 50% affirmative action: opportunity for SC, ST, OBC, minorities, women

Conditions

  • Not an MP, MLA or MLC
  • Not a member of any local body
  • minimum age 45
  • not removed or dismissed from government service
  • no other office of profit, no business activity or profession
  • no affiliation with any political party

Appointment

Selection committee may appoint a 7-member search committee to shortlist (also with 50% affirmative action) but may go beyond its names

FLOW OF A Complaint

Complaint → Lokpal may order a preliminary inquiry by its Inquiry Wing, CVC or agencies like CBI; first seeks the public servant's explanation to find a prima facie case; Group C and D complaints go to the CVC; inquiry ends in 60 days
After the inquiry report and a hearing of the public servant, Lokpal can: order full investigation, start departmental proceedings, close the case, or proceed against the complainant if allegations are false
Investigating agencies report to Lokpal and the court; a Lokpal Bench (min 3 members) decides on sanction to prosecute, departmental action, or closure
Lokpal replaces the appointing/dismissing authority in granting prosecution sanction under CrPC 218 and PCA 19

Powers

  • Search, seizure, asset attachment, Civil Procedure Code powers for inquiries, superintendence over CBI for cases Lokpal refers. Removal: on grounds of misbehaviour, through a reference to the Supreme Court, but only if signed by at least 100 MPs
  • On other grounds (insolvency, private employment, infirmity of body or mind, prejudicial financial interest) the President may dismiss on the advice of the Council of Ministers. Also: annual declaration of assets and liabilities by public servants
  • annual report to the President, tabled in Parliament, with a memorandum explaining why recommendations were rejected
  • 7-year limitation period for filing complaints.

Issues AND WAY Forward

Issues

  • jurisdiction too wide
  • very convoluted appointment process
  • PM only partially covered
  • 7-year limitation period is a limit
  • wide scope can lead to intimidation of NGOs
  • delays in appointments
  • Functioning since 2019:
    • Allegations of emasculating the body through legislation
    • Govt delayed rules on the complaint form, asset disclosure, and Lokpal's rules for preliminary inquiry and investigation
    • Prosecution and investigation wings not set up by 2020
    • Yet by Sept 2019 it had disposed of 1,000 of 1,065 complaints
    • Complaints keep falling: 1,427 (2019-20) → 110 (2020-21) → 12 till August of 2021-22

2nd ARC

  • give constitutional status (now only statutory) to both the Union and State bodies, for independence and respect
  • Rashtriya Lok Ayukta (Union level): Union Ministers, MPs, State Chief Ministers
  • State Lokayukta: State Ministers and state legislatures
  • CVC should handle civil servants
  • Local Bodies Ombudsman for political and administrative officials of local bodies

Not a panacea

  • corruption is a hydra-headed monster, needing a multi-pronged strategy
  • the bureaucracy is huge, so Lokpal cannot handle all complaints
  • how well govt bodies deal with petty complaints should be examined, since Lokpal gets them too
  • inadequate judicial process
  • Developed nations have an ombudsman, empowered citizens and willingness to complain
  • India lacks these

5 · Whistleblower Protection and the PCA 1988#

Handout only · M2 p.19-20
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: 2015, 2022. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • Competent authority: PM (Union Ministers), CM (state ministers), Presiding Officer (legislators), CVC (civil servants)
  • No anonymous complaints; frivolous complaint penalised with 2-year jail
  • 2015 amendment Bill introduced, then lapsed (never became law): OSA to override the Act
  • SEBI tipping mechanism: reward up to Rs 1 crore
  • PCA 2018: bribe-giving an offence; 7-day complaint defence; jail 3 to 7 years; trial in 2 years, max 4; Sec 17A extends to retired servants

Mains hooks

  • Challenges with the Whistleblower Act and measures to strengthen it
  • 2nd ARC and Law Commission framework
  • Why the 2018 PCA amendment is both strengthening and diluting

A whistleblower law without anonymity protects the disclosure but not the discloser.

In simple words
  • The Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014 lets anyone disclose corruption or wilful misuse of power by a public body. Disclosures go to the competent authority (PM for Union Ministers, CM for state ministers, Presiding Officer for legislators, CVC for civil servants). Anonymous complaints are not entertained; frivolous ones can earn 2 years' jail.
  • Challenges: no anonymity (Satyendra Dubey), weak implementation and awareness, little protection from victimisation. Needs: wider coverage including the private sector; SEBI's tipping reward up to Rs 1 crore.
  • The Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 covers every public servant: government staff, PSU employees, local body functionaries, judges, PSC members, university employees, MPs and MLAs.
  • The 2018 Amendment: giving a bribe is an offence (with a 7-day complaint exemption in coercive cases); Sec 17A extends protection to retired servants; jail raised from 3 to 7 years; trial in 2 years (up to 4); commercial organisations can be prosecuted.

Remember it as

PCA 2018 cuts both ways: it penalises the bribe-giver and shields the officer.

Whistleblower protection map

The 2014 Act has no anonymity, narrow safeguards and a lapsed 2015 amendment bill that would have let the Official Secrets Act override it. The PCA 1988 covers all public servants; the 2018 amendment criminalised bribe-giving, raised penalties and added Sec 17A.

Whistle Blowers Protection ACT 2014

Value addition

PIDPI Act 2014 (Public Interest Disclosure and Protection of Individuals making Disclosure): anyone can be a whistleblower about any public body or agency. Disclosure goes to the competent authority. No anonymous complaints. The CVC can issue directions for protection. Frivolous complaints: 2 years' jail. A 2015 amendment bill (introduced, later lapsed) proposed that the Official Secrets Act 1923 should override the Whistleblowers Act. (Handout also describes it as a mechanism to receive complaints on corruption or wilful misuse of power or discretion, with inquiry and safeguards against victimisation.)

Challenges

  • lack of anonymity:
    • The Act mandates disclosing the whistleblower's identity, exposing them to retaliation
    • The murder of Satyendra Dubey, who reported corruption in the NHAI, shows the danger and discourages whistleblowers
  • weak organisational implementation: no clear guidelines or awareness, so employees hesitate to use official channels
  • inadequate legal safeguards: limited protection against victimisation, career setbacks, harassment, even physical harm
  • rising complaints show latent potential: in 2018, NSE-listed companies got over 3,500 complaints

Flow charts#

The sanction-to-prosecute chain (Sections 15, 17)

Single Directive: sanction needed even for preliminary inquiry against Joint Secretary and above
1997 Vineet Narain: Supreme Court strikes the Single Directive
DSPE Act amended: Single Directive made law as Sec 6A
2014: Sec 6A struck down (equality)
2018: PCA Sec 17A: permission for preliminary inquiry for all public servants, except if caught red-handed (extends to retired servants)
Meanwhile: PCA Sec 19 and CrPC Sec 197 still need sanction to prosecute; Lokpal replaces the sanctioning authority in its cases

A complaint before the Lokpal (Section 18)

Any individual files a complaint for a PCA 1988 offence
Lokpal orders a preliminary inquiry (Inquiry Wing, CVC or CBI) and seeks the public servant's explanation
Complaints against Group C and D officials go to the CVC
Preliminary inquiry finishes within 60 days
Lokpal bench (min 3) hears the public servant, then: full investigation, departmental proceedings, close the case, or act against a false complainant
If PM is the accused: full bench, two-thirds majority; not public order, security, international relations, atomic energy, space
Prosecution wing and court; time limit for the complaint: 7 years

  3. Sec 6A chain Single Directive -> struck 1997 (Vineet Narain) -> Sec 6A DSPE -> struck 2014 -> Sec 17A PCA 2018.
  4. Georgia, Singapore, Hong Kong examples Georgia disbanded Traffic Police (2004); Singapore CPIB and Hong Kong ICAC as single agencies.
  5. Karnataka's social audit feature Audit teams rotated after every three cycles.
  6. CBI: origin and name SPE 1941; DSPE Act 1946; named CBI 1963; no suo motu jurisdiction.
  7. CVC term, age and members 4 years, retirement at 65; CVC plus not more than two Vigilance Commissioners.
  8. P.J. Thomas case (2011) CVC selection must be by consensus of the committee, not majority.
  9. Lokpal composition Nine members: Chair + 8; half judicial; 50% affirmative action; minimum age 45.
  10. Lokpal limits Preliminary inquiry in 60 days; complaint within 7 years; Groups C and D to CVC.
  11. Whistleblower Act competent authorities PM (Union Ministers), CM (State ministers), Presiding Officer (legislators), CVC (civil servants).
  12. PCA 2018 headline changes Bribe-giving offence (7-day defence if coerced); jail 3 to 7 years; trial in 2 (max 4) years; Sec 17A.

2022 · 10 marks · Whistle blower, who reports corruption and illegal activities, wrongdoing and misconduct to the concerned auth…

Question. Whistle blower, who reports corruption and illegal activities, wrongdoing and misconduct to the concerned authorities, runs the risk of being exposed to grave danger, physical harm and victimization by the vested interests, accused persons and his team. What policy measures would you suggest to strengthen protection mechanism to safeguard the whistle blower?

The 2014 Act mandates disclosure of identity and offers limited protection; Satyendra Dubey's murder shows the risk.

  • Confidentiality and anonymity (Law Commission framework)
  • Legal protection against victimisation and administrative safeguards against harassment and bodily harm
  • Extend coverage to corporate whistleblowers; 2nd ARC: stronger law for public and private sectors
  • Organisational guidelines and awareness; SEBI-style rewards up to Rs 1 crore; CVC power to direct protection

Close. Protect the person, not just the disclosure.

2021 · 15 marks · The jurisdiction of the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) regarding lodging an FIR and conducting a probe …

Question. The jurisdiction of the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) regarding lodging an FIR and conducting a probe within a particular State is being questioned by various States. However, the power of the States to withhold consent to the CBI is not absolute. Explain with special reference to the federal character of India.

CBI draws its powers from the DSPE Act 1946 and has no suo motu jurisdiction; it handles cases transferred to it.

  • Vineet Narain 1997: CVC superintends CBI on PCA cases; DoPT sets only policy
  • Issues: politicised 'caged parrot', one-third vacancies
  • Federal angle: not in the modules, so add it separately

Close. State consent protects federalism but cannot be used to block corruption probes. (Partial fit: no content on state consent or federal case law.)

2021 · 10 marks · An independent and empowered social audit mechanism is an absolute must in every sphere of public service, inc…

Question. An independent and empowered social audit mechanism is an absolute must in every sphere of public service, including the judiciary, to ensure performance, accountability and ethical conduct. Elaborate.

Social audit shares scheme details with beneficiaries at public hearings so the people can find loopholes.

  • Independence: AP's SSAAT, Karnataka's rotation of audit teams after three cycles, Meghalaya 2017 Act as model
  • Empowerment: statutory in very few programmes (NREGA); needs trained auditors, Gram Sabha awareness, digital portals
  • Results: crores recovered in AP; Jharkhand SAU exposed ghost beneficiaries; SC in Swaraj Abhiyan (2016)
  • Judiciary: RTI coverage held in 2019, but the notes give no audit model for it

Close. Audit works when information is open and the auditor is independent of the audited.

2019 · 10 (150 words) marks · "Non-performance of duty by a public servant is a form of corruption" Do you agree with this view? Justify you…

Question. "Non-performance of duty by a public servant is a form of corruption" Do you agree with this view? Justify your answer.

Agree, in the wider sense: corruption includes any behaviour that harms public interest.

  • Handout list: lethargy, dereliction or negligence of duty, repeated carelessness, non-punctuality, favouritism and nepotism
  • TI's definition ('private abuse of public office') and the Elephant Test: society recognises it
  • Non-performance harms citizens like speed money does (white corruption tolerated)
  • Caveat: stretching the definition too wide dilutes focus

Close. A public servant who does not perform abuses the office for a private ease.

2016 · 12.5 marks · In the integrity index of Transparency International, India stands very low. Discuss briefly the legal, politi…

Question. In the integrity index of Transparency International, India stands very low. Discuss briefly the legal, political, economic, social and cultural factors that have caused the decline of public morality in India.

TI's index is perception-based, but the handout's factor list explains a low rating.

  • Legal: Art 311 protection, Sec 19 PCA and Sec 197 CrPC sanctions, delayed prosecution, no effective whistleblower law, weak autonomy of CBI and CVC
  • Political: no will on Lokpal, criminalisation of politics, money power, weak party-funding disclosure, TINA
  • Economic: licence-permit-quota to clearance raj, consumerism, poor pay, MNC willingness to bribe
  • Social: shaming of government staff, no popular sanction, social evils like dowry, thymos (low self-worth)
  • Cultural: long history of corruption, tolerance, culture-specific norms

Close. Public morality will recover only when opportunity, impunity and tolerance fall together.

2015 · 12.5 marks · If amendment bill to the Whistleblowers Act, 2011 tabled in the Parliament is passed, there may be no one left…

Question. If amendment bill to the Whistleblowers Act, 2011 tabled in the Parliament is passed, there may be no one left to protect. Critically evaluate.

The 2015 amendment bill proposed that the Official Secrets Act 1923 would override the Act; it lapsed.

  • Protection already weak: no anonymity (Satyendra Dubey murder), limited safeguards against victimisation
  • Competent authority route: PM, CM, Presiding Officer, CVC
  • Frivolous complaints face 2-year jail
  • Fixes: Law Commission framework (confidentiality, anonymity, protection from bodily harm, corporate whistleblowers); SEBI tipping up to Rs 1 crore

Close. A whistleblower law that yields to the Official Secrets Act protects secrecy, not the whistleblower.

2014 · 10 marks · It is often said that poverty leads to corruption. However, there is no dearth of instances where affluent and…

Question. It is often said that poverty leads to corruption. However, there is no dearth of instances where affluent and powerful people indulge in corruption in a big way. What are the basic causes of corruption among people? Support your answer with examples.

Poor pay is blamed, but the handout says raising salaries alone may not dent corruption.

  • Psycho-social: thymos (low self-worth), consumerism, Squirrel Nuts Syndrome, opportunity theory
  • Policy (grand) corruption: crony capitalism and state capture by business houses; MNCs able and willing to bribe
  • Systemic: clearance raj, red tape, middlemen, weak sanctions (Art 311, Sec 19)
  • Examples in the handout: Mundhra scandal, pork-barreling, German car insurance case

Close. Opportunity and impunity, not poverty, are the common thread.

2013 · 10 marks · 'A national Lokpal, however strong it may be, cannot resolve the problems of immorality in public affairs'. Di…

Question. 'A national Lokpal, however strong it may be, cannot resolve the problems of immorality in public affairs'. Discuss.

The handout calls Lokpal 'not a panacea': corruption is a hydra-headed monster.

  • Needs a multi-pronged strategy; huge bureaucracy; petty complaints need departmental handling
  • Judicial process inadequate; no citizen empowerment or willingness to complain
  • Causes of immorality are psycho-social, economic, legal, administrative and political (Sec 15)
  • What Lokpal adds: inquiry and prosecution wings, search, seizure, attachment, sanction replaced

Close. Institutions police conduct; immorality is cured by opportunity reduction, transparency and ethics.

Practice · Should the Prime Minister be brought under the Lokpal? Examine in light of the Lokpal Act 2013.

The Act covers the PM partially.

  • 2nd ARC against: cripples administration, foreign exploitation, PCA already applies
  • For: CBI works under DoPT; lead by example; Art 361 exempts only President and Governors
  • Act: no inquiry on public order, security, international relations, atomic energy, space; full-bench two-thirds majority

Close. Partial coverage balances accountability against continuity, but leaves the main objection unanswered.

Practice · How can the success of Andhra Pradesh's social audit model be scaled up nationally?

AP's SSAAT is an autonomous society that trained village resource persons.

  • Result: crores of wage misappropriation recovered; World Bank global best practice
  • Scale: national law on Meghalaya 2017 lines; independent audit units; PRI capacity
  • Add: trained auditors, rotation of audit teams (Karnataka), wall writing and grievance link (Chhattisgarh), digital portals

Close. Independence of auditors and access to information decide whether audits scale.
