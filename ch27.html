The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Because India in 1885 had no single political community, the Moderates built unity and a parliamentary habit, backed Indian capitalists, and then proved with the Drain Theory that Britain used its political control to move India's wealth away. The drain ran through plunder, tariffs, exchange rates, Council Bills and Home Charges, so the economic critique became a demand for Swaraj.

  1. A land with no single people. In 1885 the first INC session at Bombay declared its goal as building national unity. India was a land of regions, languages, castes and religions with no pre-existing single political community, so the Moderates decided that building one came before demanding rights from the British.
    → So how do you build a nation inside a Congress?
  2. One versus only. In 1886 Naoroji laid down the informal rule that the INC takes only political questions, not social or class ones, so the fight is Indians against British and not Hindu against Muslim, upper against lower caste, men against women, worker against capitalist or ryot against landlord. In 1888 the INC added a veto (no resolution if the majority of Hindu or Muslim members disagree) and in 1889 the Minority Clause, the INC's own demand for reserved seats in the ILC and PLCs.
    → Rules alone do not make leaders, so the structure had to help.
  3. The host never presides. Each session was held in a different part of the country and the President was never from the host region. A Marathi leader presiding in Calcutta and speaking to Bengali concerns builds an all-India leadership and inter-regional bonds, nation building by simple design.
    → Those sessions also taught India a new habit.
  4. A parliament before a parliament. The President functioned like a Speaker and members spoke, debated and voted on resolutions, which was new for India. Today's Lok Sabha procedures have their roots in the culture of INC sessions, so the modern political era began with the INC.
    → But a nation also needs an economy, and the Moderates had a view on that.
  5. Why back the capitalists? The Moderates were not anti-poor, they were pro-industrialisation: every industrialised country had risen on its capitalist class, communist industrialisation was born only in 1917 and showed results after 1929, and Indian capitalists were struggling against British imports and British firms in India.
    → Yet their sharpest weapon was an idea about wealth leaving India.
  6. The Drain Theory. After the disappointment with the Indian Councils Act of 1892 the Moderates built the Drain Theory: an evidence-based argument that Britain used its political control over the Indian State for its own economic benefit, so the White Man's Burden was false. Naoroji (Poverty & Un-British Rule in India, 1901), R.C. Dutt (1902 and 1904) and Ranade (father of Indian economics) made it, spread by press, speeches and books.
    → The teacher first separates rupees from wealth, then asks how the machine worked.
  7. How British factories rebuilt India. Before British industrialisation India was a net exporter of finished goods. Afterwards it was a raw-material source and an export market: farms grew cotton, indigo, jute and opium for British mills, handicrafts were destroyed, and artisans with no Indian factories to join were forced into an overburdened agriculture.
    → Then steam made the pump faster.
  8. Steam, rail and the Suez shortcut. The steam engine (1769) later brought machines, ships and railways, and the Suez Canal (1859-69) cut the sea distance by 4,500 miles. The teacher's board shows the rail-road-port loop carrying raw materials out and finished goods in, with railways built under Dalhousie 48-56 for the army, and he writes that the loot escalated.
    → Now, how exactly did the money leave?
  9. Plunder first, policy later. Before 1813 the drain was Dastak misuse, the Plassey plunder of 1757-65 and land revenue from 1765. After 1813 it was policy: no protective tariff until World War I, a managed exchange rate that favoured British business, and Council Bills in which the importer's forex stayed in London and the GOI paid rupees from taxpayers.
    → And then the bills were sent in the name of Britain's services.
  10. The cafe bill and the double drain. Home Charges are what India paid Britain for goods and services Britain said it gave, Home meaning Britain; the teacher says to think like a British nationalist, since the bill reads as services (governance and security of the territory), goods (stores), stability (War Office) and development (FDI interest). The eight heads include salaries and pensions, EIC dividends, loan and foreign-debt interest, India Office costs, stores, War Office payments and FDI interest. The army abroad is a drain but not an HC, because India gave that service. India sold more than it bought in rupees (Ex > Im) yet lost the forex it earned, a trade surplus but a wealth deficit, the double drain.
    → The British had answers, and the notes have replies.
  11. Railways, canals and only 2 per cent. The teacher marks this whole debate 'mmp'. The British pointed to railways (but with a guaranteed 5% in London, army purpose, 78% coverage by 1947; on his board FDI became FD: free land on a 99-year lease, buy-back, losses on the Indian taxpayer), canals (but only 25% irrigated, outside Permanent Settlement areas, water not free) and said the drain was only 2%. Naoroji replied that 2% was surplus capital that would have compounded, and the chapter closes that the critique was inseparable from the demand for Swaraj.

In simple words#

  • The Moderates saw that India had no single political community in 1885, so the INC's first goal was building national unity: political questions only (1886), a communal veto (1888), the Minority Clause (1889) and rotating sessions.
  • INC sessions copied parliament (President like a Speaker; speak, debate, vote), and the Moderates backed Indian capitalists because they wanted industrialisation and no communist model existed yet.
  • Their great idea was the Drain Theory:
    • Britain used political control over the Indian State to take economic benefit, so the White Man's Burden was false
    • Naoroji, Dutt and Ranade built it with books, press and speeches
  • British industrialisation made India a raw-material source and export market: cash crops, ruined handicrafts, artisans pushed into overburdened agriculture
  • Steam transport and the Suez Canal made the loot faster
  • The drain ran through Dastaks, the Plassey plunder and LR before 1813, then no protective tariffs, a managed exchange rate, Council Bills and Home Charges, and India lost both raw material and forex.
  • The British replied with railways, canals and 'only 2%' (all marked 'mmp')
  • The notes answer each (the army, the 5% guarantee that made FDI become FD, 25% irrigated, Naoroji's compounding) and tie the drain to the demand for Swaraj

1 · Nation building#

Story link

Because India had no single political community, unity came before rights. Ask: how did the 1886 rule, the 1888 veto and the 1889 clause each serve unity?

What was the first goal the INC declared?

  • At the very first INC session in 1885 in Bombay, the Congress declared its goal as "building national unity."
  • This was not an accident. India in 1885 was a land of regions, languages, castes and religions, with no pre-existing sense of a single Indian political community.
  • The Moderates understood that building such a community was the first task, even before demanding rights from the British.
  • So they made a deliberate choice to focus only on issues common to all Indians.
  • In 1886, Dadabhai Naoroji laid down an informal INC rule: the Congress would take up only political questions, not social or class questions, because social issues could divide Indians against each other.

2 · Building a culture of parliamentary politics#

Story link

Because unity needed a shared habit, INC sessions copied parliament. Ask: which parts of a Lok Sabha sitting did a session reproduce?

How did INC sessions contribute beyond passing resolutions?

  • The INC sessions were consciously modelled on parliamentary practice.
  • A President functioned like a Speaker.
  • Members spoke, debated and voted on resolutions. For India, this was new.
  • Watch a Lok Sabha session today: the procedures, the order of speaking, the formal adoption of resolutions all have roots in the culture built by INC sessions from 1885 onwards.
  • That is why it can be said that the modern political era in India, in a true sense, began with the INC.

3.2 · Drain Theory#

Story link

Because the ICA 1892 disappointed them, the Moderates built their sharpest idea. Ask: why is wealth not always equal to rupees?

What is the most important intellectual contribution of the Moderates?

  • Without question it is the Drain Theory: the systematic, evidence-based argument that British rule was economically exploiting India, not developing it, and that the entire claim of the White Man's Burden (WMB) was false.
  • The theory gained particular prominence after the bitter disappointment with the Indian Councils Act of 1892 (ICA 1892), which failed to deliver any meaningful reform (see Chapter 28).
  • Core idea: the British were using their political control over the Indian State to extract economic benefits for Britain.
  • This was not charity or development: it was exploitation.
  • The Moderates proved it with data and argument, building an economic critique of colonialism that was sharp, factual and difficult to counter.
  • Ahead of its time: Lenin and Lala Lajpat Rai would later argue along similar lines, that colonialism is the product of British capitalism needing new markets and raw materials.
  • The Moderates reached this conclusion through an economic lens well before it became a standard argument in global socialist circles.

The net effect: double drain through asymmetric trade (Lecture 30, slide 4)

  • (i) Net exporter of raw materials. India shipped out more raw material (cotton, jute, indigo, tea, opium) than it imported, on paper a "positive" trade balance.

  • The 5% started the moment the FDI was made, so investors earned 5% even in the years the railway was under construction and earning nothing.

The British argumentThe counter-arguments

The British rejoinder: "the drain was only 2%"

The British argumentThe counter-arguments
The drain was only 2% of India's export value in the early 1900s. This small amount paid for real development: it attracted FDI into railways, roadways and irrigationQuantity: GDP growth was not substantial
