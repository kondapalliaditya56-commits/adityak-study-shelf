1 · Article 19 · Restrictions · Defamation · Subramanian Swamy#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights handout pp. 17-20. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Art 19(1) gives citizens seven (now six) freedoms; each has its own list of grounds for restriction. Speech has the longest list (eight). Press, silence, information and privacy are inferred. Criminal defamation survived in Subramanian Swamy (2016), now sits in BNS s.356.

Article 19(1): THE Rights (citizens ONLY)

(b)assemble peaceably and without arms19(3)
(c)form associations, unions, co-operative societies19(4)
(d)move freely throughout India19(5)
(e)reside and settle anywhere in India19(5)
(f)omitted (property)-
(g)practise any profession, occupation, trade or business19(6)

Grounds OF Restriction

19(2): speech and expression (eight grounds)

  • Sovereignty and integrity of India
  • Security of the State
  • Friendly relations with foreign States
  • Public order
  • Decency or morality
  • Contempt of court
  • Defamation
  • Incitement to an offence

Eight grounds: Sovereignty · Security · Friendly relations · Public order · Decency-morality · Contempt · Defamation · Incitement. (own mnemonic)

ClauseReasonable restrictions in the interests of
19(3) assemblysovereignty and integrity · public order
19(4) associationsovereignty and integrity · public order · morality
19(5) movement, residencegeneral public · protection of any Scheduled Tribe
19(6) profession, tradegeneral public; may also lay down professional or technical qualifications and allow State or State-owned corporations to carry on a trade, wholly or partly excluding citizens

Rights Inferred FROM 19(1)(a)

Four inferred rights

  • Right to silence: freedom to speak includes, in context, the freedom not to speak
  • Freedom of the press:
    • Expressing other people's views rests on freedom of expression
    • Not separately listed
  • Right to information:
    • You cannot express well without information
    • Right to know is an aspect of 19(1)(a)
  • Right to privacy:
    • Constant surveillance creates a chilling effect
    • Privacy is a Fundamental Right chiefly under Art 21, linked with Art 19
Memory aid · SPIP

Inferred from speech: Silence · Press · Information · Privacy. (own mnemonic)

Defamation

Legal position

  • Defamation is a ground in 19(2)
  • Gives rise to civil and criminal proceedings
  • criminal defamation:
    • Section 356 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023
    • Earlier IPC Sections 499 and 500
  • exceptions under s.356:
    • Good-faith imputations
    • Comment on public conduct of public servants
    • Truth for the public good
    • Magistrates are said to apply it mechanically without checking exceptions

Issues with criminal defamation

  • public remedy for a private wrong: civil wrong between parties in most democracies
  • chilling effect:
    • Self-censorship of journalists and opposition
    • Followers file complaints
    • Social media lets cases be filed in many parts of the country
    • Used to harass activists and journalists
  • access:
    • Costs and time favour the powerful
    • The common person struggles
Note

Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India (2016): challenge to IPC 499/500 as an unreasonable restriction on 19(1)(a). For retention: State must protect reputation (dignity); freedom is not absolute. Against: chilling effect; civil damages suffice. SC upheld criminal defamation: right to reputation is part of Art 21 (life and dignity), balanced against free speech; stressed dignity and fraternity. Critics: missed a chance to decriminalise.

2 · Contempt OF Court#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights handout pp. 20-22. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Courts can punish contempt under Arts 129 and 215 and the 1971 Act. The trouble is vagueness: scandalising the court, judge in own cause, intent vs effect. Mulgaokar (1978) says use sparingly; the UK abolished scandalising in 2013. Reform: objective standards.

Meaning AND Types

Constitutional and statutory basis

  • Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 defines and regulates it
  • Art 129: Supreme Court is a Court of Record and can punish its own contempt
  • Art 215: same for High Courts

Civil contempt

  • wilful disobedience of any judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or process
  • or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court

Criminal contempt

  • scandalising or lowering the authority of a court
  • prejudicing or interfering with judicial proceedings
  • interfering with or obstructing the administration of justice in any other way
Value addition

Why the power exists: unpopular rulings can trigger campaigns that undermine the independence and authority of the judiciary; without independence the public interest suffers.

Issues

Five issues

  • Nemo judex in causa sua (no one is judge in their own cause): the court whose authority is attacked may itself decide the contempt
  • Vagueness:
    • "scandalising the court"
    • No objective yardstick
    • Depends on judges' subjective assessment, so outcomes vary, which touches equality before law
    • Broad use stifles genuine criticism
  • Intent vs effect:
    • Proceedings may look at the tendency of criticism rather than mens rea
    • Relevance of intention depends on the type of contempt
  • Impression of infallibility:
    • Judges are human
    • Just criticism of a judge is not criticism obstructing the institution
  • Judge vs court:
    • Contempt of court is not contempt of an individual judge
    • The line blurs
Memory aid · NVIJ

Issues: Nemo judex · Vagueness · Intent vs effect · Infallibility · Judge vs court. (own mnemonic)

Note

In Re S. Mulgaokar (1978): Justice V. R. Krishna Iyer: judges should have broad shoulders; protect the administration of justice, not the personal reputation of judges.

Mulgaokar guidelines

  • Sparing use
  • Not every criticism merits contempt
  • act seriously when an attack is calculated to obstruct or destroy the judicial process
  • distinguish judge from court
  • press freedom: the press may criticise courts within responsible limits
  • judicial restraint even if language is harsh
  • for malicious or intimidatory attacks, use contempt in the public interest

Safeguards IN THE 1971 ACT

Section 13 and Section 15

  • s.13:
    • No sentence unless the contempt substantially interferes with due course of justice
    • After the 2006 amendment truth is a defence if in public interest and bona fide
  • s.15:
    • Criminal contempt (other than in the court's presence): suo motu by SC or HC
    • Others need consent of the Attorney General or Solicitor General (SC) or Advocate General (HC)
    • Consent rule does not restrict suo motu jurisdiction

Comparisons AND Reform

CountryPosition
UKabolished scandalising the judiciary as contempt in England and Wales in 2013; "old fools" illustration: being old is a fact, being a fool is opinion
USAstrong free-expression protection; contempt is more restrictive for criticism outside the courtroom

Reform agenda

  • objective standards in place of vague phrases (Art 19(1)(a) compatibility)
  • Criticism is not obstruction
  • Keep contempt for conduct that obstructs justice
  • judicial accountability: criticism improves institutions
  • protect the administration of justice, not judges' reputation

3 · BOOK BANS · Sedition · Obscenity · FILM Certification · Section 66A#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights handout pp. 23-27. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Controversy alone does not justify a ban; the State must stay inside Art 19(2). Sedition (s.124A) was read down in Kedar Nath, put in abeyance in 2022 and replaced by BNS s.152. Obscenity moved from Hicklin to community standards. Section 66A fell in Shreya Singhal (2015).

Banning OF Books

Position and arguments against bans

  • Restrictions only on grounds in Art 19(2)
  • A book being controversial, unpopular or offensive is not enough
  • freedom of expression central to liberal democracy, even for unpopular ideas
  • right to receive information: bans hurt readers too
  • chilling effect: writers and historians self-censor
  • threat of violence:
    • The State should not reward it by suppressing lawful expression
    • It must protect expression and keep order
Note

S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram (1989): expression cannot be suppressed because of threatened demonstrations or violence; State must protect lawful expression; need a proximate connection to harm, not illusory or remote threats. A film case, but applies to books too.

Sedition

History

  • Colonial offence put in the IPC in 1870
  • Used against Tilak and against Gandhi (1922)
  • IPC s.124A:
    • Hatred, contempt or disaffection against the government established by law
    • Criticised for vague wording and misuse against journalists, activists, students and opponents
Note

Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar (1962): SC upheld s.124A but restricted it to speech or acts involving incitement to violence or tendency to create public disorder. Strong criticism of government alone is not sedition.

Issues

  • colonial legacy
  • misuse against dissent
  • chilling effect on 19(1)(a)
  • government of the day is not the State or the nation
  1. 1870
    sedition enters the IPC
  2. 1922
    Gandhi prosecuted
  3. 1962

  4. 2022
    SC directs s.124A be kept in abeyance; deals with fresh and pending cases
  5. 1 Jul 2024

BNS Section 152: acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity

  • covers purposely or knowingly exciting secession, armed rebellion, subversive activities, encouraging separatist feelings, endangering sovereignty, unity, integrity
  • by words, signs, visible representation, electronic communication, financial means or otherwise
  • punishment: life imprisonment or up to seven years, plus fine
  • Explanation protects lawful criticism: disapproval of government measures to get them changed by lawful means
  • critics:
    • "subversive" and "separatist" need careful reading or can catch dissent
    • Must satisfy 19(1)(a) and 19(2)

Obscenity

  1. 1868
    Hicklin test: obscene if it can deprave and corrupt susceptible minds; isolated passages can be judged
  2. 1965
    Ranjit Udeshi: SC applied Hicklin; upheld conviction over sale of Lady Chatterley's Lover
  3. 2014
    Aveek Sarkar: SC rejected Hicklin; contemporary community standards; judge the work as a whole in context; mere nudity is not obscenity

Law and balance

  • Earlier IPC 292-294
  • Now BNS 294-296
  • Online: IT Act ss.67 and 67A
  • Sexuality or nudity is not automatically obscene
  • Balance artistic expression with decency and morality
Memory aid · HRA

Obscenity tests in order: Hicklin (1868) → Ranjit Udeshi (1965, applied it) → Aveek Sarkar (2014, community standards). (own mnemonic)

FILM Certification

Framework

  • cinema is protected by 19(1)(a) subject to 19(2)
  • CBFC:
    • Statutory body under the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting, set up under the Cinematograph Act, 1952
    • Films need prior certification (pre-censorship)
  • K. A. Abbas (1970): upheld pre-censorship with safeguards because of the film's visual and emotional impact
  • Rangarajan (1989):
    • No suppression for threats of protest
    • No Heckler's Veto

Cinematograph (Amendment) Act, 2023

  • age-based categories UA 7+, 13+, 16+
  • perpetual validity of certificates (earlier 10-year limit removed)
  • anti-piracy provisions on unauthorised recording and exhibition
  • removed the Centre's revisional powers under s.6(1), consistent with K. M. Shankarappa
  • Cinematograph (Certification) Rules, 2024: online applications, transparency, priority screening

Issues

  • subjectivity
  • paternalism toward adults
  • political influence
  • chilling effect (cuts, delays)
  • certification should guide, not censor

Section 66A, IT ACT

What it was

  • Inserted by the IT (Amendment) Act, 2008, in force 2009
  • Punished messages that were grossly offensive, menacing, annoying, inconvenient or misleading
  • Up to 3 years and fine
  • problems:
    • Vague terms, arbitrary use against criticism, chilling effect
    • Mere annoyance is not a 19(2) ground

Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015)

  • SC struck down s.66A as violating 19(1)(a), not saved by 19(2). Principles: discussion and advocacy are protected, only incitement tied to a 19(2) ground can be restricted
  • vagueness
  • overbreadth
  • chilling effect. 2022:
    • Cases still being filed
    • SC said no one can be prosecuted under 66A and ordered references removed from pending cases

Significance

  • online speech is protected by 19(1)(a)
  • cannot criminalise speech for being annoying, inconvenient or offensive
  • restrictions must be clear, reasonable, within 19(2)
  • reaffirms judicial review

4 · Freedom OF Speech AND Expression · Significance · Balance · Threats#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights handout pp. 27-28. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Speech has value in itself, keeps democracy accountable, builds community, drives change and the marketplace of ideas. Restrictions must be proportionate; err on the side of freedom. Threats: old laws, weak politicians, weak courts, compliant media, Heckler's Veto, pliant officials.

WHY Freedom OF Speech Matters

Five reasons

  • Inherent value:
    • A faculty unique to humans (imaginative and visual communication)
    • Expression of human nature
  • Cornerstone of democracy:
    • Accountability through free media and criticism
    • Not absolute, but society must tolerate unpopular views (Galileo, Socrates)
  • Enables community life:
    • Minorities feel they belong only if their views are heard
    • Collective life needs equal worth of views
  • Enables progressive change:
    • Women's suffrage, workers' conditions, African American voting rights
    • Better governance through ideas and criticism
  • Promotes innovation:
    • Marketplace of ideas
    • Best ideas gain acceptance
    • Contest refines ideas
Memory aid · ICC-PI

Significance: Inherent value · Cornerstone of democracy · Community life · Progress · Innovation. (own mnemonic)

Balancing Freedom AND Restriction

Proportionality

  • freedom is not absolute: it must not become a tool for mischief or for violating others' rights
  • restrictions must be proportionate: only as wide as the objective needs
  • when in doubt, err on the side of freedom
  • Example:
    • Banning two-wheelers to stop road accidents is over-restriction
    • Tackle the causes of accidents instead
Note

Current affairs (verify): the August 2026 magazine notes In Re Noise Pollution (2005): Art 19(1)(a) does not permit amplified noise that violates others' rights. Use it as a boundary example for speech vs others' rights.

Threats TO Freedom OF Speech IN India

Five threats

  • Obsolete colonial laws and moral standards not revised for modern times
  • Weakness of the political class in amending or repealing restrictive laws
  • Misuse to silence dissent
  • Weakness of the judiciary:
    • Reluctance to strike down or narrow such laws
    • Sedition shows a law can chill speech even with few convictions
    • Relief comes after long litigation
  • Weakness of media and publishers:
    • Aligning with government
    • Publishers withdrawing books under threat = Heckler's Veto
  • Moral corruption of officials: carrying out orders under political pressure despite weak charges
Memory aid · OPJMO

Threats: Obsolete laws · Politicians · Judiciary · Media and publishers · Officials. (own mnemonic)

Way forward / key point

Heckler's Veto: the threat of disruption or violence by one group effectively silences a speaker. The remedy (Rangarajan): the State must protect lawful expression and keep order, not give in to the heckler.
