1 · Administration as the machinery of the State#

  • Administration is the machinery of the State: how the State implements its policies, laws and schemes on the ground.
  • When the EIC changed from a trading company into a Company State (the Charter Act 1833 ended its commercial function; the slide dates the Company State 1765-1858), it had to build this machinery from scratch. More accurately, it had to decide how much of the existing Indian machinery to keep and how much to replace.
  • The chapter covers three pillars: Judiciary, Civil Services and Police.
Teacher's board

This links to his L, E, J shorthand (legislative, executive, judicial power) from Chapter 8 and Chapter 10: the Company State had taken the territory; this chapter is about how it handled the people.

2 · Judiciary#

2.1 Why judicial reform was so complicated

The EIC was not walking into a blank slate. It entered a society with established legal traditions, different religious communities and a functioning (though imperfect) court system. It had to answer six questions:

2.2 The Mughal judicial system in Bengal (before 1772)

The Mughal system in Bengal had two separate hierarchies, one for civil and one for criminal matters.

Civil matters (Diwani = finance, land revenue and civil justice):

  • Courts ran from the local level up to the Sadar Diwani Adalat (SDA) at the suba (provincial) level.
  • In inter-personal matters (marriage, inheritance) the personal laws of Hindus and Muslims applied. Pandits judged Hindu cases and Maulvis judged Muslim cases. At the top the Diwan served as Chief Justice of the SDA.
  • For land revenue and non-personal civil disputes, the original jurisdiction lay with the Zamindar at the local level, in his revenue court, the Kachari.
  • Appeals went up: Zamindar, then Amil (the provincial revenue officer at district level), then the SDA at the provincial capital (Murshidabad), presided over by the Diwan or Naib Diwan.
  • Around this time Muhammad Reza Khan, the Naib Nazim, held the posts of Chief Justice of both the SDA and the Sadar Nizamat Adalat.
Note

"Amil" versus "Amalguzar." The handout uses Amil only because it is a snapshot of the corrupted, mutated system of 18th-century Bengal; the canonical Mughal-administrative term is Amalguzar. The teacher's general board (slide 5) writes Amalguzar = revenue officer at district level; his Bengal board (slide 6) writes Amil (Rev officer). Same post.

Criminal matters:

  • Nizamat Adalats (commonly called Faujdari Adalats) sat at the district level, with the Sadar Nizamat Adalat (SNA) at the top.
  • The Mughal State was an Islamic state, so Islamic law governed criminal proceedings, applied uniformly regardless of religion.
  • At the village level the Zamindar held petty criminal jurisdiction alongside his police duties. Major crimes bypassed the Zamindar and went straight to the district Faujdari Adalat.
  • Strict division of labour: the Kazis were the actual judges who pronounced verdicts (the board calls it a "2 People Bench," Kazi with Mufti), the Muftis gave expert interpretation of Islamic law, and the district Faujdar (executive police chief) secured the accused and physically executed the sentence. The Naib Nazim headed the SNA.
Teacher's board

Civil courts were religion-specific (your judge depended on your faith); criminal courts were State-uniform (the law of the ruling State applied to everyone). On the board: civil has two tracks under the SDA: the personal-law track (Diwani Adalat, Pandits and Maulvis) and the LR / non-personal track (Amalguzar, then the Zamindar's Kachari). Criminal has one track, with "Law = Islamic Law."

For Bengal before 1772, use Naib Nazim for the SNA, and for the SDA the handout says Diwan (the board adds that Reza Khan, the Naib Nazim, held both chief justiceships, having been made the EIC's Diwan in 1765).

Teacher's board

Teacher's board

2.3 The Dual Government period (1765-1772)

  • After the Treaty of Allahabad 1765 the EIC gained the Diwani rights over Bengal: revenue administration, Treasury and control of property / civil courts. (Modern welfare duties such as health and education were not included: they were traditionally handled by private or religious patronage, not by the Mughal government.)
  • The Nawab retained the Nizamat (law and order). This created the Dual Government.
  • Guided by Orientalism ("rule India the Indian way"), the EIC did not rush to impose its own system. Muhammad Reza Khan continued to head both the Diwani (on the EIC's behalf) and the Nizamat (on the Nawab's behalf). Indian officials remained the effective decision-makers on the ground. The EIC was mainly concerned with collecting land revenue.
  • Outcome: revenue collection was poor; law and order deteriorated, made catastrophic by the Great Bengal Famine of 1770, which killed roughly one-third of Bengal's population.
Note

There is no ink on slide 11, but its typed outcome row carries a starred note: "Poverty leads to Crimes". Hunger pushed people into crime, so law and order failed because of the famine, and the famine was worsened by a hands-off administration. (For the Dual Government itself, Diwani versus Nizamat and Reza Khan as the EIC's Diwan, see Chapter 10.)

Memory hook

One man, two hats, two masters.

Decode: Reza Khan headed the Diwani (for the EIC) and the Nizamat (for the Nawab) during 1765-72; Orientalism meant Indian officials stayed in charge; the EIC only wanted revenue; the result was poor revenue and a collapse of law and order, then the 1770 famine (one-third of Bengal).

Why it sticks: one Indian official was carrying both the Company's and the Nawab's bag.

2.4 Direct Government (1772) and the judicial plan of Hastings 73-85

In 1772 Hastings 73-85 (Governor from 1772, GG of Bengal 1773-1785) ended the Dual Government and took direct administration of both the Diwani and the Nizamat.

  • Rationale: EIC administration, based on rule of law and equality before law, would be fairer and more efficient and would firmly establish British authority. (The ideology shifts slightly from pure Orientalism to a hybrid: rule more directly, but still use Indian legal frameworks.)

#### 2.4.1 The changes of 1773

Two further adjustments:

  1. A proper attempt at separation of revenue and judicial powers: an officer serving as DC was not to serve as judge too. In practice the overlap remained, but the principle was established.
  2. To bring justice physically closer to the people, the district-level Diwani Adalats were replaced by 18 Mofussil courts, each headed by a European judge. (Mofussil = rural areas and countryside districts below the district headquarters.)
Teacher's board

"PROPER SoP @ DC by CW 86-93." The handout calls 1773 a "proper attempt" at separation; the teacher corrects the weight of the word: the real, proper Separation of Powers at the level of the DC was done by Cornwallis 86-93 in 1793. In 1773 it was only the principle. Expect the exam to attach the DC's loss of judicial power to 1793 and Cornwallis 86-93 (this is Prelims 2010).

Handout alert

1773 versus 1793, do not mix. 1773 (Hastings 73-85): separation attempted, overlap remained, 18 Mofussil courts. 1793 (Cornwallis 86-93): the DC is stripped of all judicial powers, the 18 Mofussil courts are abolished and the district Diwani Adalats come back (the teacher's slide 19 note: "Mofussil courts ended, DA back").

#### 2.4.2 The Supreme Court (1774)

  • The Regulating Act 1773 led to a Supreme Court in Calcutta in 1774, specifically to hear cases involving British subjects in Bengal, under British law. (British people, British judges, British law: a completely separate judicial track.)
  • Supreme Courts followed in Madras (1800) and Bombay (1823). The Supreme Court was entirely separate from the Indian court hierarchy.
Teacher's board

The 1774 picture on one board: SDA headed by the GG; SNA headed by Naib Nazim Md Reza Khan (reinstated 1774); Mofussil courts with a European judge assisted by Pandits and Maulvis; Nizamat courts with Kazi and Mufti under the European DC's supervision. The Supreme Court sits outside this ladder, only for Britishers.

#### 2.4.3 Codification of laws

  • 1775: a committee of 11 Pandits compiled Hindu law into a single coherent code so courts could interpret it uniformly. The English translation was published as the Gentoo Code (1776). ("Gentoo" comes from the Portuguese "Gentios," a derogatory term for Hindus, used because Hindus worshipped multiple Gods unlike monotheistic Christians.)
  • 1778: a similar effort for Islamic law. Because of Mughal rule, Islamic law was already well organised, so the EIC simply translated the most authoritative existing Arabic legal manual, the Al-Hidayah, first into Persian by Indian scholars and then into English as The Hedaya (1791).
  • The Code of 1781 made written orders compulsory and prescribed rules for court functioning.
Teacher's board

"WRITTEN LAW": this answers question 2 of slide 4 (written or discretion?). "ENGLISH WRITTEN": the Gentoo Code and the Hedaya are both the English written versions of Indian law, so British judges could use them. The Code of 1781 is boxed "in Bengal": it applied only in Bengal (see slide 22 below).

Memory hook

75-76, 78-91, 81: the three codes.

Decode: 1775 11 Pandits compile Hindu law, 1776 English Gentoo Code; 1778 Islamic law effort, Arabic Al-Hidayah to Persian to English, 1791 The Hedaya; 1781 Code: written orders compulsory (in Bengal). Path of the Hedaya: Arabic, Persian, English: A-P-E.

Why it sticks: the years climb 75, 76, 78, 81, and only the Hedaya (91) comes late.

2.5 The Cornwallis 86-93 Code of 1793 (judiciary)

Cornwallis 86-93 (GG 1786-1793) introduced the most systematic judicial reform of the early period. He is rightly called the father of civil services, but his influence on the judiciary was equally significant.

#### 2.5.1 Why did Cornwallis 86-93 reform the judiciary?

  • He had just introduced the Permanent Settlement of 1793 (PS1793), creating a new class of large zamindars as private property owners.
  • For this to work, zamindars needed secure property rights: courts they could trust, and revenue officials who could not harass them. An officer who collected your tax should not also be the judge hearing your appeal against his own actions.
  • So Cornwallis 86-93 properly implemented Separation of Powers at the level of the DC: the District Collector was stripped of all judicial powers and confined to revenue collection and administration.
Teacher's board

"Imagine": PS1793 has a sunset clause (on his board, applying it lands the zamindar's estate up for auction). Suppose the DC applies the sunset clause on a zamindar (Z) and his land goes up for auction. The zamindar files a case in the Diwani Adalat. What if the judge is the same DC? Then there is a conflict of interest, and security of property falls for the zamindar (he draws a sad face). That is why the DC could no longer be a judge. Beside it, in yellow: Cornwallis 86-93 = AUTHORITARIAN, therefore he removed Indians from all senior positions (the other side of the same reform).

Memory hook

Land-lords need Law-lords: why the DC lost the gavel.

Decode: PS1793 made zamindars owners with a sunset clause; a DC who auctions an estate and then judges the owner's appeal has a conflict of interest; so Cornwallis 86-93 made the DC revenue-only (separation of powers, Prelims 2010 answer (c)).

Why it sticks: the new landlords wanted a different man in the judge's chair.

2.6 Judicial system in Ryotwari areas

The Bengal model of separated judicial and revenue functions worked in a zamindari system. In Ryotwari areas, where the ryot is recognised as the direct landowner, conditions were very different:

  • A very large number of small landowners meant a very large number of land-related disputes.
  • District courts were physically distant for poor ryots in villages.
  • Madras, 1814: the Bengal system was replaced on Munro's advice. Separation of judicial and revenue powers was ended. The European DC and his subordinates were given judicial powers so disputes could be resolved at the ground level. (Less paperwork, less travel, faster justice, at least in theory.)
  • This led to greater Indianisation at the lower judiciary, because the DC's subordinates included Indian officials.
  • The downside: enormous concentration of power in one official. The DC now collected taxes, maintained order and judged disputes, including disputes arising from his own tax collection decisions.
  • Bombay, 1819: the same Ryotwari system was applied by Elphinstone, a follower of Munro's philosophy.

Memory hook

Decode: Naib Nazim (head), Faujdar (rural Sarkars), Kotwal (towns), Zamindar (villages, pays the Village Watchmen).

Why it sticks: four letters, four levels, from province down to village.

4.2 Warren Hastings 73-85's reforms of 1781

  • The Great Bengal Famine of 1770 had led to a sharp increase in crime.
  • Hastings 73-85 replaced Faujdars with English Magistrates (DC/DM) in rural districts. Zamindars kept their police duties under the DM's supervision.
  • The problem: very few DMs, and poor supervision of zamindars, who continued to oppress the masses freely.

4.3 The Daroga system of Cornwallis 86-93 (1793)

  • Ended the police powers of zamindars: under PS1793 zamindars were to be agrarian capitalists, not local police authorities.
  • Divided each district into thanas of 30 square miles each. Each thana was under a new officer, the Daroga (Indian), appointed and supervised by the DM.
  • The English District Magistrate (DM) was made the absolute, supreme head of law and order within his district. Rather than an intermediary like an English Naib Nazim or an Inspector General, Cornwallis 86-93 centralised ultimate oversight of criminal and police administration in the GG and his Council. Chain of command: Darogas, then DM, then the central government.

#### 4.3.1 DC and DM: two roles, usually one person

In most of British India (and in India today) the District Collector and District Magistrate are the same person holding two roles. Cornwallis 86-93 separated the judicial function from this officer (the DC/DM lost the power to be a judge), but the revenue and law-and-order functions stayed combined:

HatRoleMughal ancestor
DC (revenue)Collects land revenue, manages government land, maintains land records, collects various taxesThe Mughal Diwani / Amalguzar function
DM (law and order)Heads the district's criminal administration; coordinates with the SP to keep peace, prevent riots and issue executive orders such as Section 144 of the CrPCThe Mughal Nizamat / Faujdari function

#### 4.3.2 Result: the Daroga-Zamindar nexus

  • A Daroga-Zamindar nexus quickly developed: zamindars bribed Darogas and kept private armed men (lathiyals). The nexus became the primary instrument of oppression of the rural poor in the early 1800s. The British blamed "Indian corruption" rather than the structural weaknesses of the system.
  • 1799: Wellesley 98-05 restored police powers to zamindars, in effect admitting the Daroga system had not worked.
  • 1802: the Daroga system was extended to Madras but was later abolished there too because of its failures. The resulting atrocities of revenue officials were exposed by the Madras Torture Commission report of 1855, which led to corrective measures.

4.4 The Police Act 1861

The Daroga system had failed. The British looked for a proven alternative and found it in the Irish Constabulary model: a centralised, hierarchical, quasi-military police force, effective in Ireland since 1787, designed as much for suppressing political agitation as for preventing ordinary crime. (The British were not primarily building a crime-prevention force; they were building a political control mechanism.)

5 · The three-pillar administrative legacy (handout only, no class notes)#

The EIC's administrative evolution across judiciary, civil services and police shows a consistent pattern: structural improvement followed political convenience, not principle.

  • Each major reform was triggered by a crisis (the Great Bengal Famine 1770, the 1857 revolt) or was a concession wrested by the Indian National Movement after sustained pressure.
  • The British never voluntarily Indianised their administration beyond what was economically necessary (to reduce costs) or politically unavoidable (to manage nationalist pressure).
  • They left behind, however inadvertently, a set of institutions that independent India would inherit, adapt and build upon: codified law, an organised civil service, a hierarchical police.

  5. 1772 Hastings 73-85: DC civil judge with Pandits and Maulvis; Kazi and Mufti under DC supervision (law and order too sensitive); SDA and SNA under Governor and Council; Reza Khan jailed 1772-74, reinstated at the SNA.
  6. 1773: proper separation attempted, 18 Mofussil courts. 1774: Supreme Court at Calcutta (Regulating Act 1773), British subjects, British law; Madras 1800, Bombay 1823.
  7. Codification: 1775 11 Pandits, Gentoo Code 1776 (from "Gentios"); 1778 Al-Hidayah to Persian to English, Hedaya 1791; Code of 1781 (written orders, Bengal, by the GG so changeable).
  8. Cornwallis 86-93, Code 1793: PS1793 zamindars need trusted courts; DC stripped of judicial power (Prelims 2010 (c)); Mofussil courts replaced by district Diwani Adalats and City Courts; four divisions Patna, Murshidabad, Calcutta, Dacca; Provincial Courts of Appeal; SDA to Calcutta; Charter Act 1793 (Parliament) codified regulations in vernaculars.
  9. Criminal 1793: district Nizamat Adalats abolished; four Courts of Circuit (rotating); SNA on top; Anglo-Muhammadan law (intent not weapon, no diyat, no mutilation: 7 to 14 years) until IPC 1860; Naib Nazim abolished; Kazis and Muftis assistants; Indians out of senior posts; lawyers in.
  10. Litigation up after 1793: DC lost judicial power, court fees abolished (back 1795), Vakils and a rigid appeal ladder.
  11. Ryotwari: Madras 1814 (Munro) and Bombay 1819 (Elphinstone) merge the DC's roles (many small landowners); Indianisation below; concentration of power; Madras Torture Commission 1855.
  12. Charter Act 1833: GG single legislature, one law for all, Law Member Macaulay, Law Commission 1834; CPC 1859, IPC 1860, CrPC 1861; lower judicial posts to Indians. Bentinck 28-35: abolished Provincial Courts and Courts of Circuit (parking grounds, expense), Persian replaced 1835 (Act XXIX 1837), NWP Adalats (1801, 1836).
  13. Indian High Courts Act 1861: High Courts 1862 (Calcutta merged SDA, SNA and Supreme Court); GOI Act 1935: Federal Court 1937, ancestor of the Supreme Court (1950).
  14. Civil services: patronage and purchase; training on the job, Fort William 1800-06 (Wellesley 98-05), Haileybury 1806-58, universities from 1858. Cornwallis 86-93: CCS (Group A, covenant, Europeans) and UCS (Group B, local hire); pay up, private trade curbed, seniority; no merit entry until 1853 (Prelims 2010 (c)).
  15. Charter Act 1833: UCS to Indians; Bentinck 28-35 some senior posts. Charter Act 1853: exam, Indians eligible; 1854 Macaulay's committee; first exam 1855 in London; ages 23, 22, 21, 19; English Literature, Brown Sahibs; Satyendranath Tagore 1864, four more 1867.
  16. Indianisation: WEMC from 1860 wants simultaneous exam and higher age; Statutory Civil Services Act (Lytton 76-80, lateral entry for loyalists, 1878 handout, 1879 slide); Aitchison 1886-87 (Dufferin 84-88), executive order 1892 (Lansdowne 88-94): ICS, PCS, Subordinate; Islington 1912-17: 25%, no simultaneous exam; GOI Act 1919: exam in India 1922, PSC 1926, 33%; Lee 1923-24: 20-40-40 and 50:50; GOI Act 1935: FPSC 1937, over 50% Indianised by 1947, UPSC under Article 378 (26 January 1950); Articles 315-316, 312.
  17. Police: Mughal Naib Nazim, Faujdar, Kotwal, Zamindar; 1781 Hastings 73-85 English Magistrates; 1793 Daroga (thana of 30 square miles, DM supreme), Daroga-Zamindar nexus, Wellesley 98-05 restores zamindar police 1799, Madras 1802; Irish Constabulary road-test Sindh 1843, Punjab 1849, Bombay 1853, Madras 1859; Police Act March 1861 (IG, SP, SI); Napier did not head the 1860 commission (M.H. Court); 1893 first exam; Fraser 1902-03; 1920 Indians; 1948 IPS; AIS Act 1951.
