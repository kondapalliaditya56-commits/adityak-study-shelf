The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: The EIC kept the Mughal frame for courts, services and police until a crisis (the 1770 famine) forced it to take control; Cornwallis 86-93 then built the structure, and Indians were let in only as far as cost or pressure from the Indian movement forced, leaving independent India with codified law, an organised civil service and a hierarchical police.

  1. A trading company must run a state. Administration is the machinery by which a State carries out its policies, laws and schemes on the ground. When the EIC turned from a trading company into a Company State (1765-1858; the Charter Act 1833 ended its commercial function) it had to decide how much Indian machinery to keep and how much to replace. Three pillars carry the story: judiciary, civil services and police. The teacher calls the whole chapter the history of Public Administration in British India.
    → First, what court system did the EIC inherit?
  2. The Mughal ladders of Bengal. Before 1772 Bengal had two separate hierarchies. In civil matters (Diwani) courts ran up to the Sadar Diwani Adalat, with Pandits and Maulvis judging personal law and the Zamindar's Kachari handling land revenue disputes. In criminal matters (Nizamat) Islamic law applied to everyone: the Kazi judged, the Mufti interpreted and the Faujdar, like today's SP, caught the accused and carried out the sentence.
    → Then the EIC took over the revenue, but not the rest.
  3. One man, two hats. After the Treaty of Allahabad 1765 the EIC held the Diwani and the Nawab kept the Nizamat: a Dual Government. Guided by Orientalism, the EIC let Muhammad Reza Khan head both and cared mainly about revenue. Revenue fell, law and order broke down, and the Great Bengal Famine of 1770 killed about one-third of Bengal.
    → So the EIC decided to rule directly.
  4. Hastings 73-85 steps in. In 1772 Hastings 73-85 ended the Dual Government. The British DC sat as judge in the civil courts, while in criminal courts the Kazi and Mufti stayed as judges under the DC's watch, because law and order was too sensitive to replace. The Regulating Act 1773 led to the Supreme Court at Calcutta in 1774 for British subjects under British law, with Madras (1800) and Bombay (1823) to follow. Laws were written down too: the Gentoo Code (1776), the Hedaya (1791) and the Code of 1781.
    → But the DC was still both collector and judge.
  5. Cornwallis 86-93's Code of 1793. With the Permanent Settlement of 1793, zamindars became property owners and needed courts they could trust: if the DC auctioned an estate and then judged the owner's appeal, there was a conflict of interest. Cornwallis 86-93 stripped the DC of all judicial power (the Prelims 2010 answer), divided Bengal into four divisions with Provincial Courts of Appeal and Courts of Circuit, created Anglo-Muhammadan law, removed Indians from all senior posts and permitted lawyers.
    → Yet the new system clogged, and the model did not fit everywhere.
  6. Ryotwari, Bentinck 28-35 and the codes. Courts clogged after 1793: the DC could no longer settle disputes on the spot, court fees were abolished (back in 1795), and lawyers prolonged cases. In Ryotwari areas, with many small landowners, Madras (1814, on Munro's advice) and Bombay (1819, Elphinstone) merged the DC's roles again. Bentinck 28-35 abolished the divisional courts, replaced Persian in 1835, and the Charter Act 1833's Law Commission under Macaulay led to the CPC 1859, IPC 1860 and CrPC 1861. High Courts came in 1862 and the Federal Court in 1937.
    → Now the people who staffed the courts.
  7. From patronage to a service. A service means a career ladder with rules, not total discretion. Early EIC officials came by nomination and patronage, and Directors lobbied Parliamentarians with jobs for their sons. Cornwallis 86-93 organised them into the Covenanted Civil Service (senior, signed a covenant in London, Europeans only, like Group A) and the Uncovenanted (junior, hired locally, where Indians served, like Group B), with higher pay, curbed private trade and strict seniority; but entry stayed by nomination until the Charter Act 1853.
    → Then came the exam, with three locks on the door.
  8. The exam and its three locks. The Charter Act 1853 mandated an exam for the CCS and the first exam was held in London in 1855. Indians were eligible on paper, but the age limit was kept low, the syllabus was dominated by English Literature (to produce Macaulay's Brown Sahibs) and the venue was London. Satyendranath Tagore became the first Indian to clear it in 1864 and four more followed in 1867.
    → The western-educated Indians began to push back.
  9. The long fight for Indianisation. From 1860 the WEMC demanded a simultaneous exam in India and a higher age limit. The British answered with Lytton 76-80's lateral entry for loyal elite Indians (a Statutory Civil Services Act the teacher calls unimportant). Aitchison 1887 led to the ICS, PCS and Subordinate services in 1892 by executive order under Lansdowne 88-94; Islington (1912, report 1917) offered 25% and refused a simultaneous exam; the GOI Act 1919 brought the exam to India in 1922, the PSC in 1926 and a 33% target; Lee (1923-24) set 20-40-40; the GOI Act 1935 gave the Federal PSC (1937), over 50% were Indianised by 1947, and Article 378 made it the UPSC in 1950.
    → The third pillar, the police, followed its own path.
  10. From Faujdar to Daroga. Under the Mughals the chain ran Naib Nazim, Faujdar, Kotwal, Zamindar. After the 1770 famine raised crime, Hastings 73-85 put English Magistrates in place of the Faujdars in 1781. Cornwallis 86-93 ended the zamindars' police powers in 1793: thanas of 30 square miles under an Indian Daroga, the DM supreme in his district. But a Daroga-Zamindar nexus of bribes and lathiyals oppressed the rural poor; Wellesley 98-05 restored the zamindars' powers in 1799, and the Madras trial of 1802 ended in the Torture Commission report of 1855.
    → The British then went looking for a proven model.
  11. The Police Act 1861. The British turned to the Irish Constabulary model, a centralised, quasi-military force built as much for political control as for crime. It was road-tested in Sindh (1843, Charles Napier), Punjab (1849), Bombay (1853) and Madras (1859); M.H. Court's 1st Police Commission (1860), set up by Canning 56-62, borrowed from Sindh, and Napier did not head it. The Police Act (March 1861) set up the IG, SP and SI hierarchy; Fraser's commission (1902-03) created the Imperial Police cadre; the IPS came in 1948 and the AIS Act in 1951.
    → What did all three pillars leave behind?
  12. Crisis or pressure. Each major reform followed a crisis (the 1770 famine, the 1857 revolt) or a concession won by the Indian National Movement. The British Indianised only as far as cost or politics demanded. What they left behind, however inadvertently, was codified law, an organised civil service and a hierarchical police, which independent India inherited and adapted.

2.2 The Mughal judicial system in Bengal (before 1772)

  • The Mughal system in Bengal had two separate hierarchies, one for civil and one for criminal matters.
  • Civil matters (Diwani = finance, land revenue and civil justice):
  • Courts ran from the local level up to the Sadar Diwani Adalat (SDA) at the suba (provincial) level.
  • In inter-personal matters (marriage, inheritance) the personal laws of Hindus and Muslims applied.
  • Pandits judged Hindu cases and Maulvis judged Muslim cases.
  • At the top the Diwan served as Chief Justice of the SDA.
  • For land revenue and non-personal civil disputes, the original jurisdiction lay with the Zamindar at the local level, in his revenue court, the Kachari.
  • Appeals went up: Zamindar, then Amil (the provincial revenue officer at district level), then the SDA at the provincial capital (Murshidabad), presided over by the Diwan or Naib Diwan.
  • Around this time Muhammad Reza Khan, the Naib Nazim, held the posts of Chief Justice of both the SDA and the Sadar Nizamat Adalat.

  • the canonical Mughal-administrative term is Amalguzar.
  • The teacher's general board (slide 5) writes Amalguzar = revenue officer at district level
  • his Bengal board (slide 6) writes Amil (Rev officer). Same post

Criminal matters:

  • Nizamat Adalats (commonly called Faujdari Adalats) sat at the district level, with the Sadar Nizamat Adalat (SNA) at the top.
  • The Mughal State was an Islamic state, so Islamic law governed criminal proceedings, applied uniformly regardless of religion.
  • At the village level the Zamindar held petty criminal jurisdiction alongside his police duties.
  • Major crimes bypassed the Zamindar and went straight to the district Faujdari Adalat.
  • Strict division of labour: the Kazis were the actual judges who pronounced verdicts (the board calls it a "2 People Bench," Kazi with Mufti), the Muftis gave expert interpretation of Islamic law, and the district Faujdar (executive police chief) secured the accused and physically executed the sentence.
  • The Naib Nazim headed the SNA.

2.3 The Dual Government period (1765-1772)

  • After the Treaty of Allahabad 1765 the EIC gained the Diwani rights over Bengal: revenue administration, Treasury and control of property / civil courts.
  • (Modern welfare duties such as health and education were not included: they were traditionally handled by private or religious patronage, not by the Mughal government.)
  • The Nawab retained the Nizamat (law and order). This created the Dual Government.
  • Guided by Orientalism ("rule India the Indian way"), the EIC did not rush to impose its own system.
  • Muhammad Reza Khan continued to head both the Diwani (on the EIC's behalf) and the Nizamat (on the Nawab's behalf).
  • Indian officials remained the effective decision-makers on the ground.
  • The EIC was mainly concerned with collecting land revenue.
  • Outcome:
    • Revenue collection was poor
    • Law and order deteriorated, made catastrophic by the Great Bengal Famine of 1770, which killed roughly one-third of Bengal's population.

  • There is no ink on slide 11, but its typed outcome row carries a starred note: "Poverty leads to Crimes".
  • Hunger pushed people into crime, so law and order failed because of the famine, and the famine was worsened by a hands-off administration.
  • (For the Dual Government itself, Diwani versus Nizamat and Reza Khan as the EIC's Diwan, see Chapter 10.)

2.4 Direct Government (1772) and the judicial plan of Hastings 73-85

  • In 1772 Hastings 73-85 (Governor from 1772, GG of Bengal 1773-1785) ended the Dual Government and took direct administration of both the Diwani and the Nizamat.
  • Rationale: EIC administration, based on rule of law and equality before law, would be fairer and more efficient and would firmly establish British authority.
  • (The ideology shifts slightly from pure Orientalism to a hybrid: rule more directly, but still use Indian legal frameworks.)

2.4.1 The changes of 1773

  • Two further adjustments:
  • 1. A proper attempt at separation of revenue and judicial powers: an officer serving as DC was not to serve as judge too.
  • In practice the overlap remained, but the principle was established.
  • 2. To bring justice physically closer to the people, the district-level Diwani Adalats were replaced by 18 Mofussil courts, each headed by a European judge.
  • (Mofussil = rural areas and countryside districts below the district headquarters.)
Teacher's board
  • "PROPER SoP @ DC by CW 86-93." The handout calls 1773 a "proper attempt" at separation
  • The teacher corrects the weight of the word: the real, proper Separation of Powers at the level of the DC was done by Cornwallis 86-93 in 1793.
  • In 1773 it was only the principle.
  • Expect the exam to attach the DC's loss of judicial power to 1793 and Cornwallis 86-93 (this is Prelims 2010).

2.4.2 The Supreme Court (1774)

2.5 The Cornwallis 86-93 Code of 1793 (judiciary)

  • Cornwallis 86-93 (GG 1786-1793) introduced the most systematic judicial reform of the early period.
  • He is rightly called the father of civil services, but his influence on the judiciary was equally significant.

2.5.1 Why did Cornwallis 86-93 reform the judiciary?

  • He had just introduced the Permanent Settlement of 1793 (PS1793), creating a new class of large zamindars as private property owners.
  • For this to work, zamindars needed secure property rights: courts they could trust, and revenue officials who could not harass them.
  • An officer who collected your tax should not also be the judge hearing your appeal against his own actions.
  • So Cornwallis 86-93 properly implemented Separation of Powers at the level of the DC: the District Collector was stripped of all judicial powers and confined to revenue collection and administration.

2.6 Judicial system in Ryotwari areas

  • The Bengal model of separated judicial and revenue functions worked in a zamindari system.
  • In Ryotwari areas, where the ryot is recognised as the direct landowner, conditions were very different:
  • A very large number of small landowners meant a very large number of land-related disputes.
  • District courts were physically distant for poor ryots in villages.
  • Madras, 1814: the Bengal system was replaced on Munro's advice.
  • Separation of judicial and revenue powers was ended.
  • The European DC and his subordinates were given judicial powers so disputes could be resolved at the ground level.
  • (Less paperwork, less travel, faster justice, at least in theory.)
  • This led to greater Indianisation at the lower judiciary, because the DC's subordinates included Indian officials.
  • The downside: enormous concentration of power in one official.
  • The DC now collected taxes, maintained order and judged disputes, including disputes arising from his own tax collection decisions.
  • Bombay, 1819: the same Ryotwari system was applied by Elphinstone, a follower of Munro's philosophy.

2.9 Indian High Courts Act 1861 and 2.10 the Federal Court (GOI Act 1935)

  • The Indian High Courts Act 1861 led to High Courts in Madras, Bombay and Calcutta in 1862.
  • The Calcutta High Court was created by merging three existing courts: the Sadar Diwani Adalat, the Sadar Nizamat Adalat and the Supreme Court at Calcutta.
  • This finally ended the dual-track system of separate courts for Indians and the British.
  • The GOI Act 1935, implemented from 1937, established a Federal Court, the direct ancestor of today's Supreme Court of India.
  • It dealt with inter-governmental disputes (Centre versus Province, Province versus Province) and heard appeals from High Courts.
  • Today's Supreme Court, set up under the Constitution of India 1950, continues this role with expanded jurisdiction.

4.1 The inherited system (pre-1781)

  • The EIC treated law and order as extremely sensitive, so the Mughal system of policing was left untouched for the longest time. Under it in Bengal:
LevelPolice authority
Overall headNaib Nazim (Deputy Subadar)
Rural districts (Sarkars)Faujdars (Sarkar here means a sub-divisional unit, not government; the meaning differs by region)
Towns and urban areasKotwals
VillagesZamindars held police duties and paid Village Watchmen from their own resources

4.2 Warren Hastings 73-85's reforms of 1781

  • The Great Bengal Famine of 1770 had led to a sharp increase in crime.
  • Hastings 73-85 replaced Faujdars with English Magistrates (DC/DM) in rural districts.
  • Zamindars kept their police duties under the DM's supervision.
  • The problem: very few DMs, and poor supervision of zamindars, who continued to oppress the masses freely.

4.3 The Daroga system of Cornwallis 86-93 (1793)

  • Ended the police powers of zamindars: under PS1793 zamindars were to be agrarian capitalists, not local police authorities.
  • Divided each district into thanas of 30 square miles each.
  • Each thana was under a new officer, the Daroga (Indian), appointed and supervised by the DM.
  • The English District Magistrate (DM) was made the absolute, supreme head of law and order within his district.
  • Rather than an intermediary like an English Naib Nazim or an Inspector General, Cornwallis 86-93 centralised ultimate oversight of criminal and police administration in the GG and his Council.
  • Chain of command: Darogas, then DM, then the central government.

4.3.1 DC and DM: two roles, usually one person

  • In most of British India (and in India today) the District Collector and District Magistrate are the same person holding two roles.
  • Cornwallis 86-93 separated the judicial function from this officer (the DC/DM lost the power to be a judge), but the revenue and law-and-order functions stayed combined:
HatRoleMughal ancestor
DC (revenue)Collects land revenue, manages government land, maintains land records, collects various taxesThe Mughal Diwani / Amalguzar function
DM (law and order)Heads the district's criminal administration; coordinates with the SP to keep peace, prevent riots and issue executive orders such as Section 144 of the CrPCThe Mughal Nizamat / Faujdari function

4.3.2 Result: the Daroga-Zamindar nexus

  • A Daroga-Zamindar nexus quickly developed: zamindars bribed Darogas and kept private armed men (lathiyals).
  • The nexus became the primary instrument of oppression of the rural poor in the early 1800s.
  • The British blamed "Indian corruption" rather than the structural weaknesses of the system.
  • 1799: Wellesley 98-05 restored police powers to zamindars, in effect admitting the Daroga system had not worked.
  • 1802: the Daroga system was extended to Madras but was later abolished there too because of its failures.
  • The resulting atrocities of revenue officials were exposed by the Madras Torture Commission report of 1855, which led to corrective measures.

4.4 The Police Act 1861

  • The Daroga system had failed. The British looked for a proven alternative and found it in the Irish Constabulary model: a centralised, hierarchical, quasi-military police force, effective in Ireland since 1787, designed as much for suppressing political agitation as for preventing ordinary crime.
  • (The British were not primarily building a crime-prevention force; they were building a political control mechanism.)

4.4.2 The Police Act 1861

  • The Police Act, 1861 was passed in March 1861 by the GG's Council (as last restructured by the Charter Act 1853).
  • (The Indian Councils Act 1861 came in August 1861 and created the Imperial Legislative Council (ILC); do not mix them.) It created the provincial police structure across all provinces: each Province to have its own police organisation, with a clear hierarchy:

Inspector General (IG)The provincial policeThe Governor / Lieutenant Governor (LG)
Superintendent of Police (SP)The district policeThe DC / DM
Sub-Inspector (SI)The thanaThe SP (not to the DC as earlier)
  • Operational accountability ran upward through the police chain: SI, SP, IG.
  • Political accountability ran to civilian authority: SP to DC/DM, IG to LG / Governor.

  • The service did not yet.
  • From 1857 onwards a "Police Raj" was created: Indians were excluded from all senior positions, and the emphasis was on effective policing, intelligence collection and political surveillance.

5 · The three-pillar administrative legacy (handout only, no class notes)#

Story link

Because each pillar moved only when pushed, the handout ends with one pattern. Ask: which two triggers drove reform, and what three legacies were left?

  • The EIC's administrative evolution across judiciary, civil services and police shows a consistent pattern: structural improvement followed political convenience, not principle.
  • Each major reform was triggered by a crisis (the Great Bengal Famine 1770, the 1857 revolt) or was a concession wrested by the Indian National Movement after sustained pressure.
  • The British never voluntarily Indianised their administration beyond what was economically necessary (to reduce costs) or politically unavoidable (to manage nationalist pressure).
  • They left behind, however inadvertently, a set of institutions that independent India would inherit, adapt and build upon: codified law, an organised civil service, a hierarchical police.

Charter Act 1853 and after?Exam mandated, Indians eligible; 1854 Macaulay's committee and Civil Services Commission (London); first exam 1855; 1864 Satyendranath Tagore first Indian; 1867 four more.
How did the exam keep Indians out?Age limit (23 in 1859, 22 in 1860, 21 in 1866, 19 in 1876 implemented 1878), English Literature syllabus (Brown Sahibs), London-only venue with travel cost and weaker preparation.
Statutory Civil Services Act?Lytton 76-80: lateral entry by nomination of loyal elite Indians instead of an exam in India or a higher age; Act 1878 in the handout, 1879 on the slide; the teacher: "unimportant act".
Aitchison Commission?Appointed 1886 by Dufferin 84-88, report 1887: drop Covenanted and Uncovenanted; ICS, PCS, Subordinate; implemented 1892 by executive order under Lansdowne 88-94 (not ICA 1892).
Islington, 1919 and Lee?Islington (1912, report 1917): 25% Indians, no simultaneous exam. GOI Act 1919: exam in India 1922, PSC 1926, 33%. Lee (1923, report 1924): 20% promotion, 40% Indians, 40% Europeans, 50:50 in 15 years.
GOI Act 1935 and the UPSC?Federal PSC 1937 and provincial PSCs; over 50% Indianised by 1947; UPSC under Article 378 on 26 January 1950; Articles 315 and 316 (commissions), 312 (All India Services).
Mughal policing in Bengal?Naib Nazim (head), Faujdars (rural Sarkars), Kotwals (towns), Zamindars (villages, paid Village Watchmen).
Daroga system 1793?Zamindars lose police powers; thanas of 30 square miles, each under an Indian Daroga appointed by the DM; DM supreme; chain Daroga, DM, GG and Council; failed through the Daroga-Zamindar nexus; Wellesley 98-05 restored zamindar police in 1799.
Police Act 1861?Irish Constabulary model road-tested in Sindh 1843 (Napier), Punjab 1849, Bombay 1853, Madras 1859; 1st Police Commission 1860 (M.H. Court, not Napier); Act of March 1861: IG, SP, SI.
How was the police made a service?1893 first exam in London; Fraser Commission 1902-03 (Curzon 99-05): Imperial Police, PPS with DySP, CID, DCI 1903; 1920 IP opened to Indians; 1948 IPS; AIS Act 1951.
