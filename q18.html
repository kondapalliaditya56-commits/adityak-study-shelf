2010PYQ 1(Prelims 2010) — Cornwallis86-93's Separation of Judicial Powers from DC

By a regulation in 1793, the District Collector was deprived of his judicial powers and made the collecting agent only. What was the reason for such regulation?

Answer: (C)

(c) ✓ — Cornwallis was alarmed that one officer combining revenue + judicial powers held absolute power over the very people whose appeals he would hear; the Permanent Settlement of 1793 had just created a new zamindar landowner class who needed independent courts they could trust against revenue officials. Per (Why Did Cornwallis86-93 Reform the Judiciary?), an officer who collected your tax should not also be the judge hearing your appeal against his own actions — so the DC was stripped of all judicial powers & confined to revenue collection.

Why others wrong: (a) revenue-efficiency was a side-benefit, not the principal motivation — frames the change around separation of powers, not collection efficiency; (b) wrong on facts — Cornwallis86-93 in fact removed Indians from ALL senior positions, so the European-judge / Indian-collector framing inverts what he actually did; (d) the "DC lacked legal knowledge" reason is a partial truth at best — Chapter nowhere flags lack of India-knowledge or law-training as the trigger; the trigger was structural conflict of interest post-PS1793.

Memory hook: Tax-man can't be judge of his own tax — PS1793 zamindars needed clean courts, so Cornwallis split the hat.

2010PYQ 2(Prelims 2010) — Cornwallis86-93 as Founder of CCS

Who among the following Governor-Generals created the Covenanted Civil Service of India which later came to be known as the Indian Civil Service?

Answer: (C)

(c) ✓ — Cornwallis86-93 reorganised EIC officers into the two-tier Covenanted Civil Service (CCS, senior, European-only, signed a "covenant" with the Court of Directors before sailing) + Uncovenanted Civil Service (UCS, junior, hired locally). Per, he is "called the founder of civil services in India" because he converted a corrupt, patronage-ridden set of employees into a structured, professionalised service. The CCS was later renamed Indian Civil Service (ICS) in 1892 by executive order under Lansdowne88-94 on the Aitchison Commission 1887 recommendations — NOT by ICA 1892.

Why others wrong: (a) Warren Hastings73-85 ended Dual Government & built the judicial plan of 1772–74, but did not create the CCS structure; (b) Wellesley98-05 set up Fort William College, Calcutta (1800–1806) for CCS training, but the cadre structure pre-dated him by 7 years; (d) Bentinck28-35 opened UCS to Indians under Charter Act 1833 but did not create the CCS — he came 40 years after Cornwallis86-93.

Memory hook: Cornwallis = Covenant — sign the contract in London, sail to India, draw senior pay.

1999PYQ 3(Prelims 1999) — Indian Leader Dismissed from ICS

Which one of the following Indian leaders was dismissed by the British from the Indian Civil Service?

Answer: (B)

(b) ✓ — Surendranath Banerji was among the four Indians who cleared the CCS examination in 1867 and was dismissed from the ICS in 1874 on a technical/age-related ground — a politically motivated removal that turned him into one of the earliest militant Moderates. The wider context — post-1857 British insecurity & determination to keep Indians out of senior positions — is captured in: Post-1857, British administrators were insecure about Indian assertion & determined to keep Indians out of senior positions.

Why others wrong: (a) Satyendranath Tagore — first Indian to clear CCS (1864) — had a full ICS career & was NOT dismissed; (c) R.C. Dutt — cleared ICS in 1869, retired honourably as a Divisional Commissioner; not dismissed; (d) S.C. Bose — resigned in 1921 in protest after clearing the ICS exam in 1920; resignation is not dismissal.

Memory hook: S.N. Banerji = "Surrender Not" — sacked by the British in 1874, the sacking itself made him a nationalist.

2024PYQ 1 Industrial Revolution + Decline of Indian Handicrafts

(Cross-chapter answer required)

Mains 2024 · 15 Marks · 250 words "How far was the Industrial Revolution in England responsible for the decline of handicrafts and cottage industries in India?"

Demand of the question: "How far" calls for a calibrated assessment — Industrial Revolution was a major driver but not the only one. The directive verb is implicit but evaluative — weigh evidence on both sides. ⚠ Cross-chapter content note: This Mains question's substantive content lives outside the EIC Administration chapter. The fullest answer requires:

  • British Imperial Ideology chapter ('Three-Pillar Administrative Legacy' — Charter Act 1813 ended EIC India trade monopoly + Industrial Revolution 1750–1850 created British manufacturer demand for Indian raw materials + export market for finished goods).
  • Land Revenue Systems chapter ('Three-Pillar Administrative Legacy' — Commercialisation of Agriculture: cash revenue forced ryots from food crops to cash crops like cotton/indigo/jute/sugarcane = raw materials for British factories).
  • Moderates / Drain Theory chapter (R.C. Dutt + Dadabhai Naoroji's Drain Theory framework on de-industrialisation as a structural feature of British economic policy).

Suggested intro (~25 words): The Industrial Revolution in England (1750–1850) was a major but not exclusive driver of the decline of Indian handicrafts; British policy choices and global trade restructuring compounded its effects.

Guiding themes (pick 4–5 for a 250-word answer):

1. End of EIC's India trade monopoly (Charter Act 1813) — opened India to British manufactured goods; British factory output flooded Indian markets at prices Indian artisans could not match. (British Imperial Ideology chapter — 'Three New Ideologies (Post 1813): Liberalism + Utilitarianism + Evangelicalism'.)

2. Tariff asymmetry — British goods entered India at low/no tariff; Indian goods faced high tariffs in Britain. (Imperial Preference logic, see British Imperial Ideology chapter — 'Imperial Preference'.)

3. Railway expansion — initially built to move British goods to interior India + raw materials to ports; not to support Indian artisanal value-chains. (See Drain Theory chapter for railways as a Drain instrument.)

4. Commercialisation of agriculture forced shift from food crops to cash crops feeding British factories — undermined the agrarian base that supported handicrafts. (See Land Revenue chapter, 'Three-Pillar Administrative Legacy'.)

5. Loss of patronage — Mughal court patronage that had sustained skilled artisanal traditions collapsed with the Mughal State's legal end in 1858 (see Constitutional History chapter — 'ICA 1892 Summary'.

Suggested conclusion (~25 words): Industrial Revolution was the trigger; British policy choices (tariff asymmetry, end of monopoly, commercialisation, railway logic) were the structural amplifiers — together they produced de-industrialisation.

2022PYQ 2 Sudden Spurt in Famines in Colonial India Since Mid-18th Century

(Cross-chapter answer required)

Mains 2022 · 10 Marks · 150 words "Why was there a sudden spurt in famines in colonial India since the mid-eighteenth century? Give reasons."

Demand of the question: "Why" + "Give reasons" calls for enumeration of causal factors. The temporal anchor is "since mid-18th century" — i.e. starting with the Bengal Famine 1770. ⚠ Cross-chapter content note: This Mains question's substantive content lives in the Land Revenue Systems chapter ('Three-Pillar Administrative Legacy' commercialisation → late-19th-century famines causal chain) and the Drain Theory chapter (Drain of Wealth + Home Charges starving Indian agrarian economy). The EIC Administration chapter only provides the administrative trigger (Bengal Famine 1770 → Hastings73-85's 1772 Direct Government).

Suggested intro (~20 words): Famines spurted from the mid-18th century due to high cash-revenue demands, commercialisation of agriculture, food-security collapse, and inadequate famine-relief administration.

Guiding themes (pick 4 for a 150-word answer):

1. Cash revenue demand (replacing Mughal in-kind grain payment) forced ryots to grow cash crops (indigo/cotton/opium/jute/sugarcane) for British factories instead of food. (See Land Revenue chapter, 'Three-Pillar Administrative Legacy'.)

2. Commercialisation of agriculture reduced food grain reserves — when droughts/floods hit, less cushion. (See Land Revenue chapter, 'Three-Pillar Administrative Legacy'.)

3. Drain of Wealth + Home Charges drained capital from Indian agriculture to Britain. (See Drain Theory chapter — the substantive home for this argument.)

4. Famine relief inadequate — the Bengal Famine 1770 itself triggered Hastings73-85's 1772 Direct Government and the 1781 police reform (chapter 'Warren Hastings73-85's Reforms of 1781'), but later famines (1876–78, 1896–97, 1899–1900, 1943) showed the colonial state's chronic failure to invest in rural relief.

Suggested conclusion (~20 words): Famines spurted because the colonial economic order was structurally extractive; food security was sacrificed to revenue generation and global commodity markets.

2022PYQ 3 Why Did EIC Armies Win Consistently? (Cross-chapter answer

required)

Mains 2022 · 10 Marks · 150 words "Why did the armies of the British East India Company — mostly comprising of Indian soldiers — win consistently against the more numerous and better equipped armies of the then Indian rulers? Give reasons."

Demand of the question: "Why" + "Give reasons" calls for enumeration of causal factors. The framing acknowledges the paradox — Indian soldiers (sepoys) winning against Indian rulers' armies. ⚠ Cross-chapter content note: This Mains question's substantive content lives in the Carnatic Wars chapter (why the EIC won — the institutional answer) and the 1857 Revolt chapter (Sepoy Army composition + grievances). The EIC Administration chapter only provides the administrative-discipline backdrop.

Suggested intro (~20 words): The EIC's armies won consistently due to superior institutional discipline, regular pay, professional training, naval supremacy, and a fragmented Indian political landscape.

Guiding themes (pick 4–5 for a 150-word answer):

1. Regular pay and professional discipline — sepoys received timely salaries and pensions, unlike the irregular pay of Indian rulers' armies. (See Carnatic Wars chapter — why the EIC won.)

2. Modern training + uniform drill based on European military doctrine.

3. Naval supremacy — Royal British Military intervention at Plassey 1757 + October 1857 (see Constitutional History chapter, 'b').

4. Fragmented Indian political landscape — no unified resistance; EIC played rivals against each other (Subsidiary Alliance + Doctrine of Lapse mechanisms).

5. Centralised command structure — the GG-led centralised hierarchy under the Regulating Act 1773 + Pitt's India Act 1784 contrasted with decentralised Indian command (see Constitutional History chapter, 'Judiciary' + 'Civil Services').

Suggested conclusion (~20 words): Discipline + pay + technology + central command + political fragmentation of opponents — institutional rather than racial superiority — explain EIC military success.

2017PYQ 4 Decline of Traditional Artisanal Industry Crippled the Rural

Economy (Cross-chapter answer required)

Mains 2017 · 15 Marks · 250 words "Examine how the decline of traditional artisanal industry in colonial India crippled the rural economy."

Demand of the question: "Examine how" calls for a causal-chain explanation — the directive is to trace the mechanism by which artisanal decline rippled through to the rural economy. ⚠ Cross-chapter content note: This Mains question's substantive content lives in the Drain Theory chapter (R.C. Dutt's Economic History of India + de-industrialisation thesis) and the Land Revenue Systems chapter (commercialisation + ryot indebtedness). The EIC Administration chapter only provides the legal-institutional backdrop (Charter Act 1813 ending EIC monopoly).

Suggested intro (~25 words): The decline of traditional artisanal industry in colonial India crippled the rural economy by stripping it of its non-agricultural income, deepening dependence on land, and accelerating ryot indebtedness.

Guiding themes (pick 4–5 for a 250-word answer):

1. Loss of non-agricultural income — pre-British rural India had a balanced agrarian-artisanal economy; weavers, smiths, potters, dyers supplemented cultivator incomes.

2. Reverse migration to land — displaced artisans crowded back to agriculture, fragmenting holdings + lowering productivity per capita.

3. Ryot indebtedness rose — without artisanal-income cushion, ryots borrowed from moneylenders to meet cash revenue demand → land loss in auctions/mortgage. (See Land Revenue chapter, 'Recruitment — From Patronage to Examination' Phase 1 vicious cycle + 'The Police Act, 1861' Mahalwari distress.)

4. Loss of skill base — multi-generational artisanal knowledge collapsed; village specialisation reduced to subsistence farming.

5. Famine vulnerability increased — economically thinner rural communities had less reserve to weather crop failures (see Drain Theory chapter for full famine-causation argument).

Suggested conclusion (~25 words): Artisanal decline transformed the rural economy from balanced + diversified to monocultural + extractive — the structural origin of present-day rural distress and farm crisis.

2014PYQ 5 Critical Examination of British Economic Policies in India

(Cross-chapter answer required)

Mains 2014 · 10 Marks · 150 words "Examine critically the various facets of economic policies of the British in India from mid-eighteenth century till independence."

Demand of the question: "Examine critically" + "various facets" calls for an evaluative survey across multiple economic policy dimensions over ~200 years. ⚠ Cross-chapter content note: This Mains question's substantive content spans multiple chapters: British Imperial Ideology chapter (Charter 1813 monopoly end + Imperial Preference — 'Imperial Preference') · Land Revenue Systems chapter (PS1793 + Ryotwari + Mahalwari + Commercialisation) · Drain Theory chapter (Drain of Wealth + Home Charges + de-industrialisation) · EIC Administration chapter (this chapter — covers civil services + police as instruments of economic extraction).

Suggested intro (~20 words): British economic policies in India from mid-18th century till independence were structurally extractive — designed to serve British capital, not Indian development.

Guiding themes (pick 4 for a 150-word answer):

1. Land Revenue extraction — Permanent Settlement 1793 + Ryotwari + Mahalwari all squeezed cultivators for cash revenue. (See Land Revenue chapter, 'Judiciary'-4.)

2. De-industrialisation — Charter Act 1813 monopoly end + tariff asymmetry destroyed Indian handicrafts. (See British Imperial Ideology chapter, 'Three-Pillar Administrative Legacy'.)

3. Commercialisation of agriculture — cash crops for British factories displaced food crops, fed late-19th-century famines. (See Land Revenue chapter, 'Three-Pillar Administrative Legacy'.)

4. Drain of Wealth + Home Charges + Imperial Preference — capital flight from India to Britain. (See Drain Theory chapter for full mechanism + British Imperial Ideology chapter — 'Imperial Preference' for Imperial Preference.)

5. Administrative cost — civil services + army salaries + police imposed on Indian taxpayers via increased revenue demands. (See this chapter, 'Civil Services'-4.)

Suggested conclusion (~20 words): British economic policies were not failures of well-meaning reform — they were the deliberate operating logic of an extractive colonial state.

FYQ 1 Trace the evolution of the British civil service in India from the patronage system to open competitive examination.Mains-FYQ · 10 marks · 150 words

Demand: Trace the recruitment shift over time and explain what each phase achieved.

Guiding themes:

1. Cornwallis86-93 set up the covenanted civil service in 1793 — closed to Indians, recruited via Director patronage at Haileybury College ('Cornwallis86-93 — Founder of Civil Services').

2. Charter Act 1833 retained patronage but Bentinck28-35 opened minor judicial posts to Indians — first crack in the racial bar ('Charter Act 1833 and Bentinck28-35 (1828–35)').

3. Charter Act 1853 ended patronage and introduced open competitive examination in 1855 — formally Indians could now compete ('Charter Act 1853 — Exam Opened to Indians').

4. Real Indianisation lagged because exams were held only in London and the upper age limit was 21 — Satyendranath Tagore (1863) and Surendranath Banerjee (1869) were rare successes ('The Fight for Indianisation (1860 Onwards)').

5. Conclusion: the shift from patronage to exam was procedural, not substantive — equality of opportunity stayed paper-deep until the simultaneous-exam reforms of 1922 onwards ('Lee Commission / Royal Commission on Superior Civil Services (1923)').

FYQ 2 Examine the colonial judicial system as an instrument of state power rather than justice.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Assess the judicial reforms across phases, weighing the state-power motive against the justice-delivery motive.

Guiding themes:

1. The Mughal qazi-based system was already in place when the EIC took Diwani in 1765 — early EIC simply slotted itself above it, keeping Hindu and Muslim personal law intact for revenue stability ('The Dual Government Period (1765–1772)').

2. Warren Hastings73-85's Judicial Plan of 1772 set up parallel civil + criminal courts under EIC supervision, but kept Indian law-officers (pandits + maulvis) — appearance of continuity ('Direct Government (1772) and the Judicial Plan of Warren Hastings73-85').

3. Cornwallis86-93's 1793 Code separated revenue and judicial powers, introduced the Sadr Diwani Adalat and Sadr Nizamat Adalat — appeared as justice-delivery reform but ALSO firmly subordinated Indian law-officers to British judges ('The Cornwallis Code of 1793 (Judiciary)').

4. Indian High Courts Act 1861 unified the Supreme Courts and Sadr Adalats into High Courts — a constitutional reorganisation tied to the post-1857 reassertion of state authority, not to enlargement of Indian access ('Indian High Courts Act, 1861').

5. GOI Act 1935 created a Federal Court in 1937 — a court of constitutional appeal that bypassed the Privy Council in some matters, yet the Privy Council in London remained the apex right up to 1949 ('Government of India Act 1935 — Federal Court').

6. Conclusion: each reform doubled as a state-power instrument — separating Indians from law-making power, embedding British procedure as the legitimacy standard, and maintaining London as the final appellate forum. Justice delivery was a by-product, not the design ('Three-Pillar Administrative Legacy').
