1 · Union AND ITS Territory · Linguistic States · Small States · Second SRC#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Polity handout pp. 15-17. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • After 1947 language became the main basis for redrawing states. Know the arguments for and against, the three committees, Andhra and the 1956 Act, then the small-states debate, the Uttar Pradesh case and the case for a Second SRC.

Linguistic Reorganisation OF States

Value addition

After Independence language became the main basis for reorganising states, replacing the colonial emphasis on administrative convenience. 1920s: Congress accepted it in principle (Gandhi reorganised Pradesh Congress Committees on linguistic lines). After 1947: Nehru opposed immediate reorganisation, fearing threats to national unity after Partition.

Against

  • administrative difficulties from numerous linguistic demands
  • national integration: strong linguistic identities weaken common identity
  • secessionism: linguistic nationalism may encourage separatism
  • regionalism: demands for jobs, subsidies, benefits raise regional hostility

In favour

  • administrative efficiency: governance in the people's language
  • education and culture: mother-tongue education
  • democratic participation: easier engagement
  • secular basis: alternative to religion and caste in drawing boundaries
Memory aid · AINR vs AEDS

Against: Administration · Integration · No secession · Regionalism. For: Administration · Education · Democratic participation · Secular basis. (own mnemonic)

Committee / CommissionMajor recommendations
S. K. Dhar Commission (1948)rejected linguistic reorganisation; favoured administrative convenience
JVP Committee (1948)Nehru, Patel, Pattabhi Sitaramayya opposed immediate linguistic reorganisation
Fazl Ali Commission (1953)chair Fazl Ali, with K. M. Panikkar and H. N. Kunzru; accepted language as an important basis subject to national unity, economic viability, administrative efficiency; rejected one state, one language
Memory aid · DJF

Order: Dhar (no) → JVP (not now) → Fazl Ali (yes, with limits). (own mnemonic)

Major developments

  • Andhra State (1953):
    • After the death of Potti Sreeramulu in a hunger strike for a Telugu state
    • The first linguistically reorganised state
  • States Reorganisation Act, 1956: 14 states and 6 Union Territories

Issue OF Small States

Note

Demand for smaller states persists due to administrative convenience, regional identities and development deficits. Demands: Koshal (Odisha), Kodagu (Karnataka), Vidarbha and Konkan (Maharashtra), Bodoland (Assam), Maru Pradesh (Rajasthan), Mithilanchal (Bihar), Gorkhaland (West Bengal).

In favour

  • administrative convenience:
    • Large states have varied terrains and priorities
    • Smaller ones are more homogeneous, so planning is easier
  • economic growth: higher growth in some cases (Punjab, Haryana; Uttarakhand, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh)
  • democratic participation: capital nearer the people, though not always realised

Issues

  • definitional problem: what is small or large (population, area, both)?
  • inter-state disputes: borders and water
  • fragmentation: undoing Sardar Patel's work
  • no guarantee of human development: needs resources, planning, bureaucracy, leadership
  • financial constraints: lower resources, costs of capitals, central dependence
  • alternative: decentralisation via districts, panchayats, urban local bodies
Memory aid · AEG vs DIFNFA

For: Administration · Economy · Grassroots democracy. Against: Definition · Inter-state disputes · Fragmentation · No human-development guarantee · Finance · Alternative (decentralise). (own mnemonic)

Way forward / key point

Democratic decentralisation should be the first line of action, followed by adequate funds and empowerment with functions and functionaries.

Case of Uttar Pradesh

  • Ambedkar was wary of one huge state
  • He proposed dividing UP into three states for administrative convenience, disproportionate influence in national politics and better minority representation
  • recent proposals:
    • Harit Pradesh, Awadh, Purvanchal, Bundelkhand
    • Economic viability questions remain
  • counter:
    • If smaller is the solution, create more districts
    • Empower local bodies first
    • If population is an asset for India, so for UP: greater representation is its democratic right, not disproportion

CASE FOR A Second SRC

Why

  • continuing statehood demands: reorganisation is an evolving exercise, not one-stop
  • changing regional realities: three decades of LPG reforms caused demographic, economic, social, regional imbalances and neglect allegations
  • growing population in certain states
  • objective decision-making: assess on administrative convenience, economic viability, development deficits, cultural identity, national unity, not politics

Way forward

  • Second SRC with rational, scientific, objective criteria
  • economic viability assessment
  • democratic decentralisation (funds, functions, functionaries)
  • address development deficits: packages, autonomous councils, development boards
  • statehood as a last resort, not a panacea
Memory aid · SEDAL

Way forward: Second SRC · Economic viability · Decentralise · Address deficits · Last resort statehood. (own mnemonic)

2 · Demand FOR Delhi Statehood#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Polity handout pp. 17-18 · 2023 Act: verify. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Delhi has an elected government but no control over police, public order and land. Know the constitutional position, the pro and anti arguments, the 2018 and 2023 Supreme Court rulings, the 2023 Act and the way forward.

Constitutional Position

Where Delhi stands

  • national capital, but the elected government lacks full control of police, public order and land
  • 69th Amendment Act, 1991 inserted Article 239AA: Legislative Assembly and Council of Ministers
  • designated National Capital Territory (NCT), administered by a Lieutenant Governor appointed by the President
  • police, public order, land are outside the Assembly's competence
  • Parliament retains power to legislate on State and Concurrent List subjects for Delhi

Arguments

In favour of statehood

  • delay in decision-making:
    • Centre handles police, order, land
    • Elected government and LG share some responsibility, so fragmented responsibility means fragmented accountability
  • population: many full states are smaller than Delhi
  • democratic accountability:
    • The elected government needs adequate powers
    • Greater autonomy reduces LG-government conflict

Against statehood

  • national capital:
    • Sentimental value for all Indians, cannot be the property of one section
    • Houses Parliament, the Supreme Court, foreign missions
  • financial viability:
    • An urban agglomeration, industry shifting out due to pollution norms
    • Infrastructure needs large resources
  • resource dependency: relies on neighbouring states for water, power, resources
  • population argument limits:
    • Many cities exceed small states
    • Should they all be states?
Memory aid · DPD vs NFRP

For: Delay · Population · Democratic accountability. Against: National capital · Finance · Resource dependence · Population argument stretched. (own mnemonic)

Judicial AND Legislative Developments

  1. 2018
    SC judgment: stressed constitutional morality, representative government and collaborative federalism in Delhi's administration
  2. 2023
    SC judgment: the elected Delhi Government has legislative and executive powers over services except police, public order, land
  3. 2023
    GNCTD (Amendment) Act, 2023: statutory framework under which the Union retains significant control over appointments, transfers and postings of officers

Way forward

  • rather than full statehood, focus on institutional clarity: LG in a constitutional/nominal advisory role on non-reserved matters
  • greater administrative autonomy to the elected government over civic governance and public service delivery, while keeping Union control over core strategic functions

3 · Ladakh · Statehood · Sixth Schedule#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Polity handout pp. 18-19 · 2025-26 developments: verify. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Ladakh became a Union Territory without a legislature in 2019. Know its set-up, the grievances, the four-point demand of LAB and KDA, why Sixth Schedule is sought, the Centre's position and a balanced way forward.

Constitutional Position AND Grievances

Position

  • J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019 made Ladakh a separate UT without a legislature on 31 October 2019
  • administration: Lieutenant Governor under Article 239, with AGMUT cadre officers
  • local governance: two Hill Councils, LAHDC Leh and LAHDC Kargil, with limited autonomous powers
  • judiciary: shares the High Court of J&K and Ladakh
  • representation: one Lok Sabha seat, no Legislative Assembly or Chief Minister

Major grievances

  • democratic deficit: no elected Assembly
  • tribal identity: fear over land, employment, resources
  • ecological vulnerability: unregulated tourism, mining, infrastructure threaten glaciers, water, biodiversity of a fragile high-altitude desert

Four-point Demand

Leh Apex Body (LAB)

  • and Kargil Democratic Alliance (KDA) demand: 1) full statehood with a Legislative Assembly
  • 2) Sixth Schedule status
  • 3) a separate Public Service Commission
  • 4) two Lok Sabha seats (Leh and Kargil).
Memory aid · SSPL

Four demands: Statehood · Sixth Schedule · PSC of its own · Lok Sabha seats (2). (own mnemonic)

Sixth Schedule demand

  • Article 244(2): provides Autonomous District and Regional Councils with powers over land, forests, customs, local administration in specified Northeastern tribal areas
  • NCST recommendation (2019): Sixth Schedule protection for Ladakh given its predominantly tribal population
  • significance: tribal self-government, cultural protection, local control over resources and ecological conservation
  • requirement: a constitutional amendment, as coverage is limited to specified Northeastern states

Centre's Position AND WAY Forward

Centre's position and developments

  • strategic concerns: sensitive borders with China and Pakistan
  • alternative safeguards: Article 371-style protections for land, culture, employment discussed
  • MHA High-Powered Committee to negotiate with Ladakhi representatives
  • public mobilisation: Sonam Wangchuk and Ladakhi groups: protests, marches, hunger strikes
  • 2025:
    • Domicile, reservation, official language and recruitment safeguards, plus Hill Council provisions
    • 2026: talks continue with differences over the extent of self-government (verify)

Way forward

  • democratic representation: examine an elected Assembly with national-security safeguards
  • constitutional protection: Sixth Schedule or Article 371-style
  • strengthen Hill Councils with Funds, Functions and Functionaries (3Fs)
  • local employment:
    • Recruitment safeguards
    • Separate PSC demand
  • sustainable development: local say on glaciers, water, fragile ecosystems
  • continued dialogue with LAB and KDA
Memory aid · DCSLSC

Way forward: Democratic representation · Constitutional protection · Strengthen Hill Councils (3Fs) · Local jobs · Sustainable development · Continued dialogue. (own mnemonic)

Way forward / key point

A lasting solution must balance democratic autonomy, tribal identity, ecological protection and national security.
