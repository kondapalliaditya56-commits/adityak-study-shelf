The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Peasants and tribals lose their security (land revenue, forests, religion), so they rise first, led by elites, peasants, sects or chiefs. The revolts are local, uncoordinated and not democratic, yet political and public, so they are the pre-history of modern nationalism and the culmination of a hundred years before 1857.

  1. The bottom of the order moves first. The Western Educated Middle Class believes in the benevolence of British rule and answers it with socio-religious reform. Peasants and tribals, at the very bottom of the new colonial order, answer with defiance and often violence. The teacher's one-line logic: the common man wants security, economic, physical and socio-cultural.
    → But what exactly did the peasant lose?
  2. The peasant's squeeze. The Farming System of 1772 auctions land revenue to a revenue farmer (ijaradar). PS1793 makes the zamindar owner, with high rents, illegal abwabs and no occupancy right. The Rent Act 1859 promises occupancy after over twelve years but is ineffective in Bengal. In Ryotwari and Mahalwari areas the road is auction, moneylender, then landlessness.
    → And in Bengal and Bihar a planter adds a second trap.
  3. The indigo trap. The European planter gives a loan at the start, sells inputs to the ryot and buys the indigo back at a price below market. The ryot cannot pay rent and loan, the soil is exhausted, and he must borrow and grow indigo again, or face eviction. The teacher's two systems: Bengal Ryoti (a quadrant of the plot, Indigo Revolt 1859) and Bihar Tinkathia (3/20th, Champaran Satyagraha 1917).
    → Then fear for faith adds fuel.
  4. Religion adds fuel. As a grievance: temple and mosque lands are taxed, revenue-free tenures end, missionaries may enter after 1813, and Sati 1829, Lex Loci 1850 and Widow Remarriage 1856 are seen as interference. As an organiser: with no parties or newspapers, a sect already has a leader, a philosophy and unity.
    → Meanwhile the tribals, in their forests, lose even more.
  5. The tribal's world turned upside down. Tribals lived by cultivation, hunting and gathering, under their own tribal law and chief. The British pushed land revenue in through four routes (zamindars as owners, revenue farmers, the chief made a zamindar, ryots made individual owners), demanded cash, and banned Jhum and hunting because they wanted settled peasants paying LR regularly.
    → Then the law takes the forest itself.
  6. The forest is fenced (1878). Under Lytton 76-80 the Indian Forests Act 1878 splits forests into Reserved, Protected and Village, driven by the railway need for timber (about 2,000 sleepers a mile). Customary rights become privileges, and millions become trespassers on ancestral land. The Abkari system adds an auctioned liquor monopoly, second only to land revenue.
    → Now the elites, too, have lost something.
  7. The elites rise: civil uprisings. Raja Chait Singh of Benaras (1778-81) loses his estates for unpaid LR. Wazir Ali 97-98, the Poligars, the Bundelas and Veluthampi (1809) each lose status, land or autonomy to British rule or its Resident. The Paikas of Odisha (1817) draw multi-class support and are debated as a candidate for the true First War of Independence.
    → Peasants, meanwhile, begin to lead themselves.
  8. Peasants lead: Rangpur and Mysore. In 1783 the Rangpur peasants, hurt by the Farming System and the 1770 famine, loot the grain stores of Debi Singh and Gobinda Singh, free prisoners, attack courts and run their own government. In Mysore (1830-31) peasants rise against the Wodeyars and British agents because the SA 1799 subsidy forced high LR.
    → Elsewhere the leader comes from religion.
  9. Religion leads. Dasnami Sanyasis and Madari Fakirs (1760-1850s, peak about 1800 with 50,000) inspire Anandamath. Karim and Tipu Shah lead the Pagal Panthis. Titu Mir builds a bamboo fort, the Faraizi (Dudu Mian) say land belongs to God, and the Moplahs lose rights because the British recognise only the Janmies.
    → In the hills and forests, the tribals rise in a chain.
  10. The tribals rise, ending in the Hool. Pahariya 1778, Bhil 1818-31, Ramosi 1822, Khasi 1829-33, Kol 1831-32 and Kandh 1837-56 each rise against loss of land, forest rights or religion. The Santhals (1855-56) give a public prior warning, rise with bows and arrows against the unholy trinity of British, zamindars and moneylenders, and about 50,000 are killed. The Santhal Parganas follow, with a bar on land transfer to non-Santhals.
    → Two more risings come after 1857.
  11. After 1857: Birsa and Rampa. Birsa Munda's Ulugulan (1899-1900) begins on Christmas 1899, with a belief that British bullets turn to water. The Rampa revolt, led first by Muttadars and crushed by 1880, merges into the NCM in 1920. After 1922 Alluri Sitarama Raju restarts the Fituri and is executed in 1924.
    → So what do all these revolts add up to?
  12. The verdict. They were not all-India, not coordinated, with local leaders, no plan and no democratic goal, so not modern nationalism. But they had ideology, organisation, programme, awareness and symbolism, and crimes hide while politics shows. Hence the pre-history of modern nationalism, and the culmination of a hundred years before 1857.

In simple words#

  • Peasants and tribals, at the very bottom of the colonial order, rose first, with defiance and often violence, while the WEMC used reform movements.
  • The peasant lost security to land revenue (Farming System 1772, PS1793, auction and moneylender, the indigo debt trap)
  • The tribal lost forests and autonomy too (Forests Act 1878, Abkari, Jhum ban)
  • Religion was both a grievance (missionaries, ended free tenures, reform laws) and an organiser (a sect already had a leader and unity).
  • There were four kinds of revolt: civil (elite-led), peasant-led, religion-led and tribal, from Chait Singh 1778-81 to the Santhal Hool 1855-56, then Birsa 1899-1900 and Rampa.
  • They were local, uncoordinated and not democratic, so not 100% modern, but political, public and anti-colonial: the pre-history of modern nationalism and the culmination before 1857.

1 · Setting the stage#

Story link

Because the WEMC chose reform, the bottom of the order spoke first, in defiance. Ask: why are these revolts called the pre-history of modern nationalism?

  • The Western Educated Middle Class (WEMC) responded to British rule largely through socio-religious reform movements.
  • They believed in the benevolence of British rule, that the British would modernise India, and many benefited from the new economy and jobs British rule created.
  • But peasants and tribals, the people at the very bottom of the new colonial order, responded first: not with petitions but with defiance, often with violence.
  • INM leaders later called these pre-1857 revolts the "pre-history of modern nationalism." That does not mean they were failures: they were the first draft of a political resistance that would, over a century, become the Indian National Movement.
  • The 1857 revolt was in many ways the culmination of a hundred years of such small and big rebellions.
  • To understand 1857, first understand what drove peasants and tribals to revolt again and again.
Teacher's board
  • Western = Medicine, Law, Admin. The WEMC got its jobs from the new Western professions: medicine, law and administration (ADMIN underlined).
  • And the pre-British era jobs that the new system replaced: Mansabdars, Faujdars, Amalguzars, Amils, Pundits, Maulvis, Kazis, Muftis (military and revenue officers and the religious-legal class).
  • Reading: the old job-holders lost their positions while the new Western-educated class gained, so the old elite had a grievance of its own.
  • "Pre-history" = "unwritten history" (the teacher's gloss: these revolts are the part of the story that the standard nationalist history did not write).
  • And the verdict in his words: "NOT 100% MODERN BUT anti-colonial."
Teacher's board
  • Who is a peasant and who is a tribal?
  • A cultivator who farms for food security is poor: a ryot or peasant.
  • A cultivator who farms for profit is rich: a farmer.
  • The basis of the word "peasant" is OCCUPATION (cultivation).
  • A tribal is one who lives away from the mainstream, in hills, forests, deserts or islands (hills and forests underlined), and is not part of the majority population of the plains.
  • Therefore the tribals traditionally enjoyed autonomy in three ways: political (the tribal chief), economic (forest resources) and socio-cultural (their own way of life).
  • The dotted line: the land revenue systems of the plains were not applied by Indian kings to these areas.
  • That is what the British changed.

2 · Drivers of the peasant revolt#

Story link

Because the peasant's security was land revenue, every British system squeezed him in its own way. Ask: which system, which villain, which loss?

2.1 Why did the ryot have so many grievances?

  • The British introduced not one but several land revenue (LR) systems, and each squeezed the peasant in its own way.
System and periodHow it squeezed the ryot
Farming System of 1772 (Bengal)LR collection was auctioned to the highest bidder, a revenue farmer (the teacher: ijaradar). He extracted as much rent as he could, with the support of British officials, since collection happened under the District Collector's supervision. The peasant had no protection
Permanent Settlement 1793 (PS1793)Replaced the revenue farmer with the zamindar as permanent owner. The ryot had no occupancy rights (no security of tenure), and British law was on the zamindar's side since all legal rights in land were vested in him

Ryotwari and Mahalwari areasThe oppression came directly from the British government through high LR demands. If the ryot could not pay, his land was auctioned; this drove him to the moneylender; high interest led to deeper indebtedness; default meant losing land to the moneylender. 2.2 Contract farming: the indigo system

  • European indigo planters forced ryots to cultivate indigo on a portion of their land.
  • The handout dates it in Bengal until 1860, and later in Bihar until 1918.
  • The ryot was made to take a loan from the planter at the outset, then sold the indigo back to the planter at a price far below market rate. He was perpetually in debt.
  • Indigo exhausted the soil, reducing the productivity of the land he used for food crops.
  • If he refused, the planter's Indian agents, local British officials and courts came down on him. There was no escape.

Teacher's board

  • Indigo model: the zamindar still pays LR to the State, but now the European planter takes the land on lease (lease amount to the zamindar) and collects rent from the ryots (the planter becomes the middle landlord), and in addition there is a contract between the planter and the ryot: the ryot must grow indigo on a part (one quadrant) of his plot.

The indigo debt trap (the teacher's board)

  • Planter gives a loan (the board says low-interest) to the ryot at the start
  • Ryot buys equipment and indigo seeds from the planter
  • Ryot sells the indigo to the planter and gets a price that is not remunerative
  • Inability to pay: the rent and the loan
  • Soil productivity falls (indigo exhausts the soil; the quadrant turns Banjar, fallow land)
  • Debt trap: the ryot is forced to grow indigo by taking more loan, else eviction, plus oppression by the planter's Indian agents

2.3 What role did religion play?

  • Religion played a dual role: grievance and organiser.
  • As a grievance:
    • (1) Christian missionaries were converting people
    • (2) the British ended revenue-free and rent-free land tenures held by temples, mosques and religious institutions
    • (3) social reform laws were seen as interference in religion: the Sati Abolition Act 1829, the Lex Loci Act 1850 (inheritance even after conversion to another religion) and the Hindu Widow Remarriages Act 1856.

3 · Drivers of the tribal revolt#

Story link

Because the tribal also lived by forest and chief, the British reach into the forest hurt him doubly. Ask: how did LR enter the forest, and what did the Act of 1878 take?

3.1 What was different about the tribal experience?

  • Tribals lived in forests and hills, away from the plains.
  • Two things gave their life its shape: tribal autonomy (in civil and criminal matters they governed themselves by their own customary law) and their relationship with the forest (home, livelihood, identity). British rule destroyed both.
Peasant versus tribal: what was lost
Teacher's board
  • Tribal livelihood = three lives. Cultivation (the tribal as a ryot or peasant), hunting (hunters), and gathering forest produce (gatherers), with barter trade with plains people.
  • Today the major forest produce is timber
  • the minor is bamboo, tendu patta, sal, honey and so on.
  • Law = tribal law, and L, E, J (legislative, executive, judicial) = the tribal chief: all three powers sat with him, which is the political autonomy.
  • "Don't get confused if the phrase 'Peasant Revolt' is used for a tribal revolt": many tribals were cultivating, so a tribal revolt can be called a peasant revolt in some books
  • Keep the two kinds apart by the leader and the grievance (forest and autonomy for tribals)
  • How the British pushed LR into the forests: four routes (the primary British concern was extraction of land revenue):
  • 1. Zamindars from the plains were made legal owners of tribal land.
  • 2. Tribal lands were farmed out to revenue farmers.
  • 3. In some cases the tribal chief was recognised as a zamindar, with the same exploitative powers.
  • 4. In others tribal ryots were made individual landowners, breaking up the collective, community-based relationship with their land.
  • High LR demands and the British insistence on cash payment (instead of produce) brought in moneylenders: indebtedness, then loss of land.
  • (The handout's explanation: a subsistence farmer who grows grain but must pay in rupees has to sell in the market first, exposing him to price swings and middlemen at every step.)
Teacher's board
  • The "both LR systems" on tribal land: the British used both the ryotwari idea (cultivator = landowner) and the zamindari idea (big person = landowner) and also the contract of LR, that is auction, of the right to collect LR (the revenue farmer).
  • That matches the handout's four routes.

5.7 The Paika Rebellion (1817)

  • Who: the Paikas were the traditional landed militia of Odisha, a warrior class rooted in the feudal order.
  • Leaders and goal: feudal aristocrats, chiefly Bakshi Jagabandhu (military commander of the Raja of Khurda) and marginalised zamindars.
  • The goal was to restore lost hereditary privileges, authority and rent-free estates.
  • Multi-class support: despite elite leadership, massive grassroots support from ordinary peasants, salt makers and the tribal Kondhs of Ghumusar: a true multi-class uprising.
  • EIC provocations (after conquering Odisha, 1803):
    • Stripped the Paikas of their rent-free land tenures (jagirs)
    • imposed extortionate short-term settlements demanding revenue strictly in silver (Sicca) rather than the traditional cowrie shells
    • banned local sea-salt production to create a monopoly that sent prices soaring
    • And dethroned the revered Raja of Khurda, uniting elite loyalists and the masses.
  • Course: rebels destroyed British symbols of authority (police stations, courts, salt monopoly offices), briefly captured Puri, forced the EIC forces to retreat and proclaimed the Raja of Khurda as sovereign.
  • Crushed: the British declared martial law and crushed the core uprising with overwhelming force by 1818.
  • Bakshi Jagabandhu led a jungle guerrilla war until he surrendered on negotiated terms in 1825.
  • Verdict:
    • One of the most formidable pre-1857 armed uprisings
    • Because of its scale and multi-class participation, it is debated in modern historiography as a candidate for India's true "First War of Independence."

9 · Understanding the character of pre-1857 revolts#

Story link

Because the revolts were many and scattered, the question is what they add up to. Ask: modern nationalism, or its pre-history?

9.1 Were these revolts modern nationalism?

  • No. Modern nationalism rests on a specific idea of the Nation and a specific idea of Politics.
  • The handout's model is the French Revolution's Liberty, Equality, Fraternity: a modern nation is one where citizens feel united as one people (Fraternity/Unity), equal before the law (Equality), and collectively supreme (the national interest, not that of any king, caste or class, is paramount: Liberty/Sovereignty).
  • Modern politics needs:
    • A united leadership
    • a clear ideology and goal
    • a plan (long-term) and a programme (short-term)
    • a mouthpiece (newspaper, website) to articulate ideology and justify actions
    • And an organisational structure through which directions reach every follower
Teacher's board
  • Two halves of "modern INM": the idea (modern nation = ideology) and the method (modern politics).
  • Nation = the sum of people, and three words: one (fraternity), equal (equality), supreme (liberty): the modern meaning given by the French Revolution 1789.
  • Consequences: Polity (democracy, republic, fundamental rights, DPSPs, right to equality), Economy (capitalism: equality of opportunity, right to property, LPG; socialism: land reforms, redistribution of wealth, welfare state), Society (feminism because 50% of the nation is female; anti-casteism; no discrimination on religion or language).
  • Modern politics (the method of the INM): A ideology, B goals, C plan (long-term), D programme (short-term: "what today?"), E organisation, strong united leadership, G money (the rupee sign), and "voice" to carry all of A to E and G (fasts, yatras, symbols, flags, slogans, poems, songs, plays and films, graffiti, memes, press, social media, radio, television).

  • there is no letter F (leadership sits between E and G without a circled letter)

Where the pre-1857 revolts fell short:

  • 1. Not all-India movements: no unity across regions.
  • 2. No coordination with each other.
  • 3. Only local leaders: nobody thought of India first and their region second.
  • 4. Lacked proper planning.
  • 5. Their goal was not democracy in any modern sense.

9.2 Then why "pre-history of modern nationalism"?

  • Because they were unmistakably political: not apolitical, not merely criminal, not merely religious. They had:
  • Ideology: they wanted to restore the moral order British rule had disturbed.
  • Their vocabulary was not rights, democracy, colonialism or "No Taxation Without Representation," but immoral rule implies oppression, which implies denial of economic, cultural and political rights: different language, same moral architecture.
  • Organisation: religion provided unity, leadership and the habit of collective action, especially when a whole sect revolted.
  • Many set up their own governments, courts and taxes: a functioning parallel state, however temporary.
  • Programme: meetings were held, plans were made.
  • Political awareness:
    • The Rangpur peasants hit grain stores, prison and courts
    • the Kols targeted property, not lives
    • the Santhals said "We have always hunted game in these forests. Now we hunt our oppressors." They knew exactly what instrument oppressed them
  • Political symbolism:
    • Public marches and meetings (not secret)
    • the Santhal warning of 1855
    • Birsa Munda's uprising timed for Christmas 1899
    • the Santhal idea of the fatherland that must be reclaimed
Key takeaway
  • Crimes are committed secretly. Politics is done in public. These revolts were public: that is why INM leaders called them the pre-history of modern nationalism.

Story link

10.1 Munda Ulugulan (1899-1900), Chhota Nagpur

  • Leader: Birsa Munda.
  • Grievances:
    • Loss of lands to outsiders
    • oppression by British officials
    • missionary activities of Christian missionaries in tribal areas
  • Role of religion: people believed Birsa had supernatural healing powers and that British bullets would turn to water when fired at him.
  • (A recurring feature where people face a vastly stronger enemy: it makes resistance feel possible; religion gave courage where reason might have said surrender.)
  • Political symbolism: he chose Christmas of 1899 to launch the Ulugulan: a deliberate rising against British power on the most sacred day of the religion the missionaries were using to erode Munda identity.
  • Result: crushed by the British. (Prelims 2020: 'Ulgulan' = the Great Tumult = Birsa Munda's revolt 1899-1900.)

10.2 The Rampa Rebellion / Fituri, Andhra

Grievances (Rampa Hills of Andhra):

  • 1. Commercial exploitation of forests.
  • 2. Land acquisition for road construction.
  • 3. Loss of lands to moneylenders and merchants.
  • 4. Prohibition of Podu cultivation (shifting cultivation, the Jhum equivalent).
  • 5. Restriction on use of forest resources.
  • 6. Tax on toddy (the Abkari system at work).
  • Leadership: the Muttadars, local estate holders, roughly poligars or taluqdars: the same pattern as civil uprisings: elite leaders with popular participation.
  • First phase: crushed by 1880 (the 1870s start).
  • Second phase (Gandhian overlap):
    • In 1920 the Rampa tribals' movement merged into the Non-Cooperation Movement
    • their civil disobedience was violation of forest laws, in Andhra under Alluri Sitarama Raju.
  • After NCM withdrawal (1922): Raju concluded India could be liberated only by force and re-initiated the Fituri (the violent rebellion). Captured and executed in 1924.
  • The intersection: a pure tribal economic revolt in the 1870s, merges with Gandhi's mass movement in 1920, then after Gandhi's withdrawal produces a leader who draws the opposite lesson.
  • Raju becomes a revolutionary martyr in the Andhra tradition.
Teacher's board

  • He also underlined the 1920 merger, "violation of forest laws" as civil disobedience, "after NCM withdrawal (1922)," "re-initiated the Fituri," "captured and executed in 1924."
