1 · How NABARD came to be#

  • The importance of institutional credit for the rural economy was clear early in planning. At the Government’s insistence RBI constituted the Committee to Review the Arrangements for Institutional Credit for Agriculture and Rural Development (CRAFICARD) on 30 March 1979, chaired by B. Sivaraman, former member of the Planning Commission.
  • Its interim report (28 November 1979) asked for a new organisational device giving “undivided attention, forceful direction and pointed focus” to rural credit: a unique development financial institution.
  • NABARD was approved by Parliament through Act 61 of 1981 and came into existence on 12 July 1982, by transferring RBI’s agricultural credit functions and the refinance functions of the Agricultural Refinance and Development Corporation (ARDC).
  • After the share capital of the Government and RBI was revised, NABARD is today fully owned by the Government of India.
Mission (handout)

“Promote sustainable and equitable agriculture and rural development through participative financial and non-financial interventions, innovations, technology and institutional development for securing prosperity.”

2 · Functions at a glance#

Three heads, touching almost every aspect of the rural economy: Financial, Developmental, Supervision.

  • Refinance support and rural infrastructure; district-level credit plans and guiding banks to meet targets.
  • Supervising Cooperative Banks and Regional Rural Banks (RRBs), helping them adopt sound practices and the CBS (core banking) platform.
  • Designing new development schemes and implementing the Government of India’s schemes; training handicraft artisans and giving them a marketing platform.
Claims to remember (handout)

The SHG Bank Linkage Project (1992) “has blossomed into the world’s largest microfinance project”. Kisan Credit Card was designed by NABARD. It has financed one-fifth of India’s total rural infrastructure and was a pioneer in watershed development as a tool for climate proofing.

Long Term Irrigation Fund (LTIF)

  • Set up in 2016 within NABARD with an initial corpus of ₹20,000 crore to fund and fast-track incomplete major and medium irrigation projects. A Mission was set up in the Ministry of Water Resources, River Development and Ganga Rejuvenation.
  • Aim: bridge the resource gap and finish the projects during 2016-2020. Priority I: 23 projects by 2016-17; Priority II: 31 projects by 2017-18; Priority III: balance 45 projects by 2019-20.

PMAY-Grameen (PMAY-G)

  • A pucca house with basic amenities for all houseless households and those in kutcha or dilapidated houses by 2022; implemented by the Ministry of Rural Development. About 2.95 crore houses by 2022; one crore by March 2019.
  • For one crore houses over 3 years (2016-17 to 2018-19) the requirement was ₹81,975 crore: ₹60,000 crore from budgetary sources and ₹21,975 crore through borrowings from NABARD. In 2017-18 NABARD sanctioned ₹9,000 crore to NRIDA for the central share.

4 · Developmental functions#

  • More than 50% of rural credit is disbursed by cooperative banks and RRBs, and NABARD regulates and supervises them. Under Institutional Development (ID) it supports: Rural Credit Cooperatives; State Cooperative Banks (StCBs); Central Cooperative Banks (CCBs); Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS); State Cooperative Agriculture and Rural Development Banks (SCARDBs); Primary Cooperative Agriculture and Rural Development Banks (PCARDBs).
  • Farm sector: Farm Sector Development Department (FSDD) and Farm Sector Policy Department (FSPD).
  • Off-farm sector: financial inclusion; micro credit innovation; women Self-Help Groups, Joint Liability Groups and the SHG-Bank Linkage programme; microfinance to various bodies.
  • RUDSETI / RUDSETI-type institutions / RSETIs: specialised institutions giving entrepreneurship and skill training to rural youth and women; NABARD assists those meeting its criteria.

6 · Supervision functions#

  • Section 35(6) of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 empowers NABARD to inspect State Cooperative Banks, Central Cooperative Banks (DCCBs) and Regional Rural Banks.
  • NABARD also inspects, on a voluntary basis, state-level cooperative institutions such as SCARDBs, Apex Weavers Societies and Marketing Federations.
Objectives of supervision
#Objective
1Protect the interest of present and future depositors

3Ensure observance of rules and guidelines of NABARD, RBI and the Government
4Examine the financial soundness of the banks
5Suggest ways to strengthen institutions so they can purvey rural credit more efficiently

7 · NBFCs: what they are and how they differ from banks#

  • A Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC) is a company registered under the Companies Act, 1956 engaged in loans and advances; acquisition of shares, stocks, bonds, debentures, securities; leasing; hire-purchase; insurance business; chit business. It excludes institutions whose principal business is agriculture, industry, trading in goods (other than securities), providing services, or buying, selling or constructing immovable property.
  • A company whose principal business is receiving deposits under any scheme, lump sum or instalments, is also an NBFC: the Residuary Non-Banking Company (RNBC).
NBFC versus bank (the three differences)
BankNBFC
Demand depositsCan acceptCannot accept demand deposits
Payment and settlement systemPart of it; issues cheques drawn on itselfNot part of it; cannot issue cheques drawn on itself
Deposit insurance (DICGC)AvailableNot available to NBFC depositors
  • Systemically important NBFCs: asset size of ₹500 crore or more (last audited balance sheet), because their activities bear on the financial stability of the economy.
  • Registration: under Section 45-IA of the RBI Act, 1934, no NBFC may carry on business without a Certificate of Registration and Net Owned Funds (NOF) of ₹25 lakh (₹2 crore since April 1999).
  • Exempt from RBI registration (to avoid dual regulation), subject to conditions: Venture Capital Fund, Merchant Banking and Stock-broking companies (SEBI); insurance companies (IRDA); Nidhi companies (Ministry of Corporate Affairs; Section 620A Companies Act 1956); Chit companies (Chit Funds Act 1982; State Governments); Housing Finance Companies (National Housing Bank); Stock Exchanges; Mutual Benefit companies. Mortgage Guarantee Companies are notified as NBFCs (Section 45-I(f)(iii)); Core Investment Companies under ₹100 crore, or ₹100 crore and above but not accessing public funds, are exempt.

This handout classifies systemically important NBFCs at ₹500 crore; Chapter 7 (the newer scale-based regulation, 2022) uses layers with the Base Layer at non-deposit NBFCs below ₹1,000 crore. The handout also quotes the DICGC cover as ₹1 lakh, which may be dated; check the current limit.

9 · Residuary NBFCs, prudential norms, interest and deposits#

  • RNBC: principal business is receiving deposits under any scheme, and it is not an Investment, Asset Financing or Loan company. It must maintain investments as per RBI directions, in addition to liquid assets, and prudential norms apply. No ceiling on deposits, but 100% of deposit liability must be invested in highly liquid, secure instruments: Central/State government securities, fixed deposits with scheduled commercial banks, CoDs of SCBs/FIs, units of mutual funds.
  • Prudential norms: NBFC (Deposit Accepting or Holding) Prudential Norms Directions 2007; Non-SI Non-Deposit NBFCs Directions 2015; SI Non-Deposit NBFCs Directions 2015. They cover income recognition, asset classification, provisioning, exposure norms, disclosures, capital adequacy, limits on investment in land and building and unquoted shares, and LTV for gold-loan NBFCs. Deposit-accepting NBFCs also meet statutory liquidity requirements.
  • Interest rates: RBI has deregulated interest on loans by financial institutions (except NBFC-MFIs). The rate depends on the loan agreement but must be transparent and disclosed in the application form and sanction letter.
Who may accept public deposits
EntityRule
Banks, including co-operative banksMay accept deposits
NBFCs with a deposit-accepting CoROnly those with a specific authorisation and an investment-grade rating, to a limit of 1.5 times NOF
Housing Finance Companies; companies under the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) RulesUp to a certain limit
Co-operative credit societiesFrom members only, not the general public

  • Since 1997 RBI has issued no new CoR for deposit acceptance to NBFCs; as a matter of policy only banks should accept public deposits.
  • Deposit terms (handout): maximum interest 12.5%; paid or compounded at rests not shorter than monthly; minimum 12 months, maximum 60 months; no deposits repayable on demand. Unrated AFCs had to be rated by 31 March 2016 or could not renew or accept deposits.
  • Why so restrictive: depositors place money on trust, unlike an investor who shares risk and return; protection of depositors’ interest is supreme. Banks are the most regulated; DICGC pays insurance on deposits up to ₹1 lakh if a bank fails (handout figure).

10 · Chit funds#

  • Chit funds / chitty / kuri are saving institutions with no standard form: members make periodic subscriptions; each period’s collection goes to one member chosen by bid, lot, auction or tender; every member gets a turn before a second round. They are the Indian version of Rotating Savings and Credit Associations (ROSCAs).
  • Chit Funds Act, 1982, Section 2(b): a “chit” is a transaction where a person agrees with a specified number of persons that each subscribes a sum (or grain) in instalments over a definite period, and each subscriber in turn gets the prize amount by lot, auction or tender. It is not a chit if some, but not all, subscribers get the prize without liability to pay future subscriptions, or if all get it by turns with a liability to pay future subscriptions.
  • Regulation: the Chit Funds Act 1982 is a Central Act administered by State Governments; the Centre has framed no rules, so registration and regulation are by States under their own rules. RBI counts them as NBFCs under Miscellaneous Non-Banking Company (MNBC) but has laid down no separate framework.
  • Fraud: cheating by a chit company through fraudulent schemes is an offence under the Prize Chits and Money Circulation Schemes (Banning) Act, 1978; State Governments investigate and prosecute.
  • Name rule: under Rule 8(2)(b)(iii) of the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014 a company whose main business is a chit fund cannot be incorporated unless its name indicates it (“Chit Fund”).

Handout alert

The handout gives the date of the RBI prohibition on chit funds accepting deposits as “August 2009” in one place and “2009” in another. They agree on the year. Another small point: the section on “regulation” says RBI has no framework for chit funds, yet it also says RBI can prosecute a chit fund that takes deposits. Exam-hall rule: chit funds are regulated by State Governments; RBI’s only hook is the deposit ban.

11 · Collective investment schemes, MLM and Ponzi schemes#

Who regulates what
ActivityRegulator or law
Multi-Level Marketing, direct selling, online selling companiesNot RBI. The respective State Government
Collective Investment Schemes (CIS) (money for units: time-share in resorts, profit from sale of wood, developed commercial plots)SEBI. Complaints go to SEBI and to the EOW/Police of the State Government
Money circulation, MLM / pyramid and Ponzi schemesBanned under the Prize Chits and Money Circulation Schemes (Banning) Act, 1978; acceptance of money is a cognizable offence; monitored and dealt with by State Governments. RBI’s role: only advising and assisting the Centre in framing rules
  • Pyramid / MLM: income comes not from selling a product but from enrolling more members who pay hefty fees; a portion goes up the pyramid. Any break in the chain collapses it, and the lowest members suffer most.
  • Ponzi scheme: collects money on promises of high returns; with no asset creation, one depositor’s money is paid as another’s return. The scheme becomes unviable, cannot return even the principal, and the perpetrators disappear.
  • The 1978 Act prohibits any person to promote or conduct a prize chit or money circulation scheme, enrol as a member, or participate by receiving or remitting money.

Discuss the genesis, functions and role of NABARD in India’s rural economy.

Intro: NABARD was created as a development finance institution with “undivided attention” to rural credit.

  • CRAFICARD 1979, Act 61 of 1981, 12 July 1982; RBI agri credit plus ARDC refinance; now fully GoI-owned.
  • Three functions: financial (refinance, RIDF, LTIF, PMAY-G), developmental (cooperatives, SHG-BLP, RSETIs), supervision (StCBs, DCCBs, RRBs).
  • SHG-BLP the world’s largest microfinance project; KCC; one-fifth of rural infrastructure.

Closing line: NABARD is the rural economy’s banker’s bank.

How is NABARD involved in climate finance in India?

Intro: India’s adaptation needs are met through international and national funds, with NABARD as the implementing agency.

  • NIE for the Adaptation Fund (July 2012, only NIE for India) and for NAFCC (August 2015).
  • Adaptation Fund (Kyoto, 2% of CER proceeds) and the Green Climate Fund (Cancun 2010).
  • NABARD’s own Climate Change Fund from 2016-17 profit.
  • Watershed development as climate proofing.

Closing line: Rural resilience is financed one watershed at a time.

Differentiate NBFCs from banks. Why does RBI restrict their deposit-taking?

Intro: NBFCs lend and invest like banks but lack some banking features.

  • No demand deposits, no cheque or payment-system access, no DICGC cover.
  • Registration (Sec. 45-IA); categories by liabilities, size and activity; exempt categories regulated by SEBI, IRDA, NHB, MCA, States.
  • No new deposit CoR since 1997; rated, capped at 1.5 × NOF, 12-60 months.
  • Depositors place money on trust; protection is supreme.

Closing line: Lend like a bank, but never hold the public’s trust like one.

Examine the regulatory framework for chit funds and the 1978 Act on prize chits and money circulation schemes.

Intro: Informal savings schemes sit across State and Central laws, which creates regulatory gaps.

  • Chit Funds Act 1982: Central Act administered by States; no Central rules; RBI deposit ban since 2009.
  • Chit subscriptions are not deposits.
  • MLM, pyramid and Ponzi schemes are banned by the 1978 Act and enforced by States.
  • CIS regulated by SEBI.

Closing line: A fraud that spans three regulators is a fraud that is hard to catch.

Explain the types of NBFCs and the conditions that define each.

Intro: NBFCs are classified by what they do and how large they are.

  • IDF-NBFC, CIC, MFI (85%), Factors (50%/50%), MGC (90%, ₹100 crore).
