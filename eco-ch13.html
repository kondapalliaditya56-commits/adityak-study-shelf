In simple words#

In simple words
  • NABARD began with the CRAFICARD committee (30 March 1979, B. Sivaraman). It was created by Act 61 of 1981 and started on 12 July 1982. It is now fully owned by the Government of India.
  • It has three kinds of functions: financial (refinance, RIDF, LTIF, PMAY-G), developmental (cooperatives, SHG-Bank Linkage, RSETIs) and supervision (StCBs, DCCBs, RRBs).
  • NABARD is the only National Implementing Entity in India for the Adaptation Fund (July 2012) and also for NAFCC.
  • An NBFC lends and invests like a bank but cannot take demand deposits, is not in the payment system and has no DICGC cover.
  • Some firms are not registered by RBI because another regulator handles them: SEBI, IRDA, NHB, MCA or the State Governments.
  • Chit funds are run under a State-administered Act. CIS is regulated by SEBI. MLM, pyramid and Ponzi schemes are banned by the 1978 Act.

1 · How NABARD came to be#

🧠 Understand it firstPicture rural credit before 1982 as many small taps run by different bodies. The Government asked RBI to review the arrangements, and the Sivaraman committee said rural credit needed one institution giving it “undivided attention”. Parliament passed an Act, and the new bank took over RBI’s farm-credit work and the refinance work of an older corporation. Today the Government owns it fully.
✏ ExampleThe handout’s dates: CRAFICARD constituted 30 March 1979 (chair B. Sivaraman); interim report 28 November 1979; Act 61 of 1981; NABARD comes into existence 12 July 1982 by transferring RBI’s agricultural credit functions and the refinance functions of ARDC.
  • The importance of institutional credit for the rural economy was clear early in planning. At the Government’s insistence RBI constituted the Committee to Review the Arrangements for Institutional Credit for Agriculture and Rural Development (CRAFICARD) on 30 March 1979, chaired by B. Sivaraman, former member of the Planning Commission.
  • Its interim report (28 November 1979) asked for a new organisational device giving “undivided attention, forceful direction and pointed focus” to rural credit: a unique development financial institution.
  • NABARD was approved by Parliament through Act 61 of 1981 and came into existence on 12 July 1982, by transferring RBI’s agricultural credit functions and the refinance functions of the Agricultural Refinance and Development Corporation (ARDC).
  • After the share capital of the Government and RBI was revised, NABARD is today fully owned by the Government of India.

2 · Functions at a glance#

🧠 Understand it firstNABARD wears three hats. As financier it supplies money (refinance and rural infrastructure) and guides district credit plans. As developer it designs and runs schemes and trains artisans. As supervisor it watches cooperative banks and Regional Rural Banks and helps them adopt sound practice and core banking.
✏ ExampleThe handout’s claims: the SHG Bank Linkage Project (1992) “has blossomed into the world’s largest microfinance project”; the Kisan Credit Card was designed by NABARD; it has financed one-fifth of India’s rural infrastructure and pioneered watershed development as climate proofing.

Three heads, touching almost every aspect of the rural economy: Financial, Developmental, Supervision.

  • Refinance support and rural infrastructure; district-level credit plans and guiding banks to meet targets.
  • Supervising Cooperative Banks and Regional Rural Banks (RRBs), helping them adopt sound practices and the CBS (core banking) platform.
  • Designing new development schemes and implementing the Government of India’s schemes; training handicraft artisans and giving them a marketing platform.
Claims to remember (handout)

The SHG Bank Linkage Project (1992) “has blossomed into the world’s largest microfinance project”. Kisan Credit Card was designed by NABARD. It has financed one-fifth of India’s total rural infrastructure and was a pioneer in watershed development as a tool for climate proofing.

  • The formal credit delivery mechanism proved inadequate and was not fully integrated into rural social and community development. Since some kind of collateral is required, a vast proportion of poor rural households were automatically out of the credit network.

4 · Developmental functions#

PYQs for this topic: 1 Prelims · 0 Mains
🧠 Understand it firstMore than half of rural credit goes out through cooperative banks and RRBs, so NABARD’s developmental job is to strengthen them: the whole ladder from the village society to the state apex bank. It also runs farm-sector and off-farm departments, promotes women’s SHGs and Joint Liability Groups, and backs rural training institutes.
✏ ExampleThe handout’s ladder under Institutional Development: Rural Credit Cooperatives; State Cooperative Banks (StCBs); Central Cooperative Banks (CCBs); Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS); SCARDBs; PCARDBs. Training: RUDSETI and RSETIs for rural youth and women.
  • More than 50% of rural credit is disbursed by cooperative banks and RRBs, and NABARD regulates and supervises them. Under Institutional Development (ID) it supports: Rural Credit Cooperatives; State Cooperative Banks (StCBs); Central Cooperative Banks (CCBs); Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS); State Cooperative Agriculture and Rural Development Banks (SCARDBs); Primary Cooperative Agriculture and Rural Development Banks (PCARDBs).
  • Farm sector: Farm Sector Development Department (FSDD) and Farm Sector Policy Department (FSPD).
  • Off-farm sector: financial inclusion; micro credit innovation; women Self-Help Groups, Joint Liability Groups and the SHG-Bank Linkage programme; microfinance to various bodies.
  • RUDSETI / RUDSETI-type institutions / RSETIs: specialised institutions giving entrepreneurship and skill training to rural youth and women; NABARD assists those meeting its criteria.

5 · Climate finance: the funds NABARD works with#

🧠 Understand it firstClimate money has four pots. Two are international (Adaptation Fund and Green Climate Fund) and two are Indian (NAFCC and NABARD’s own Climate Change Fund). NABARD is the national channel: it is the accredited National Implementing Entity, which means it carries full responsibility for managing, financing, monitoring and reporting the projects, while the Environment Ministry is the National Designated Authority that endorses proposals.
✏ ExampleThe handout’s facts: Adaptation Fund under the Kyoto Protocol, established 2001, launched 2007 at Marrakech, funded partly by 2% of CER proceeds, HQ Washington. Green Climate Fund: decision at COP 16 Cancun (December 2010), operational at COP 17 Durban (2011), HQ Songdo. NAFCC: August 2015, ₹350 crore for 2015-16 and 2016-17.

Step 1 · A bank’s balancing act (NCERT)
  • A bank accepts deposits from the public and lends part of them. It keeps a portion to repay depositors whenever they demand their funds. The interest paid to depositors is lower than the interest charged to borrowers; the difference is the spread, the bank’s profit.
  • Any bank would like to lend the maximum possible, but being able to repay depositors on demand is crucial to its survival. Depositors keep funds in a bank only if they are fully confident of getting them back on demand. So a bank must balance lending so that sufficient funds are available to repay any depositor.
  • Banks can lend because they do not expect all depositors to withdraw at the same time.
Step 2 · The rule that limits lending (NCERT)
  • The RBI fixes a percentage of deposits which every bank must keep as reserves, so that no bank is “over lending”. This is a legal requirement: the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR). Banks must also keep some reserves in liquid form in the short term: the Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR).
Step 3 · Lender of last resort (Glossary)
  • The function of the monetary authority in which it provides a guarantee of solvency to commercial banks in a situation of liquidity crisis or bank runs.

10 · Chit funds#

🧠 Understand it firstA chit fund is a savings circle written into law. Members pay in every period; each period the pot goes to one member chosen by bid, lot, auction or tender; everyone gets a turn before the next round. The Central Act is run by State Governments, so there is no single national rulebook. RBI’s only hook is that chit funds may not accept public deposits. A chit subscription is not a deposit.
✏ ExampleThe handout’s name rule: a company whose main business is a chit fund must carry “Chit Fund” in its name (Rule 8(2)(b)(iii), Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014). Fraud by a chit company is an offence under the Prize Chits and Money Circulation Schemes (Banning) Act, 1978.
  • Chit funds / chitty / kuri are saving institutions with no standard form: members make periodic subscriptions; each period’s collection goes to one member chosen by bid, lot, auction or tender; every member gets a turn before a second round. They are the Indian version of Rotating Savings and Credit Associations (ROSCAs).
  • Chit Funds Act, 1982, Section 2(b): a “chit” is a transaction where a person agrees with a specified number of persons that each subscribes a sum (or grain) in instalments over a definite period, and each subscriber in turn gets the prize amount by lot, auction or tender. It is not a chit if some, but not all, subscribers get the prize without liability to pay future subscriptions, or if all get it by turns with a liability to pay future subscriptions.
  • Regulation: the Chit Funds Act 1982 is a Central Act administered by State Governments; the Centre has framed no rules, so registration and regulation are by States under their own rules. RBI counts them as NBFCs under Miscellaneous Non-Banking Company (MNBC) but has laid down no separate framework.
  • Fraud: cheating by a chit company through fraudulent schemes is an offence under the Prize Chits and Money Circulation Schemes (Banning) Act, 1978; State Governments investigate and prosecute.
  • Name rule: under Rule 8(2)(b)(iii) of the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014 a company whose main business is a chit fund cannot be incorporated unless its name indicates it (“Chit Fund”).

11 · Collective investment schemes, MLM and Ponzi schemes#

🧠 Understand it firstThree kinds of money-collecting scheme, three owners. Collective Investment Schemes (money for units such as time-share or plots) belong to SEBI. Multi-level marketing and direct selling belong to the State Government. Money circulation, pyramid and Ponzi schemes are banned outright by the 1978 Act, and States deal with them. A pyramid pays from new members’ fees; a Ponzi pays old investors from new investors’ money, so both collapse when new money stops.
✏ ExampleThe handout’s Ponzi picture: high-return promises, no asset creation, one depositor’s money is paid as another’s return, the scheme cannot return even the principal and the perpetrators disappear. In a pyramid, the lowest members suffer most.

Exam traps#

Each trap ends with a rule for the exam hall.

Prelims trap: An NBFC is not a bank

An NBFC cannot accept demand deposits, is not part of the payment and settlement system and cannot issue cheques drawn on itself. Its depositors have no DICGC cover.

Rule: lends like a bank, but all three banking features are missing.

Prelims trap: Chit funds are not regulated by RBI

The Chit Funds Act 1982 is a Central Act administered by State Governments. A chit subscription is not a deposit. RBI's only hook is the ban on chit funds accepting deposits (2009).

Rule: chit funds = State Governments; RBI only has the deposit ban.

Prelims trap: Two climate funds, two headquarters

The Adaptation Fund has its HQ in Washington, USA, and the Green Climate Fund has its HQ in Songdo, Incheon, Republic of Korea.

Rule: Adaptation Fund = Washington; GCF = Songdo, Korea.

Concept trap: Section 35(6) is not Section 45-IA

Section 35(6) of the Banking Regulation Act 1949 lets NABARD inspect StCBs, DCCBs and RRBs. Section 45-IA of the RBI Act 1934 is the NBFC registration rule.

Rule: 35(6) = NABARD inspects rural banks; 45-IA = NBFC registration.

Handout alert: Rs 500 crore or Rs 1,000 crore

This handout makes a systemically important NBFC Rs 500 crore. Chapter 7 (scale-based regulation, 2022) puts the Base Layer at non-deposit NBFCs below Rs 1,000 crore.

Rule: use the figure that matches the wording of the question; the older NBFC-ND-SI wording means Rs 500 crore.

Handout alert: Adaptation Fund: 2007 and CoP 7

The handout says the Adaptation Fund was established in 2001 and launched in 2007 at CoP 7, Marrakech. The year and the CoP number do not obviously match.

Rule: use the wording only if an option repeats it; check the COP numbering in your notebook.

Discuss the genesis, functions and role of NABARD in India’s rural economy.

Intro: NABARD was created as a development finance institution with “undivided attention” to rural credit.

  • CRAFICARD 1979, Act 61 of 1981, 12 July 1982; RBI agri credit plus ARDC refinance; now fully GoI-owned.
  • Three functions: financial (refinance, RIDF, LTIF, PMAY-G), developmental (cooperatives, SHG-BLP, RSETIs), supervision (StCBs, DCCBs, RRBs).
  • SHG-BLP the world’s largest microfinance project; KCC; one-fifth of rural infrastructure.

Closing line: NABARD is the rural economy’s banker’s bank.

How is NABARD involved in climate finance in India?

Intro: India’s adaptation needs are met through international and national funds, with NABARD as the implementing agency.

  • NIE for the Adaptation Fund (July 2012, only NIE for India) and for NAFCC (August 2015).
  • Adaptation Fund (Kyoto, 2% of CER proceeds) and the Green Climate Fund (Cancun 2010).
  • NABARD’s own Climate Change Fund from 2016-17 profit.
  • Watershed development as climate proofing.

Closing line: Rural resilience is financed one watershed at a time.

Differentiate NBFCs from banks. Why does RBI restrict their deposit-taking?

Intro: NBFCs lend and invest like banks but lack some banking features.

  • No demand deposits, no cheque or payment-system access, no DICGC cover.
  • Registration (Sec. 45-IA); categories by liabilities, size and activity; exempt categories regulated by SEBI, IRDA, NHB, MCA, States.
  • No new deposit CoR since 1997; rated, capped at 1.5 × NOF, 12-60 months.
  • Depositors place money on trust; protection is supreme.

Closing line: Lend like a bank, but never hold the public’s trust like one.

Examine the regulatory framework for chit funds and the 1978 Act on prize chits and money circulation schemes.

Intro: Informal savings schemes sit across State and Central laws, which creates regulatory gaps.

  • Chit Funds Act 1982: Central Act administered by States; no Central rules; RBI deposit ban since 2009.
  • Chit subscriptions are not deposits.
  • MLM, pyramid and Ponzi schemes are banned by the 1978 Act and enforced by States.
  • CIS regulated by SEBI.

Closing line: A fraud that spans three regulators is a fraud that is hard to catch.

Explain the types of NBFCs and the conditions that define each.

Intro: NBFCs are classified by what they do and how large they are.

  • AFC and IC by principal business; IFC (75% infrastructure loans, ₹300 crore NOF, rating A, CRAR 15%).
  • IDF-NBFC, CIC, MFI (85%), Factors (50%/50%), MGC (90%, ₹100 crore).
  • NOFHC for promoters setting up a new bank.

Closing line: The label tells you the regulation.
