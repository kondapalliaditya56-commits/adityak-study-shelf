0 · Where these states fit#

Teacher's board

The teacher's note beside the title: [Part of the Vijayanagar empire, 1336-1646]. (Mysore's roots are in the Vijayanagar Empire; see section 6.)

  • These are the third class of regional States from Chapter 3: Already Autonomous States, where even when the Mughal was powerful he did not rule. The Rajputs were brought into the Mughal system by Akbar 56-05 but kept their home territories; Travancore was never part of the Mughal Empire; Mysore grew out of the Vijayanagar world.

2 · Rajputs against Aurangzeb 58-07, and the Marathas#

The 1680 Rajput revolt

Mewar expands: under Raj Singh, aggressively against neighbouring Rajput states
Aurangzeb 58-07 is uneasy: he did not want one Rajput power to dominate all Rajputana and rival the Mughals
1680, the trigger: a child comes to the throne in Marwar (astride the Delhi/Agra to Ahmedabad trade route)
Aurangzeb 58-07 interferes in the succession
Marwar and Mewar revolt
The rebellion collapses: the other Rajput chiefs refuse to join, fearing Mewar would dominate them
  • This arrangement held through most of the 17th century. Trouble came under Aurangzeb 58-07. Mewar, under Raj Singh, had been expanding aggressively against neighbouring Rajput states; Aurangzeb 58-07 grew uneasy, not wanting one Rajput power to dominate Rajputana and rival the Mughal State.
  • 1680: a child came to the throne in Marwar, a kingdom astride the important trade route connecting Delhi and Agra to Ahmedabad. Aurangzeb 58-07 interfered in the succession: the trigger. Marwar and Mewar both revolted, but the revolt failed. The other Rajput chiefs refused to join, fearing that if the revolt succeeded Mewar would dominate them.
  • After Aurangzeb 58-07's death in 1707 the Rajputs increasingly acted on their own terms. The most prominent figure was Sawai Jai Singh 99-43 of Amber, who ruled Jaipur and remained a powerful Mansabdar in Delhi, but on his own conditions.
  • From 1751 the Marathas began regular raids into Rajputana, extracting tribute and interfering in succession disputes, but could never fully conquer the region.
  • After the Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803-05) the EIC replaced the Marathas as the regional power and signed Subordinate Alliances (also called Subsidiary Alliances) with the Rajput states.
Teacher's board

Subordinate Alliance is like World History's Protectorate: the Indian ruler kept his throne but surrendered control of foreign policy and defence to the EIC, which stationed its own troops, paid for by the Indian ruler. Under suzerainty = loss of external sovereign functions plus loss of symbolic sovereignty. The teacher's ink: "will do in future" (the Subsidiary Alliance is taken up in Chapter 11).

3.1 The Jantar Mantars and astronomy

  • Five observatories (Jantar Mantars) were built at Delhi (1724), Jaipur (1734), Ujjain, Mathura and Varanasi. Jaipur's is the largest: a UNESCO World Heritage Site (2010). The Samrat Yantra there, the largest sundial in the world, could measure local time to an accuracy of 2 seconds.
  • Astronomical tables tell where a celestial body (sun, moon, planet, star) will be at any given date and time. The existing tables, the Zij-i-Ulugh Begi (Islamic) and the Siddhantic (Hindu), had accumulated errors over centuries. The Jantar Mantars generated fresh observational data to correct them in the form of the Zij-i-Muhammad Shahi (1733), dedicated to Muhammad Shah 19-48. He also sent a scientific delegation to Portugal (1730) and commissioned Sanskrit astronomical works.
  • He chose stone instruments over brass for greater accuracy:
InstrumentUse
Samrat YantraSundial for time
Jai Prakash YantraCelestial mapper
Ram YantraAltitude meter for the Sun's position

4 · Travancore: Marthanda Varma 29-58#

  • Travancore was never part of the Mughal Empire. The Mughal State simply never expanded far enough south to include it.
  • By the 1740s, Marthanda Varma 29-58 had built a strong, centralised State by subjugating local chiefs across the region, and a European-style military: modern weapons, trained soldiers.
  • Takeaway: Travancore established a State monopoly over the trade of the Malabar coast. The revenues were reinvested into the military, irrigation works and transport infrastructure: a classic example of a small State using trade income to build State capacity.

5 · Travancore against Mysore and the EIC#

  • Under Rama Varma 58-98 Travancore became a centre of art and scholarship, but military strength declined. The consequences were quick: in 1766 Haider Ali 61-82 of Mysore annexed Malabar and Calicut. (Malabar was the heart of the spice trade: pepper, cardamom, ginger. Whoever controlled Malabar's ports controlled a significant share of Indian Ocean commerce, which is why both Mysore and the British were so interested.)
  • After 1798 Travancore came steadily under British influence.

Teacher's board

A light note: Shashi Tharoor is from the Nair community. Dalawa meant Prime Minister and Chief of the Armed Forces, but books may use Dewan for the PM of a State subordinated by the British; as Dewan he was Head of all administration (revenue, civil, police, criminal justice), since the military was with the British. The handout adds: this was one of the earliest organised resistance movements by an Indian ruler against the Subsidiary Alliance system, and a directly tested topic in UPSC.

6 · Mysore: from Vijayanagar to Haider Ali 61-82#

  • Mysore was part of the Vijayanagar Empire (1336-1646). The Empire's effective power was shattered at the Battle of Talikota (also called Rakkasa-Tangadi) in 1565, when a coalition of Deccan Sultanates decisively defeated Vijayanagar. The dynasty lingered until 1646, but 1565 marks the operational end of Vijayanagar as a major power.
  • After Vijayanagar's decline Mysore gradually became autonomous under the Wodeyar dynasty. Under Chikka Devaraja 72-04 Wodeyar (1672-1704) it was militarily powerful and centrally governed. By 1761 the Wodeyars had been reduced to figureheads by a powerful and corrupt Prime Minister, NanjRaj.
  • Haider Ali 61-82 ended that. He began as a junior military officer, his early career made possible partly because the Wodeyars had helped pay off his family's debts. Capable, ambitious and strategic, in 1761 he overthrew NanjRaj and seized effective power, keeping the Wodeyar as a nominal head of State.
Handout alert

Pattern alert at the 18th century: the king becomes a puppet as personality weakens. Recall the Peshwa and Chhatrapati relationship (Chapter 4).

6.1 Haider Ali 61-82's military machine

  • Haider Ali 61-82 took French assistance for military training, technology transfer and organisational reform. The army was now on a European model, organised into Risalas (regiments):
  • Each Risala's commander was directly appointed by the King, ensuring personal loyalty upward.
  • Each unit had a clear chain of command.
  • Each was self-sufficient: its own weapons, transport and supplies (recall the Campoo system of Scindia, Chapter 4).
  • Result: a fast, efficient military with regiments capable of independent action.
  • He then spent roughly twenty years subjugating the Poligars (local chiefs and landlords who controlled land, the wealth of temples and had their own armed men, the real rulers of the countryside since the Vijayanagar era) and the Deshmukhs (revenue collectors who functioned like primary zamindars). Non-State power was systematically dismantled.

6.2 How Mysore funded this machine: Military Fiscalism

PolicyEffect
Khalisa land expandedMore revenue went directly to the State treasury
Jagirdari minimisedMaximised State income, reducing the nobles' independent economic power
Direct collectionDeshmukhs removed as intermediaries; State officials collected taxes and got salaries (far smaller than commissions)
Detailed field surveysAssessed LR by soil fertility, crop type, irrigation access and past production trends; prevented over-assessment
Investing in agricultureCanals repaired and built; cash crops promoted; sericulture introduced
Boosted tax collectionMore peasant income meant a larger tax base; even enemies admitted Mysore's population was among the most prosperous
  • These policies are called Military Fiscalism: the systematic generation of State wealth as a foundation for military power. (The handout's analogy: replacing a commission-based agent with a salaried employee; the State kept far more of the revenue.)
  • Khalisa land = land whose revenue went directly to the State treasury. Jagirdari land = land whose revenue was assigned to nobles or commanders.

7 · Tipu 82-99 Sultan (1782-99): innovations and why he still lost#

  • Modern navy and trading company: a government-owned trading company modelled on the EIC, trading in sandalwood, sulphur, rice, pepper, silk and cardamom, with 30 trading centres inside and outside India, including Muscat, Oman. (Before the age of machines a "factory" meant a warehouse or trading depot, not a production facility; a trading settlement meant warehouses, homes, offices and sometimes a fort. Details in Chapter 8.)
  • Iron-cased rocket artillery: developed by Haider Ali 61-82 and Tipu 82-99, 100% indigenous, range 2 km; the iron cylinder casings did not burst and could take high pressure compared with cardboard-cased rockets (fireworks), which existed for centuries in India and China. The British studied them closely: William Congreve's rockets, deployed in the early 19th century, drew directly from Tipu 82-99's designs.
  • 1785: Tipu 82-99 formally declared independence from both the Wodeyars and the Mughal Emperor, and requested a sanad from the Khalifa recognising him as independent of Mughal authority.
  • Modern embassies in France, Turkey and Egypt, alongside new coinage and the Mauludi Lunisolar Calendar.

7.1 Tipu 82-99 and Revolutionary France

  • In the 1790s Tipu 82-99 sent an ambassador to France seeking military technology and a formal alliance against the British. Since 1789 the French Revolution had set France on a collision course with the monarchies of Europe, including Britain (the 23-year wars, 1792-1815). In 1798 Napoleon attacked Egypt and the Middle East, partly to disrupt British trade in the Arabian Sea.
  • Tipu 82-99 exchanged communications with Napoleon and planted a Tree of Liberty in Mysore (a symbol of the French Revolution), and joined the Jacobin Club. The alliance did not materialise into military support. The British were alarmed, and it became a reason for the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War of 1799.
Handout alert

Prelims 2001: who established embassies in foreign countries on modern lines? Answer (d): Tipu 82-99 Sultan. He set up modern embassies in France, Turkey and Egypt, alongside new coinage and the Mauludi Lunisolar Calendar. The Jacobin membership and Tree of Liberty marked him in British eyes as an ideological adversary, not merely a military one.

7.2 Why Mysore lost

  • Mysore's long-term ambition was to dominate all of South India and control its international trade (Vijayanagar 2.0). But constant warfare against neighbouring Indian powers and the EIC drained the State's resources faster than Military Fiscalism could replenish them. The ambition was real; the capacity, ultimately, was not enough.
  • 1766: Haider Ali 61-82 annexed Malabar and Calicut, triggering the First Anglo-Mysore War (1767-69), as the EIC feared losing coastal trade access through Malabar. Four Anglo-Mysore Wars: 1767-69, 1780-84, 1790-92, 1799. The Fourth (1799) ended with Tipu 82-99's death at Seringapatam; Mysore's power was broken.
Note

Flag on Travancore: the handout's section 7 says that in 1766 Haider Ali 61-82 "had invaded Travancore and annexed Malabar and Calicut", while the same chapter (section 4) says he annexed Malabar and Calicut, and the teacher's bracket on the slide adds "could not successfully invade Travancore." Use the slide's wording: Malabar and Calicut were annexed; Travancore was not successfully invaded.
