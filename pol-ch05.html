1 · Introduction, history and the six Fundamental Rights#

The Fundamental Rights (FRs) are basic human freedoms guaranteed in Part III (Articles 12-35). Inspired largely by the American Bill of Rights, they are called the "Magna Carta of India" because they shield citizens from arbitrary State action.

Why "fundamental"

(1) guaranteed by the fundamental law of the land, the Constitution; (2) essential for the all-round development of individuals (material, intellectual, moral, spiritual); (3) limitations on State power, giving a government of laws, not of men.

They make India's democracy not only political but rooted in justice, liberty, equality and fraternity (Preamble). FRs are justiciable: any person can approach the Supreme Court under Article 32 or the High Courts under Article 226. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called Article 32 the "heart and soul of the Constitution".

Historical background

How the FRs arrived

Early demands
Bal Gangadhar Tilak stressed civil liberties ("Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it"); the Indian National Congress pressed for freedom of speech, press and association from the late 19th century
Nehru Report (1928)
First Indian constitutional document to explicitly include a Bill of Rights for citizens: equality, freedom of expression, protection from discrimination
Global influences
American Bill of Rights (1791): enforceable FRs; French Declaration of Rights of Man (1789): equality and liberty; Irish Constitution (1937): directive principles concept
Objective Resolution (1946)
Moved by Jawaharlal Nehru; laid the foundation, promising equality and freedom
Constituent Assembly
Ambedkar, K.M. Munshi and Sardar Patel argued for enforceable FRs against misuse of power by future governments; despite concern that "abstract rights" may hinder governance, the Assembly held guaranteed rights essential for citizens' dignity
1950
Part III adopted seven FRs
1978
44th Amendment removed the Right to Property (Art. 31) as an FR; it became a legal right under Art. 300A
Sources of the FR idea
TNAFI

Tilak, Nehru Report 1928, American Bill of Rights 1791, French Declaration 1789, Irish Constitution 1937: "Time Never Allows Forgetting Ideas".

Present status

Originally seven FRs; Right to Property (Art. 31) was removed in 1978 by the 44th Amendment and became a legal right under Art. 300-A. Today six FRs exist.

3 · Article 12: who is the "State"#

Article 12 defines "State" for Part III only. This matters because FRs are mainly enforceable against the State, not private individuals (limited exceptions like Arts. 17 and 23).

State under Art. 12

(1) Government and Parliament of India (Union executive and legislature); (2) Government and Legislature of each State; (3) all local authorities within India or under central control; (4) other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India.

Local authorities and "other authorities"

  • Local authorities (Section 3(31), General Clauses Act): municipalities, district boards, panchayats, improvement trusts, mining boards.
  • Other authorities is open-ended, evolved by courts: bodies that are instrumentalities or agencies of the government, even if not constituted directly by statute. Examples: statutory corporations and government-controlled societies.
Tests for "other authorities"
TestCase
Instrumentality or agency testAjay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib, 1981
Deep and pervasive State control testPradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology, 2002
Public function test(no case named in the handout)

The tests are not absolute or automatic; courts examine the overall context and functions. Hence PSUs and government-owned corporations are not automatically "State"; they count only if courts, after applying the tests, say so.

Article 12 tests
APP

Is the judiciary "State"?

Judiciary and Art. 12
CaseHolding

Private entities

Private bodies are not automatically "State", but the SC has evolved doctrines to bring them in when:

  • i. a private entity performs public functions of great importance closely related to governmental duties;
  • ii. there is deep and pervasive State control over its functioning, finance and policy;
  • iii. it is an instrumentality or agency of the State (even though legally a private corporation).
Why it matters

The ambit of Art. 12 decides against whom FRs are enforceable through the SC's writ jurisdiction under Article 32.

4 · Article 13: laws that violate Fundamental Rights#

Article 13 is the backbone of FR protection: any law made by the State that violates Part III is null and void to the extent of the violation.

Article 13 clauses
ClauseRule
13(1)Pre-Constitution laws: any law existing before 26 January 1950 that violates FRs becomes invalid from that day
13(2)Post-Constitution laws: State cannot make a law that takes away or limits FRs; if passed, void to the extent of conflict
13(3)Meaning of "law": (a) permanent laws of Parliament or State legislatures; (b) temporary laws such as ordinances of the President or Governors; (c) delegated legislation: order, byelaw, rule, regulation, notification; (d) non-legislative sources: custom or usage having the force of law
13(4)Constitutional amendments: added by the 24th Amendment Act, 1971; amendments under Art. 368 are not "law" under Art. 13. But Kesavananda Bharati (1973) held amendments can still be reviewed if they destroy the Basic Structure
Art. 13 clauses
PPMC

Pre-1950 laws, Post-1950 laws, Meaning of law, Constitutional amendments: "Police Patrol Main Corridors".

What counts as "law"
PTDN

Permanent laws, Temporary (ordinances), Delegated legislation, Non-legislative custom: "People Trust Daily Norms".

Judicial review under Article 13

Judicial review means courts check whether laws of the legislature or actions of the executive violate the Constitution. The words "judicial review" are not explicitly in the Constitution, but Art. 13 makes clear courts can strike down laws violating FRs, making the judiciary the guardian of FRs.

Constitutional amendments and Art. 13

They are not "law" within Art. 13, but remain subject to judicial review if they violate the Basic Structure.

Exceptions to Article 13

5 · Article 14: equality before law#

Art. 14: "The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India." Available to all persons (citizens and foreigners); "person" includes legal persons such as companies, corporations, societies.

Two dimensions of Article 14
AspectEquality before lawEqual protection of laws
OriginBritishAmerican
NatureNegative conceptPositive concept
IdeaNo special privileges; all equally bound by the same laws and courts; absence of arbitrary discrimination; "Rule of Law", no one above the lawEqual treatment of equals; State may make reasonable classifications and take affirmative steps
ExampleMinister or ordinary citizen committing the same offence faces the same legal process; President to ordinary citizen under the same laws and courtsSpecial provisions for socially and economically weaker sections (SCs, STs, women) to bring them at par
Origins
BNAP

British = Negative (before law); American = Positive (equal protection): "Britain No, America Plus".

Reasonable classification vs class legislation

Art. 14 forbids class legislation (arbitrary treatment on irrelevant grounds) but permits reasonable classification of persons, objects or situations, provided:

  • 1. there is an intelligible differentia (a real, substantial distinction between groups);
  • 2. there is a rational nexus between the difference and the object sought by the law.

E.g., special laws for women, children, backward classes or weaker sections are valid classification. Unequals may be treated differently if classification has a reasonable basis and valid purpose (the SC's test of reasonable classification).

Classification test
IR

Intelligible differentia + Rational nexus: "If Reasonable".

Rule of Law (A.V. Dicey) and Article 14

Equality before law links to the Rule of Law, developed by British jurist A.V. Dicey: law is supreme and applies equally to all.

Dicey's three elements
ElementMeaningIn India
1. Absence of arbitrary powerNo one punished unless a law is broken; government action must rest on law, not personal will. E.g., arrest only by legal procedure, not an officer's whimFollowed
2. Equality before lawAll, rich or poor, official or not, under the same laws and ordinary courts; no special courts for any group. E.g., civil servant and citizen tried under the same criminal lawFollowed
3. Primacy of individual rightsIn Britain rights are protected by courts as common law, not a written constitutionNot applicable: replaced by constitutional guarantee of FRs

The SC has declared the Rule of Law part of the Basic Structure: it cannot be abolished or diluted even by constitutional amendment. Art. 14 plus the Rule of Law ensure law, not the will of individuals, governs the State.

Formal equality

  • Equality in law
  • law applies to all without distinction
  • treats everyone the same regardless of actual conditions
  • E.g., "one person, one vote"

Substantive equality

  • Recognises social and economic inequalities and seeks to remove them
  • needs positive State action (reservations, protective discrimination, welfare)
  • E.g., reservation for SCs, STs, OBCs and women

6 · Article 15 and the 103rd Amendment#

Art. 15 prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. Two key words: "discrimination" (an unfavourable distinction or treating someone less favourably) and "only" (unconstitutional only if based exclusively on the listed grounds; other valid factors such as merit or physical fitness make the classification valid).

  • Introduced by the SC in Indra Sawhney (Mandal Commission, 1992) to exclude the socially advanced/affluent section of OBCs.
  • Income/occupation criteria notified by Government from time to time; applicants need valid NCL certificates.
  • Rationale: benefits reach the truly backward; prevent elite capture within OBCs.

  • Central List: proposed by States/UTs/NCBC, scrutinised by NCBC, notified by the President (Art. 342A).

Problem: a few relatively advanced OBC communities capture a disproportionate share of the 27%, while the most backward (smaller, dispersed, nomadic/denotified) struggle to benefit.

  • Rohini Commission (2017- ), Justice G. Rohini: set up to study the skew and recommend fairer distribution.
  • It examined admission/recruitment data on who actually uses OBC reservation; noted methodological gaps (absence of fresh, granular socio-educational data); explored splitting the 27% quota into layers, e.g., "backward / more backward / most backward", each with a fixed share of seats/jobs.
  • Legal backdrop: Indra Sawhney (1992) permits sub-classification within backward classes for real equality, if the framework stays reasonable and evidence-based.
  • State models: Tamil Nadu's MBC, Karnataka's 2A/2B/3A/3B, Andhra Pradesh's categories; workable approach: earmark sub-shares, apply non-creamy layer uniformly, monitor uptake.
  • Challenges are practical: defining tiers credibly across diverse states, allocating sub-percentages without breaching the 50% ceiling, avoiding fragmentation, updating lists as socio-economic status changes.

  • Up to 10% in initial appointments to civil posts and services; operationalised by a January 2019 order for all direct recruitment under the Union.

10 · Articles 17 and 18: untouchability and titles#

Article 17

"Untouchability is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden. The enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law."

  • Practice of untouchability in any form is completely banned; anyone enforcing it is guilty of an offence.
  • Parliament, under Article 35, has power to make laws prescribing the punishment.

Meaning

  • The Constitution does not define untouchability. Courts clarified it is not literal avoidance of touch but the caste-based social practice of exclusion and discrimination against certain groups.
  • E.g., denying entry to temples, refusing service in shops/hotels, prohibiting use of public wells and roads.

Freedom of assembly: Art. 19(1)(b)

Right to assemble peacefully and without arms: public meetings, demonstrations, processions, hunger strikes. A necessary corollary of free speech; recognised internationally (Article 21, UDHR). Cannot be exercised on government premises or private property without consent.

  • T.K. Rangarajan v. State of Tamil Nadu (2003): government employees have no fundamental right to strike (strikes cause hardship to the public and disrupt essential services).
  • Courts: assemblies must be peaceful; violent or armed gatherings are not protected.
  • Art. 19(3) restrictions: (1) sovereignty and integrity of India (to prevent assemblies threatening national unity); (2) public order (to prevent assemblies disturbing peace, law or social harmony). Must be reasonable and proportionate.

12 · Article 20: protection in criminal law#

Art. 20 has 3 clauses dealing with arbitrary action by the Legislature, Executive and implementing authorities. It applies only to criminal laws (not civil or tax laws). Available to citizens and foreigners, but only against conviction (not preventive detention).

The three clauses
ClauseProtectionRule
20(1)Ex-post-facto lawsNo conviction except for violating a law in force at the time of the act; no penalty greater than the law then in force allowed
20(2)Double jeopardyNo person prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once
20(3)Self-incriminationNo person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself
Art. 20 clauses
EDS

Ex-post-facto (1), Double jeopardy (2), Self-incrimination (3): "Every Day Safe".

20(1): ex-post-facto laws

  • An ex-post-facto law imposes penalties or enhances punishment retroactively; Art. 20(1) prohibits such enactment and application in criminal law.
  • Limited to criminal laws: retrospective civil or tax laws are permitted (a tax law may be retrospective; criminal liability cannot be created or enhanced retrospectively).
  • Prohibits only conviction and sentence under a retrospective criminal law, not the trial: a changed trial procedure may apply to past offences.
  • Does not apply to preventive detention or security-from-a-person measures (preventive, not punitive).
  • Retrospective law is valid if it reduces punishment or benefits the accused.

20(2): double jeopardy

  • Once convicted or acquitted for an offence, no further trial or punishment for the same offence on the same facts. Source: common-law maxim "Nemo debet bis vexari" (no one should be put twice in peril for the same offence). Upholds finality of judgment and fairness; prevents harassment and abuse of process.
  • Two conditions to claim it: (1) prosecuted once before a court of law or judicial tribunal; (2) punished for that offence as a result.
  • If the earlier proceeding did not end in punishment (investigation or departmental inquiry) it cannot be invoked.
  • Limited to judicial proceedings: not departmental, disciplinary or administrative proceedings. A public servant facing departmental action can still be prosecuted in a criminal court for the same act.

20(3): self-incrimination

  • An accused cannot be forced, coerced or induced to give incriminating evidence; preserves voluntary confessions; a shield during investigation and trial.
  • Available only to formally accused persons (not witnesses or others questioned in investigation); only in criminal proceedings.
  • Covers oral and documentary evidence; does not cover physical or mechanical (non-testimonial) evidence:

13 · Articles 21 and 21A: life, liberty and education#

Article 21

"No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to the procedure established by law."

  • Right to life and personal liberty of every person (citizens and foreigners). Paramount among FRs, taking precedence over other attributes of political and social order. Enforceable through Arts. 32 and 226, including PILs, extending to collective rights.
  • The sanctity of human life is probably the most fundamental of human social values; recognised in all civilised legal systems and international human-rights statements; a successful democracy guarantees the right to protect one's life and liberty.

Overruled Gopalan: procedure must be fair, just and reasonable, effectively importing due process of law; protects against arbitrary executive and legislative action; life means dignity and worth, not mere animal existence; "personal liberty" of the widest amplitude
Since
The SC has reaffirmed Maneka Gandhi and enlarged Art. 21 into many derivative rights

Article 32 vs Article 226

SC jurisdiction under Art. 32 is original (petitioner approaches directly) but not exclusive: it is concurrent with HC writ jurisdiction under Art. 226. The SC cannot refuse Art. 32 when an FR violation is shown; HCs may be more accessible for speedy local relief. A petitioner may go to either.

21 · Property and the shields of 31A, 31B, 31C#

History of the right to property

1950
One of the seven FRs. Art. 19(1)(f): every citizen's right to acquire, hold and dispose of property. Art. 31: no person (citizen or not) deprived of property except by authority of law, for a public purpose and with compensation. Became the most controversial FR (land reform, zamindari abolition, compensation disputes): constant legislature-judiciary conflict
1951
1st Amendment (with 39th, 42nd later): protect agrarian and socio-economic laws via Arts. 31A, 31B, 31C and the Ninth Schedule
1955
4th Amendment: adequacy of compensation cannot be challenged in courts
1971
25th Amendment: word "compensation" replaced by "amount"; further reduced judicial scrutiny
1978
44th Amendment: abolished property as an FR; Arts. 19(1)(f) and 31 repealed; Art. 300A inserted in Part XII: "No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law"
2007
I.R. Coelho: Ninth Schedule laws placed after 24 April 1973 (Kesavananda date) can be reviewed if they violate FRs forming part of the Basic Structure
Property amendments in order

1st (1951), 4th (1955), 25th (1971), 39th, 42nd, 44th (1978): read it as 1-4-25-39-42-44. The first five protected agrarian and socio-economic laws or cut judicial scrutiny; the 44th ended the Fundamental Right.
