In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • Part III (Articles 12-35) holds the Fundamental Rights (FRs): basic freedoms that shield you from the State, and courts can enforce them (justiciable).
  • There are six FRs today: Equality, Freedom, against Exploitation, Religion, Cultural-Educational and Constitutional Remedies. Property left Part III in 1978 and is now a legal right under Art. 300A.
  • A law that breaks an FR is void (Art. 13). Even a constitutional amendment cannot destroy the Basic Structure (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
  • FRs are not absolute: the State may set reasonable restrictions (for example Art. 19(2) to 19(6)). In an Emergency most can be suspended, but Arts. 20 and 21 cannot.
  • If an FR is violated, go to the Supreme Court under Art. 32 or a High Court under Art. 226. Five writs do the work: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto.
  • Reservation sits in Arts. 15 and 16: SC 15, ST 7.5, OBC 27, EWS 10 percent, with the 50% cap from Indra Sawhney (not absolute, says Janhit Abhiyan 2022).

Key terms

Can be enforced in court; the SC (Art. 32) and HCs (Art. 226) issue writs
State (Art. 12)
Union and State governments and legislatures, local authorities and other authorities; FRs mainly bind the State

A law that takes away or limits an FR is invalid to the extent of the conflict

Allowed under Art. 14 if there is an intelligible differentia and a rational nexus with the law's object
Creamy layer
Advanced OBC families kept out of the 27% quota (Indra Sawhney, 1992)

A law that punishes an act retrospectively; barred in criminal law by Art. 20(1)

Detention without trial to stop a future offence; beyond 3 months needs an Advisory Board (Art. 22)

1 · Introduction, history and the six Fundamental Rights#

  • The Fundamental Rights (FRs) are basic human freedoms guaranteed in Part III (Articles 12-35). Inspired largely by the American Bill of Rights, they are called the "Magna Carta of India" because they shield citizens from arbitrary State action.
  • Why "fundamental":
    • (1) guaranteed by the fundamental law of the land, the Constitution
    • (2) essential for the all-round development of individuals (material, intellectual, moral, spiritual)
    • (3) limitations on State power, giving a government of laws, not of men
  • They make India's democracy not only political but rooted in justice, liberty, equality and fraternity (Preamble). FRs are justiciable: any person can approach the Supreme Court under Article 32 or the High Courts under Article 226. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called Article 32 the "heart and soul of the Constitution".

Historical background

How the FRs arrived

  • Early demands:
    • Bal Gangadhar Tilak stressed civil liberties ("Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it")
    • The Indian National Congress pressed for freedom of speech, press and association from the late 19th century
  • Nehru Report (1928): First Indian constitutional document to explicitly include a Bill of Rights for citizens: equality, freedom of expression, protection from discrimination
  • Global influences:
    • American Bill of Rights (1791): enforceable FRs
    • French Declaration of Rights of Man (1789): equality and liberty
    • Irish Constitution (1937): directive principles concept
  • Objective Resolution (1946):
    • Moved by Jawaharlal Nehru
    • Laid the foundation, promising equality and freedom
  • Constituent Assembly:
    • Ambedkar, K.M. Munshi and Sardar Patel argued for enforceable FRs against misuse of power by future governments
    • Despite concern that "abstract rights" may hinder governance, the Assembly held guaranteed rights essential for citizens' dignity
  • 1950: Part III adopted seven FRs
  • 1978:
    • 44th Amendment removed the Right to Property (Art. 31) as an FR
    • It became a legal right under Art. 300A
How the FRs arrived
  • Memory aid (Sources of the FR idea): Tilak, Nehru Report 1928, American Bill of Rights 1791, French Declaration 1789, Irish Constitution 1937: "Time Never Allows Forgetting Ideas".

Present status

  • Originally seven FRs
  • Right to Property (Art. 31) was removed in 1978 by the 44th Amendment and became a legal right under Art. 300-A. Today six FRs exist

3 · Article 12: who is the "State"#

  • Article 12 defines "State" for Part III only. This matters because FRs are mainly enforceable against the State, not private individuals (limited exceptions like Arts. 17 and 23).
  • State under Art. 12:
    • (1) Government and Parliament of India (Union executive and legislature)
    • (2) Government and Legislature of each State
    • (3) all local authorities within India or under central control
    • (4) other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India

Local authorities and "other authorities"

  • Local authorities (Section 3(31), General Clauses Act): municipalities, district boards, panchayats, improvement trusts, mining boards.
  • Other authorities is open-ended, evolved by courts: bodies that are instrumentalities or agencies of the government, even if not constituted directly by statute. Examples: statutory corporations and government-controlled societies.

Tests for "other authorities"

TestCase
Instrumentality or agency testAjay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib, 1981
Deep and pervasive State control testPradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology, 2002
Public function test(no case named in the handout)
  • The tests are not absolute or automatic
  • Courts examine the overall context and functions. Hence PSUs and government-owned corporations are not automatically "State"
  • They count only if courts, after applying the tests, say so
  • Memory aid (Article 12 tests): Ajay Hasia 1981 = instrumentality, Pradeep Kumar Biswas 2002 = deep and pervasive control, Public function: "All Public Powers".

Is the judiciary "State"?

Private entities

  • Private bodies are not automatically "State", but the SC has evolved doctrines to bring them in when:
  • i. a private entity performs public functions of great importance closely related to governmental duties;
  • ii. there is deep and pervasive State control over its functioning, finance and policy;
  • iii. it is an instrumentality or agency of the State (even though legally a private corporation).
  • Why it matters: The ambit of Art. 12 decides against whom FRs are enforceable through the SC's writ jurisdiction under Article 32.
  • Memory aid (Private body as State): Public function, Deep control, Instrumentality: "Public Duties Invite" scrutiny.

4 · Article 13: laws that violate Fundamental Rights#

  • Article 13 is the backbone of FR protection: any law made by the State that violates Part III is null and void to the extent of the violation.

Article 13 clauses

ClauseRule
13(1)Pre-Constitution laws: any law existing before 26 January 1950 that violates FRs becomes invalid from that day
13(2)Post-Constitution laws: State cannot make a law that takes away or limits FRs; if passed, void to the extent of conflict
13(3)Meaning of "law": (a) permanent laws of Parliament or State legislatures; (b) temporary laws such as ordinances of the President or Governors; (c) delegated legislation: order, byelaw, rule, regulation, notification; (d) non-legislative sources: custom or usage having the force of law
13(4)Constitutional amendments: added by the 24th Amendment Act, 1971; amendments under Art. 368 are not "law" under Art. 13. But Kesavananda Bharati (1973) held amendments can still be reviewed if they destroy the Basic Structure
  • Memory aid (Art. 13 clauses): Pre-1950 laws, Post-1950 laws, Meaning of law, Constitutional amendments: "Police Patrol Main Corridors".
  • Memory aid (What counts as "law"): Permanent laws, Temporary (ordinances), Delegated legislation, Non-legislative custom: "People Trust Daily Norms".

Judicial review under Article 13

  • Judicial review means courts check whether laws of the legislature or actions of the executive violate the Constitution. The words "judicial review" are not explicitly in the Constitution, but Art. 13 makes clear courts can strike down laws violating FRs, making the judiciary the guardian of FRs.
  • Constitutional amendments and Art. 13: They are not "law" within Art. 13, but remain subject to judicial review if they violate the Basic Structure.

Exceptions to Article 13

Where Art. 13 does not fully apply

ExceptionDetail
Constitutional amendments, Art. 13(4)An amendment under Art. 368 is not "law" (24th Amendment, 1971). Not unlimited: Kesavananda Bharati (1973) says Parliament cannot change the basic structure (e.g., judicial review, rule of law, federalism, free and fair elections). Amendments escape Art. 13 directly but can be struck down if they damage the basic structure

To undo a judgment's effect the legislature must pass a new or validating law fixing the defect the court pointed out (e.g., changing the statute); it cannot simply declare the judgment wrong. The underlying law is what gets tested

  • Art. 358:
    • Only for war or external aggression
    • Art. 19 stops operating automatically
    • Protection ends with Emergency
  • Art. 359:
    • President suspends the right to move court for named FRs
    • Remedy blocked, not the right
    • Never Arts. 20 and 21

5 · Article 14: equality before law#

  • Art. 14:
    • "The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India." Available to all persons (citizens and foreigners)
    • "person" includes legal persons such as companies, corporations, societies

Two dimensions of Article 14

AspectEquality before lawEqual protection of laws
OriginBritishAmerican
NatureNegative conceptPositive concept
IdeaNo special privileges; all equally bound by the same laws and courts; absence of arbitrary discrimination; "Rule of Law", no one above the lawEqual treatment of equals; State may make reasonable classifications and take affirmative steps
ExampleMinister or ordinary citizen committing the same offence faces the same legal process; President to ordinary citizen under the same laws and courtsSpecial provisions for socially and economically weaker sections (SCs, STs, women) to bring them at par
  • Memory aid (Origins):
    • British = Negative (before law)
    • American = Positive (equal protection): "Britain No, America Plus"

Reasonable classification vs class legislation

  • Art. 14 forbids class legislation (arbitrary treatment on irrelevant grounds) but permits reasonable classification of persons, objects or situations, provided:
  • 1. there is an intelligible differentia (a real, substantial distinction between groups);
  • 2. there is a rational nexus between the difference and the object sought by the law.
  • E.g., special laws for women, children, backward classes or weaker sections are valid classification. Unequals may be treated differently if classification has a reasonable basis and valid purpose (the SC's test of reasonable classification).
  • Memory aid (Classification test): Intelligible differentia + Rational nexus: "If Reasonable".

Rule of Law (A.V. Dicey) and Article 14

  • Equality before law links to the Rule of Law, developed by British jurist A.V. Dicey: law is supreme and applies equally to all.

Dicey's three elements

ElementMeaningIn India
1. Absence of arbitrary powerNo one punished unless a law is broken; government action must rest on law, not personal will. E.g., arrest only by legal procedure, not an officer's whimFollowed
2. Equality before lawAll, rich or poor, official or not, under the same laws and ordinary courts; no special courts for any group. E.g., civil servant and citizen tried under the same criminal lawFollowed
3. Primacy of individual rightsIn Britain rights are protected by courts as common law, not a written constitutionNot applicable: replaced by constitutional guarantee of FRs
  • The SC has declared the Rule of Law part of the Basic Structure: it cannot be abolished or diluted even by constitutional amendment. Art. 14 plus the Rule of Law ensure law, not the will of individuals, governs the State.

Formal vs substantive equality

  • Formal equality:
    • Equality in law
    • Law applies to all without distinction
    • Treats everyone the same regardless of actual conditions
    • E.g., "one person, one vote"
  • Substantive equality:
    • Recognises social and economic inequalities and seeks to remove them
    • Needs positive State action (reservations, protective discrimination, welfare)
    • E.g., reservation for SCs, STs, OBCs and women

Exceptions to equality under Art. 14

  • Art. 14 is not absolute.

Non-Creamy Layer (NCL)

  • Introduced by the SC in Indra Sawhney (Mandal Commission, 1992) to exclude the socially advanced/affluent section of OBCs.

  • Applicants need valid NCL certificates
  • Rationale:
    • Benefits reach the truly backward
    • Prevent elite capture within OBCs

Identification and inclusion

  • Central List: proposed by States/UTs/NCBC, scrutinised by NCBC, notified by the President (Art. 342A).

  • Problem: a few relatively advanced OBC communities capture a disproportionate share of the 27%, while the most backward (smaller, dispersed, nomadic/denotified) struggle to benefit.

  • It examined admission/recruitment data on who actually uses OBC reservation

  • State models:
    • Tamil Nadu's MBC, Karnataka's 2A/2B/3A/3B, Andhra Pradesh's categories
    • Workable approach: earmark sub-shares, apply non-creamy layer uniformly, monitor uptake
  • Challenges are practical: defining tiers credibly across diverse states, allocating sub-percentages without breaching the 50% ceiling, avoiding fragmentation, updating lists as socio-economic status changes.

10 · Articles 17 and 18: untouchability and titles#

  • Article 17: "Untouchability is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden. The enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law."
  • Practice of untouchability in any form is completely banned
  • Anyone enforcing it is guilty of an offence
  • Parliament, under Article 35, has power to make laws prescribing the punishment.

Meaning

  • The Constitution does not define untouchability. Courts clarified it is not literal avoidance of touch but the caste-based social practice of exclusion and discrimination against certain groups.
  • E.g., denying entry to temples, refusing service in shops/hotels, prohibiting use of public wells and roads.

Laws enforcing Art. 17

  • Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955:
    • First law passed by Parliament to enforce Art. 17
    • Declared acts of untouchability crimes
  • Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 (after 1976 amendment):
    • Renamed version of the 1955 Act
    • Wider scope to protect all civil rights of SCs against untouchability-based discrimination
  • SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989:
    • Special law against atrocities, violence and exploitation of SCs and STs with stringent penalties
    • Created special courts for speedy trial

Judicial interpretation of Art. 17

  • Mysore High Court: Untouchability refers to the historical practice of caste discrimination, not every form of social boycott
  • Supreme Court: The right against untouchability is enforceable against the State and private individuals
  • Memory aid (Laws under Art. 17): Untouchability (Offences) Act 1955, Protection of Civil Rights Act (1976 renamed), Atrocities Act 1989: "Under Parliament's Article".

Article 18: abolition of titles

  • Art. 18 abolishes titles to uphold equality of status promised in the Preamble. Four provisions:

Rationale

  • Under British rule, titles like Rai Bahadur, Khan Bahadur, Maharaja created loyal classes and divided society. The framers wanted equality, dignity and democratic spirit.

What is allowed

  • Universities and other public institutions may confer titles or honours (e.g., honorary degrees).
  • State may award military distinctions (e.g., Param Vir Chakra) or academic distinctions (e.g., degrees).
  • State may give awards for merit or public service if not used as hereditary titles.

Reasonableness vs proportionality

  • Test of reasonableness:
    • Used by courts when a law limits an FR (esp. Art. 19)
    • Is it reasonable? A fair balance between individual freedom and society's interests

  • Test of proportionality:
    • Stricter, modern test borrowed from European constitutional law
    • Checks if State action goes too far. Four questions: legitimate goal
    • Suitable measure
    • Least restrictive way

12 · Article 20: protection in criminal law#

  • Art. 20 has 3 clauses dealing with arbitrary action by the Legislature, Executive and implementing authorities. It applies only to criminal laws (not civil or tax laws). Available to citizens and foreigners, but only against conviction (not preventive detention).

The three clauses

ClauseProtectionRule
20(1)Ex-post-facto lawsNo conviction except for violating a law in force at the time of the act; no penalty greater than the law then in force allowed
20(2)Double jeopardyNo person prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once
20(3)Self-incriminationNo person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself
  • Memory aid (Art. 20 clauses): Ex-post-facto (1), Double jeopardy (2), Self-incrimination (3): "Every Day Safe".

20(1): ex-post-facto laws

  • An ex-post-facto law imposes penalties or enhances punishment retroactively
  • Art. 20(1) prohibits such enactment and application in criminal law
  • Limited to criminal laws: retrospective civil or tax laws are permitted (a tax law may be retrospective; criminal liability cannot be created or enhanced retrospectively).
  • Prohibits only conviction and sentence under a retrospective criminal law, not the trial: a changed trial procedure may apply to past offences.
  • Does not apply to preventive detention or security-from-a-person measures (preventive, not punitive).
  • Retrospective law is valid if it reduces punishment or benefits the accused.

20(2): double jeopardy

  • Once convicted or acquitted for an offence, no further trial or punishment for the same offence on the same facts. Source: common-law maxim "Nemo debet bis vexari" (no one should be put twice in peril for the same offence). Upholds finality of judgment and fairness
  • Prevents harassment and abuse of process
  • Two conditions to claim it:
    • (1) prosecuted once before a court of law or judicial tribunal
    • (2) punished for that offence as a result
  • If the earlier proceeding did not end in punishment (investigation or departmental inquiry) it cannot be invoked.
  • Limited to judicial proceedings: not departmental, disciplinary or administrative proceedings. A public servant facing departmental action can still be prosecuted in a criminal court for the same act.

20(3): self-incrimination

  • An accused cannot be forced, coerced or induced to give incriminating evidence
  • Preserves voluntary confessions
  • A shield during investigation and trial
  • Available only to formally accused persons (not witnesses or others questioned in investigation)
  • Only in criminal proceedings
  • Covers oral and documentary evidence
  • Does not cover physical or mechanical (non-testimonial) evidence:

13 · Articles 21 and 21A: life, liberty and education#

  • Article 21: "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to the procedure established by law."
  • Right to life and personal liberty of every person (citizens and foreigners). Paramount among FRs, taking precedence over other attributes of political and social order. Enforceable through Arts. 32 and 226, including PILs, extending to collective rights.
  • The sanctity of human life is probably the most fundamental of human social values
  • Recognised in all civilised legal systems and international human-rights statements
  • A successful democracy guarantees the right to protect one's life and liberty

Judicial evolution of Art. 21

  • A.K. Gopalan (1950):
    • Narrow reading: protection only against arbitrary executive action, not legislative action
    • Upheld "procedure established by law" as different from American "due process of law"
    • Any validly enacted procedure was valid even if unfair, unreasonable or unjust
  • Maneka Gandhi (1978):
    • Overruled Gopalan: procedure must be fair, just and reasonable, effectively importing due process of law
    • Protects against arbitrary executive and legislative action
    • Life means dignity and worth, not mere animal existence
    • "personal liberty" of the widest amplitude
  • Since: The SC has reaffirmed Maneka Gandhi and enlarged Art. 21 into many derivative rights

Gopalan vs Maneka Gandhi

  • A.K. Gopalan (1950):
    • Procedure established by law
    • Against executive action only
    • Any enacted procedure valid
    • Articles read separately
  • Maneka Gandhi (1978):
    • Procedure must be fair, just, reasonable
    • Against executive and legislature
    • Dignity
    • Arts. 14, 19, 21 read together
  • Memory aid (Art. 21 cases):
    • Gopalan = Grudging (narrow, 1950)
    • Maneka = Mighty (broad, 1978)

Golden Triangle: Arts. 14, 19 and 21

Golden Triangle (Maneka Gandhi, 1978)

  • Art. 14:
    • Equality
    • Bedrock of fairness and non-arbitrariness
    • Ensures laws under Arts. 19 and 21 meet constitutional standards
  • Art. 19:
    • Specific freedoms
    • Curtailed only by restrictions that are reasonable and pass Art. 14's equality test
  • Art. 21: No deprivation of life or liberty except by a fair, just and reasonable procedure
  • Test:
    • Any law encroaching on liberty must pass all three: equality (14), reasonable restrictions (19), fair procedure (21)

21 · Property and the shields of 31A, 31B, 31C#

History of the right to property

  • 1950: One of the seven FRs. Art. 19(1)(f): every citizen's right to acquire, hold and dispose of property. Art. 31: no person (citizen or not) deprived of property except by authority of law, for a public purpose and with compensation. Became the most controversial FR (land reform, zamindari abolition, compensation disputes): constant legislature-judiciary conflict
  • 1951: 1st Amendment (with 39th, 42nd later): protect agrarian and socio-economic laws via Arts. 31A, 31B, 31C and the Ninth Schedule
  • 1955: 4th Amendment: adequacy of compensation cannot be challenged in courts
  • 1971:
    • 25th Amendment: word "compensation" replaced by "amount"
    • Further reduced judicial scrutiny
  • 1978:
    • 44th Amendment: abolished property as an FR
    • Arts. 19(1)(f) and 31 repealed
    • Art. 300A inserted in Part XII: "No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law"
  • 2007: I.R. Coelho: Ninth Schedule laws placed after 24 April 1973 (Kesavananda date) can be reviewed if they violate FRs forming part of the Basic Structure
  • Shortcut:
    • Property amendments in order. 1st (1951), 4th (1955), 25th (1971), 39th, 42nd, 44th (1978): read it as 1-4-25-39-42-44. The first five protected agrarian and socio-economic laws or cut judicial scrutiny
    • The 44th ended the Fundamental Right

Recall sheet#

Cover the right column and answer. Same questions as the flashcards.

What is Part III of the Constitution?
Fundamental Rights, Articles 12 to 35; inspired largely by the American Bill of Rights; called the 'Magna Carta of India'.
How many FRs were there originally and now?
Seven originally; six now. Right to Property (Art. 31) was removed by the 44th Amendment (1978) and became Art. 300A.
Article spans of the six FRs?
Equality 14-18; Freedom 19-22; Exploitation 23-24; Religion 25-28; Cultural and Educational 29-30; Constitutional Remedies 32.
Who called Art. 32 the 'heart and soul of the Constitution'?
Dr. B.R. Ambedkar.
What does Art. 12 say 'State' includes?
Government and Parliament of India; Government and Legislature of each State; all local authorities; other authorities within India or under Government of India control.
Which cases gave tests for 'other authorities' under Art. 12?
Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981): instrumentality or agency test; Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. IICB (2002): deep and pervasive State control; plus the public function test.
Is the judiciary 'State' under Art. 12?
Rati Lal: no. Antulay and Mirajkar: State for rule-making power, not State for exercise of judicial power.
What does Art. 13 do?
Laws that violate Part III are void to the extent of violation: 13(1) pre-1950 laws, 13(2) post-1950 laws, 13(3) meaning of law, 13(4) constitutional amendments (24th Amendment, 1971).
Origin of 'equality before law' versus 'equal protection of laws'?
Equality before law: British, negative concept (Rule of Law). Equal protection of laws: American, positive concept.
What are the two tests of reasonable classification?
Intelligible differentia and rational nexus with the object of the law.
Dicey's three elements of the Rule of Law and India's position?
Absence of arbitrary power and equality before law (followed in India); primacy of individual rights (replaced by constitutional FRs).
Name the exceptions to Art. 14.
President and Governors (361); publication of legislative proceedings (361A); legislative privileges (105, 194); Art. 31C; foreign sovereigns and diplomats; United Nations.
What are the five grounds in Art. 15?
Religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth. Art. 16 adds descent and residence.
Which clauses of Art. 15 add special provisions?
15(3) women and children; 15(4) SEBCs, SCs, STs (1st Am., 1951); 15(5) admissions including private unaided (93rd Am., 2005); 15(6) EWS 10% (103rd Am., 2019).
Which part of Art. 15 binds private persons too?
Art. 15(2): access to shops, restaurants, hotels, public entertainment, wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort.
What do 16(3), 16(4), 16(4A), 16(4B), 16(5), 16(6) cover?
Parliament's residence rules; reservation for backward classes; promotions for SC/ST; carry-forward of unfilled vacancies; religious posts; EWS 10%.
What are the vertical and horizontal reservations?
Vertical: SC 15, ST 7.5, OBC 27, EWS 10 (Arts. 15(4), 16(4), 15(6), 16(6)). Horizontal: within each category, e.g., 33% women (Arts. 15(3), 16(1)).
What did Janhit Abhiyan (2022) hold?
EWS amendment valid; 50% cap is not absolute; excluding SC/ST/OBC from EWS is not discriminatory.
EWS eligibility numbers?
Family income below Rs 8,00,000; agricultural land below 5 acres; flat below 1000 sq ft; plot below 100 sq yd (notified municipalities) or 200 sq yd (elsewhere).
What are the five conditions of Indra Sawhney (1992)?
27% valid; creamy layer excluded; no reservation in promotions under 16(4); 50% cap except in exceptional circumstances; carry-forward valid within the cap, with a permanent statutory body recommended.
Which Amendments followed Indra Sawhney for SC/ST?
77th (1995) promotions; 81st (2000) backlog beyond 50%; 85th (2001) consequential seniority.
Which Articles list SCs and STs?
Art. 341 (SC) and Art. 342 (ST); lists change only by Parliament by law.
Which religions can have SC status?
Hindus (1950), Sikhs (1956) and Buddhists (1990); Muslim and Christian Dalits are excluded (Soosai 1985); ST status has no religion limit.
What did the 102nd Amendment (2018) add?
Art. 338B (NCBC constitutional status) and Art. 342A (Central List of SEBCs); the 105th Amendment (2021) restored States' power over State OBC lists.
What is Art. 17?
Abolition of untouchability; practice in any form forbidden; enforcement of disability is a punishable offence; Parliament prescribes punishment under Art. 35; enforceable against private individuals too.
Name the laws under Art. 17.
Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955; Protection of Civil Rights Act (renamed after 1976); SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989.
What are the four rules of Art. 18?
No titles from the State (except military or academic); no titles from foreign States to citizens; foreigners in office need the President's consent for titles; presents and emoluments also need consent.
What did Balaji Raghavan (1996) hold?
Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are valid; not titles unless used as prefix or suffix, in which case they may be forfeited.
List the six freedoms of Art. 19.
Speech and expression (a); assemble peaceably (b); associations, unions, co-operatives (c); move freely (d); reside and settle (e); profession, trade, business (g). Property 19(1)(f) was removed in 1978.
Which clauses restrict each Art. 19 freedom?
19(2) speech; 19(3) assembly; 19(4) association; 19(5) movement and residence; 19(6) profession.
Seven grounds of Art. 19(2)?
Sovereignty and integrity (16th Am., 1963); security of the State; friendly relations with foreign States (1st Am., 1951); public order, decency or morality; contempt of court; defamation; incitement to an offence.
What are the four proportionality questions?
Legitimate goal; suitable measure; least restrictive way; benefit to society outweighs harm to individual rights.
What does Art. 20 protect against?
20(1) ex-post-facto laws; 20(2) double jeopardy (prosecution and punishment); 20(3) self-incrimination. Applies to criminal law only; citizens and foreigners.
Which evidence is outside Art. 20(3)?
Material objects, thumb impressions, specimen signatures, handwriting samples, blood, hair and DNA samples.
Gopalan (1950) versus Maneka Gandhi (1978)?
Gopalan: narrow, procedure established by law, executive action only. Maneka Gandhi overruled it: fair, just and reasonable procedure; Arts. 14, 19, 21 the Golden Triangle.
Which cases gave derivative rights of Art. 21?
Olga Tellis 1985 (livelihood); Puttaswamy 2017 (privacy); Paschim Banga 1996 (health); Unni Krishnan 1993 (education); Hussainara Khatoon 1979 (legal aid); Satwant Singh 1967 (travel abroad); Ramlila Maidan 2012 (sleep).
What is Art. 21A and the RTE Act, 2009?
Free and compulsory education for children 6-14 (86th Am., 2002); RTE: Class I-VIII, schools within 3 km and 5 km, 25% private seats, no board exam till elementary, NCTE qualifications, CCE, no corporal punishment.
What are the Art. 22 clauses?
(1) grounds and lawyer; (2) 24 hours before magistrate; (3) exceptions: enemy alien and preventive detention; (4) Advisory Board beyond 3 months; (5) grounds and representation; (6) non-disclosure; (7) Parliament's powers.
Who may legislate on preventive detention?
Parliament alone: defence, foreign affairs, security of India. Parliament and States: security of a State, public order, supplies and services essential to the community.
Name the current preventive detention laws.
COFEPOSA 1974; NSA 1980; PBMSECA 1980; PITNDPSA 1988; UAPA 1967 (amended 2004, 2008, 2012, 2019).
What does Art. 23 prohibit?
Traffic in human beings, begar and other forced labour; State may impose compulsory service for public purposes without discrimination on religion, race, caste or class; binds private persons too.
Art. 24 and the child labour laws?
No child under 14 in a factory, mine or hazardous employment. 1986 Act: 18 occupations and 65 processes; 2016 amendment: Child and Adolescent Labour Act, adolescents 14-18 barred from hazardous work.
Art. 25 versus Art. 26?
Art. 25: individual, all persons, subject to public order, morality, health and other FRs. Art. 26: collective right of denominations, not subject to other FRs.
What is the test of a religious denomination?
Common beliefs or doctrines; common organisation; a distinctive name (Ramakrishna Mission and Ananda Marga are; Aurobindo Society is not).
Which case gave the Essential Religious Practices doctrine?
Shirur Mutt (1954); a practice not essential can be regulated or banned by the State.
What is Art. 27 and how does a tax differ from a fee?
No compulsion to pay taxes for promoting any particular religion; a fee for services or management (e.g., crowd management at a shrine) is permitted.
Types of institutions under Art. 28?
Wholly State-maintained: no religious instruction; administered by State under trust: allowed, may be compulsory; recognised or aided: instruction allowed but attendance voluntary.
Art. 29 versus Art. 30?
Art. 29: any section of citizens; group right to conserve language, script, culture and individual right against denial of admission. Art. 30: religious and linguistic minorities only; right to establish and administer educational institutions.
What did Art. 30(1A) add and when?
Compensation for compulsory acquisition of a minority institution's property must not restrict the right; added by the 44th Amendment, 1978.
Name the five writs and their meanings.
Habeas Corpus (to have the body); Mandamus (we command); Prohibition (to forbid); Certiorari (to be certified); Quo Warranto (by what authority).
Art. 32 versus Art. 226?
Art. 32 is itself an FR and the SC cannot refuse; for FRs only. Art. 226 is a constitutional power of HCs, discretionary, for FRs and other legal rights.
What do Arts. 33, 34 and 35 do?
33: Parliament restricts FRs of forces and intelligence staff; 34: indemnity and validation during martial law; 35: Parliament alone legislates on 16(3), 32(3), 33, 34 and penalties for 17 and 23.
Position of the right to property today?
Art. 300A, a legal right for any person; protection only against executive action; remedy under Art. 226; compensation mandatory only for minority institutions (Art. 30) and personal land within ceiling limits (proviso to 31A).
What do Arts. 31A, 31B and 31C protect?
31A: five categories of land and corporate laws; 31B: Ninth Schedule laws; 31C: laws for 39(b) and (c). All shield laws from challenge under Arts. 14 and 19 (31B from FR challenges), but not from the Basic Structure.
Which rights lie outside Part III?
Art. 265 (tax by authority of law); Art. 300A (property); Art. 301 (free trade); Art. 326 (adult suffrage, 18 years). Remedy through the High Court, not Art. 32.

Traps#

Trap 1 · Persons versus citizens Art. 14 (equality) and Art. 21 (life) protect all persons, foreigners included. Art. 15 and the six freedoms of Art. 19 are for citizens only. Rule: equal law, life and crime shields = every person; discrimination ban, jobs and the six freedoms = citizens.
Trap 2 · Art. 16(4) versus 16(5) The handout once calls OBC job reservation 'Article 16(5)', but the clause list gives 16(5) to religious posts. Rule: OBC reservation in jobs = 16(4); religious-institution posts = 16(5); EWS = 16(6).
Trap 3 · Two months or three months The 44th Amendment cut detention without an Advisory Board from 3 to 2 months, but it was never brought into force. Rule: answer 3 months for Art. 22(4); the 2 months exists only on paper.
Trap 4 · Padma awards are not titles Art. 18 bans titles, yet Bharat Ratna and the Padma awards (1954) are valid because they are not titles of nobility (Balaji Raghavan). Rule: awards are safe unless used as a prefix or suffix to the name; then they can be forfeited.
