1 · Meaning, single citizenship and rights#

Citizenship is the legal membership of a person in a political community. It brings full civil and political rights and matching duties. It is both identity and allegiance, the bond between an individual and the State.

  • Governed by Part II (Articles 5-11) plus the Citizenship Act, 1955.
  • The framers adopted single citizenship, unlike the dual system of federal countries like the USA. Aim: national unity and equal rights across states and regions.
  • Citizenship provisions came into force on 26 November 1949.
  • Whether from Kerala or Kashmir, Gujarat or Nagaland, a person is a citizen only of India with uniform rights and obligations.

Rights and privileges only for citizens (denied to aliens)

  • Right against discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth (Article 15).
  • Equality of opportunity in public employment (Article 16).
  • Freedom of speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence and profession (Article 19).
  • Cultural and educational rights (Articles 29 and 30).
  • Right to vote in elections to the Lok Sabha and state legislative assembly.
  • Right to contest for membership of Parliament and the state legislature.
  • Eligibility to hold certain public offices: President, Vice-President, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, Governor of states, Attorney General of India and Advocate General of states.

2 · Related concepts: alien, refugee, asylum seeker, migrant#

An alien is anyone who is not a citizen of a State. Under the Foreigners Act, 1946 (amended 1957) "foreigner" is equated with alien.

Friendly aliens

  • Entitled to civil rights
  • denied political rights
  • example: Australian cricketers visiting India on visa are aliens or foreigners under Indian law

Enemy aliens

  • Citizens of states at war with India (or aiding them)
  • denied both civil and political rights
  • example: Chinese nationals in India during the 1962 war treated as enemy aliens under the Defence of India Act

3 · Constitutional framework: Articles 5 to 11 (Part II)#

Articles 5 to 11 lay down the initial basis of citizenship: who was deemed a citizen at the commencement of the Constitution (26 January 1950). They do not create a permanent law. They empower Parliament to frame one, now the Citizenship Act, 1955.

Art 6Persons who migrated from PakistanThose who migrated to India before 19 July 1948 are deemed citizens. Those migrating after that date need registration after residence in India for six months

4 · Acquisition of citizenship: the five modes#

The Citizenship Act, 1955 gives five legal modes, a blend of jus soli (citizenship by birthplace) and jus sanguinis (citizenship by descent): Birth, Descent, Registration, Naturalisation, Incorporation of Territory.

Five modes
BDRNI

Birth, Descent, Registration, Naturalisation, Incorporation: "Bright Dogs Rarely Need Instructions." The Sections run in order: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7.

1. By Birth (Section 3)

Recognises jus soli, with progressive restrictions to prevent misuse through illegal migration. Amendments in 1986 and 2003 tightened the criteria, linking citizenship to parentage and legality of residence.

Citizenship by birth (Section 3)
Period of birthEligibilityExplanation
Before 1 July 1987Every person born in India automatically became a citizen, irrespective of parents' nationalityBroad jus soli principle, immediately after Independence
1 July 1987 to 2 December 2004Citizen only if either parent was an Indian citizen at the time of birthExcludes children of foreign diplomats or enemy aliens
On or after 3 December 2004Citizen only if one parent is an Indian citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant at the time of birthRestriction brought by the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003 to control illegal immigration
Illegal migrant

A foreigner who enters India without valid travel documents or overstays beyond the permitted period. The definition became crucial after 2003 due to security and demographic concerns.

Birth rule tightening

Birth cut-offs

1987 = one parent a citizen. 2004 = one parent a citizen plus the other not an illegal migrant. Before 1987 = no parent test at all.

2. By Descent (Section 4)

Follows jus sanguinis: citizenship through parentage, not place of birth. Covers persons born outside India to Indian citizens, subject to registration and declaration.

Citizenship by descent (Section 4)
Period of birthEligibilityExplanation
26 January 1950 to 10 December 1992Person born outside India is a citizen if the father was an Indian citizen at the time of birthGendered early law: citizenship passed only through the father
10 December 1992 to 3 December 2004Citizen if either parent was an Indian citizen at the time of birthThe 1992 amendment removed gender bias and recognised both parents equally
After 3 December 2004Only if (a) parents declare the child does not hold a foreign passport, and (b) the birth is registered at an Indian consulate within one yearEnsures documentation and dual-nationality control
Not automatic

Citizenship by descent requires proactive registration. This prevents dual citizenship conflicts and ensures voluntary attachment to India.

5 · Citizenship by registration (Section 5)#

A semi-discretionary process: the Central Government may grant citizenship on application to certain categories, provided they are not illegal migrants. It mainly caters to Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) and those with close familial or historical links to India.

Who can register under Section 5
CategoryCondition
PIO residing in IndiaOrdinarily resident in India for 7 years before applying ("ordinarily resident" means residing in India throughout the period, allowing short absences)
PIO residing abroadOrdinarily resident in any country outside undivided India (before Partition)
Spouse of an Indian citizenOrdinarily resident in India for 7 years before application
Minor children of Indian citizensThe minor child of an Indian citizen may be registered (avoids statelessness)
Children of registered citizensA person of full age and capacity whose parents are already registered Indian citizens under this section
Former citizens of independent IndiaThe person or parents were citizens of independent India and the person has been ordinarily resident in India for at least 12 months before applying
OCI cardholdersRegistered as OCI for 5 years and ordinarily resident in India for 12 months before applying. Bridges quasi-citizenship (OCI) to full citizenship
Conditions for all

Applicants must satisfy the Central Government on character, intention to reside permanently, and lawful stay. The power to grant or reject rests entirely with the Government.

7 years for PIO in India and for spouses. 12 months for former citizens. 5 years OCI plus 12 months residence for OCI cardholders.

6 · Citizenship by naturalisation (Section 6)#

Granted by the Central Government to a foreign national (not an illegal migrant) who voluntarily seeks Indian citizenship and meets the qualifications of the Third Schedule of the Act.

Eligibility conditions

  • Not a citizen of a country where Indian citizens are barred from naturalisation or citizenship (reciprocity).
  • Of full age and capacity at the time of application.
  • Of good character and intends to reside permanently in India.
  • Adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule.
  • Has resided in India or been in government service:
  • for 12 years immediately preceding the application (including 12 months continuous stay before the date of application);
  • the remaining 11 years must fall within the previous 14 years.
Reading the numbers

12 months of continuous stay plus the remaining 11 years make the 12 years in total. The 11 years must fall within the previous 14 years.

Grant of certificate

Foreign national, not an illegal migrant

Eligibility and bona fides
Certificate of Naturalisation
Full Indian citizenship; discretionary, not automatic, and the Government's decision is final

Relaxation under the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019

Special exemption for persons from six specified religious minorities: Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians, from Pakistan, Afghanistan and Bangladesh, who entered India before 31 December 2014.

  • Residence requirement reduced from 12 years to 5 years.
  • Exempted from being treated as illegal migrants under the 2003 amendment.
  • Exempted from the classification of illegal migrant under the Foreigners Act, 1946 and the Passport Act, 1920.
  • New Section 6B reduces the qualifying residence period for naturalisation for these eligible persons.

Does not apply to

  • Persons from the six communities if they are from other countries
  • Muslim communities in the eligible countries

Does not extend to

  • Certain tribal areas under the Sixth Schedule
  • areas under the Inner Line Permit (ILP) regime (for example parts of North-East India)

Administrative aspects

  • Applications are processed through the online portal of the Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA).
  • MHA issued the Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024: procedures, documentation, eligibility verification and online registration.

7 · Incorporation of territory (Section 7) and the Assam case#

When a new territory becomes part of India, the Central Government decides, through a notification, who among its residents become citizens.

  • Puducherry (1962): French territories merged after the Treaty of Cession.
  • Dadra and Nagar Haveli (1961): integrated after liberation from Portuguese rule.
  • Sikkim (1975): citizens of Sikkim automatically became citizens of India after the merger through the 36th Amendment.

Result: political integration leads to automatic national belonging and avoids statelessness.

Case of Assam (Section 6A)

Assam has been a victim of large-scale illegal migration, especially from Bangladesh regardless of religion. This created ethnic anxiety among Assamese and led to violence. To deal with it the Assam Accord was signed in 1985, and Section 6A was added to the Citizenship Act 1955. It deals only with people living in Assam.

8 · Termination of citizenship (Sections 8, 9, 10)#

Citizenship is not perpetual. The Citizenship Act, 1955 gives three modes: Renunciation, Termination, Deprivation.

Three ways to lose
RTD

Renunciation (Sec 8), Termination (Sec 9), Deprivation (Sec 10): "Really Tough Decisions." Voluntary, automatic, compulsory in that order.

Renunciation, termination, deprivation
ModeSectionNatureKey points
Renunciation8Voluntary surrenderAny citizen of full age and capacity who also holds another country's citizenship may make a declaration in the prescribed form. On registration of the declaration the person ceases to be a citizen. Minor children also lose citizenship automatically, but may resume it by declaration within one year of attaining majority
Termination9Automatic cessation when a person voluntarily acquires another country's citizenship, with or without a formal declarationApplies to citizens by birth, descent, registration or naturalisation. Government of India decides whether the acquisition was voluntary. Excludes those who got foreign citizenship during a war involving India, unless the Central Government permits
Deprivation10Compulsory withdrawal by the Central Government in exceptional circumstancesApplies only to those who got citizenship by registration, naturalisation or incorporation of territory (not by birth)
Dual nationality

India does not allow dual citizenship. Anyone voluntarily acquiring foreign citizenship is deemed to have renounced Indian citizenship.

Grounds for deprivation (Section 10)

  • Fraud or concealment: citizenship obtained by fraud, false representation or suppression of facts.
  • Disloyalty: disaffection or disloyalty towards the Constitution of India.
  • Unlawful trade or communication: with an enemy during war.
  • Imprisonment: two years or more within five years after registration or naturalisation.
  • Ordinary residence abroad: resided outside India for seven consecutive years without registering annually at an Indian mission the intention to retain Indian citizenship.
Deprivation grounds
FDUIR

Fraud, Disloyalty, Unlawful trade with enemy, Imprisonment (2 years in 5), Residence abroad (7 years): "Five Deadly Unforgivable Infractions Remove citizenship."

Sec 8 versus 9 versus 10

Voluntary (8), automatic (9), compulsory (10). Only Section 10 spares citizens by birth. Numbers to recall: 2 years in 5 years (prison), 7 consecutive years (abroad), 1 year after majority (minors resume).

9 · Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI)#

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2015 replaced "Overseas Citizen of India" with "Overseas Citizen of India Cardholder" and merged the PIO category, creating one simplified scheme for the diaspora.

  • India does not permit dual citizenship. OCI is a limited form of overseas nationality: long-term residency, travel and economic rights without political participation.
  • Began under the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003; expanded by the 2005 and 2015 amendments.
  • Legal authority: Sections 7A to 7D of the Citizenship Act, 1955 (eligibility, registration, rights, cancellation).
  • Administered by the Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA) through its Foreigners Division.
  • Objective: a lifelong connection with persons of Indian origin abroad, with travel, residence and business privileges while protecting constitutional and security interests.

Eligibility for OCI registration

A person (not an illegal migrant) may be registered if they:

  • were citizens of India on or after 26 January 1950; or
  • were eligible to become citizens on that date; or
  • belonged to a territory that became part of India after 15 August 1947; or
  • are children, grandchildren or great-grandchildren of such citizens; or
  • are spouses of Indian citizens or OCI cardholders, with the marriage registered and subsisting for at least two years before application.
Not eligible

Citizens or former citizens of Pakistan or Bangladesh.

Privileges of OCI cardholders
DomainPrivileges
TravelMultiple-entry, lifelong visa; exemption from FRRO registration for any duration
EconomicParity with NRIs in economic, financial and educational fields (except agricultural or plantation property)
EducationSame admission rights as NRIs in professional courses
Social and civicEligible for Aadhaar, PAN, bank accounts and property ownership (except farm or plantation land)
EmploymentCan work in the private sector but cannot hold public or constitutional offices

What an OCI cardholder cannot do

  • Vote in Indian elections (electoral sovereignty).
  • Hold public or constitutional offices: posts such as President, Governor, judges or civil services.
  • Become a Member of Parliament or State Legislature (prevents dual loyalty concerns).
  • Acquire agricultural or plantation property.

OCI gives privileged residency and economic participation, not complete citizenship.

Grounds for cancellation (Section 7D)

The Central Government may cancel OCI registration if the cardholder:

  • obtained registration through fraud or misrepresentation;
  • shows disaffection toward the Constitution of India;
  • is imprisoned for two years or more within five years of registration;
  • assists an enemy during war;
  • poses a threat to the sovereignty, integrity or security of India;
  • (2021 Amendment) violates local laws or MHA conditions.

Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2021: new provisions

  • Prior permission needed for: (a) Research, (b) Missionary work, (c) Mountaineering, (d) Journalism, (e) visits to restricted, protected or cantonment areas.
  • Parity with Indian citizens only in certain matters: entry fees to monuments, tariffs in domestic air travel, admission to medical and engineering colleges under the NRI quota.
  • MHA periodically issues updated OCI guidelines (2021, 2023).

10 · Domicile and Inner Line Permit (ILP)#

Domicile is a person's permanent home, the place with which they have a permanent legal connection and intend to reside indefinitely. In India a person can have only one domicile, linked to the whole territory of India, not to individual states, though states may issue domicile certificates for administrative purposes such as education or employment. Domicile governs personal legal matters (succession, marriage, inheritance). Citizenship governs political and civic membership.

Citizenship versus domicile
BasisCitizenshipDomicile
MeaningA political and legal status of membership of a sovereign nationA legal residence fixing a person's permanent home and personal-law jurisdiction
Governing lawCitizenship Act, 1955Common law principles (lex fori); states issue domicile certificates for administrative purposes
NatureSingle citizenship only, no separate state citizenshipOnly one domicile for the whole of India (as per HC rulings), though states may issue certificates for benefits
PurposePolitical rights such as voting, contesting elections and protection of the StateCivil matters such as succession, marriage, wills, taxation and educational or reservation benefits
AcquisitionCan be acquired or lost (birth, registration, naturalisation, renounced when foreign citizenship is acquired)Acquired by birth or by choosing a permanent residence with intention to stay indefinitely
ChangeabilityCan be changed by legal procedures (for example renunciation)Cannot be easily changed: needs proof of long-term residence and intent to reside permanently
UniformitySame for all Indians, no variation by stateLinked to individual residence and intention, varies person to person

Inner Line Permit (ILP)

  • Origin: introduced under the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation (BEFR), 1873 by the British to restrict outsider entry and protect their commercial interests.
  • Post-Independence: the term "British subject" was replaced with "Citizen of India", keeping ILP to protect tribal rights and land ownership.
  • Applicability: currently in Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram and Manipur (since 2019).
  • Requirement: Indian citizens from outside these states need a permit to enter or stay. Land purchase by outsiders is prohibited.
  • Purpose: safeguard indigenous culture, demographic balance and socio-economic interests of tribal communities.
  • Constitutional link: aligns with Article 371 provisions giving special protections to Northeast states.
ILP states
ANMM

Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram, Manipur (since 2019): "All North-East Mountain Members."

Citizenship versus domicile

Citizenship = political, one for India, law of 1955. Domicile = personal-law residence, one for India, certificates by states for benefits.

Recall sheet#

Read once, hide it, then rebuild the chapter from memory.

  1. Part II = Articles 5-11. Single citizenship. Act = Citizenship Act 1955. Provisions in force 26 Nov 1949.
  2. Art 5 commencement (5 years residence), Art 6 from Pakistan (19 July 1948), Art 7 to Pakistan (after 1 March 1947), Art 8 origin abroad, Art 9 voluntary foreign citizenship, Art 10 continuance, Art 11 Parliament.
  3. Modes: Birth 3, Descent 4, Registration 5, Naturalisation 6, Incorporation 7.
  4. Birth: before 1 July 1987 any; 1987-2004 one parent citizen; from 3 Dec 2004 one parent citizen plus other not illegal migrant.
  5. Descent: 1950-1992 father; 1992-2004 either parent; after 2004 declaration plus consulate registration within one year.
  6. Registration: 7 years PIO in India and spouse; 12 months former citizens; OCI 5 years plus 12 months.
  7. Naturalisation: 12 years (11 within previous 14 plus 12 months). CAA 2019: 5 years, six communities, three countries, before 31 Dec 2014, Section 6B.
  8. Assam Sec 6A: before 1 Jan 1966 citizen; 1966-25 March 1971 citizen minus 10 years vote; after: deport.
  9. Loss: Renunciation 8, Termination 9, Deprivation 10 (not for citizens by birth).
  10. Deprivation grounds: fraud, disloyalty, unlawful trade with enemy, 2 years prison within 5 years, 7 years abroad.
  11. OCI: Sections 7A-7D, MHA Foreigners Division, 2003 origin, 2005 and 2015; cancellation Sec 7D; 2021 permissions list.
  12. Domicile: one for India. ILP: BEFR 1873; Arunachal, Nagaland, Mizoram, Manipur (2019). NRC citizens, NPR usual residents (6 months), Census Act 1948.
