Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • Prelims 2003 Assertion-Reason: Shah Alam II 60-06 stayed away from Delhi in his early years because he feared his own Wazir, Imad-ul-Mulk, not a foreign invasion from the North-West
  • The Assertion-Reason method: read A and mark T/F, read R and mark T/F, and only if both are true, think whether R explains A (the keys are split between (c) and (b); UPSC gave no key)
  • Bijapur 1686 and Golconda 1687; Jizya dates (712, 1564, 1679, 1712); Kashi Vishwanath 1669; GTB 1675 and Sambhaji 80-89 1689
  • Five court factions with one example each; 5% of Mansabdars held 61% of Jagir revenue; Treaty of Bhopal 1738; Nadir Shah 1739 (50 lakh, 60 days, 2 lakh, 100 crore)

Mains hooks

  • Mains 2017: 'By mid-18th century, India was a spectre of fragmented polity'. Weight it 30% Mughal decline (cause) and 70% rise of regional powers (evidence)
  • The flipped version (FYQ): if the Mughal Empire is the protagonist, give 70% to Mughal decline and 30% to regional powers
  • Why Aurangzeb 58-07's wars, religious policy and the Jagirdari crisis laid the foundation of decline, and why weak emperors could not revive the State
  • Concept first, then facts: factionalism, weak personality, military weakness plus shrinking territory (Nadir Shah outside, Marathas inside)

Aurangzeb laid the foundation, weak emperors and factionalism spread the rot, and the Marathas and Nadir Shah exposed it: a State at its peak in 1707 sat under Maratha protection by 1752.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Peak, then rot, then collapse: Aurangzeb 58-07 reaches the peak in 1707 but his wars, religious policy and the Jagirdari crisis empty the treasury and the people's goodwill; weak emperors cannot keep the Mansabdari bargain, so factions and corruption rot the court and army; the Marathas and Nadir Shah then expose the rot and the emperor ends up under Maratha protection.

  1. The peak that hid the rot (1707). By 1707 the Mughal State has its greatest extent under Aurangzeb 58-07, yet the teacher says its economic power and social power have not peaked. State power is Political + Economic + Military + Social + Technological, and the decline will weaken all five at once. Aurangzeb laid the foundation of decline; the weak emperors after him could not revive the State or added to the decline.
    → So what exactly did Aurangzeb do?
  2. The emperor who never stopped fighting. Aurangzeb 58-07 comes to power in 1658 after killing his elder brother Dara Shikoh, and wants all India, above all the Deccan. He annexes Bijapur in 1686 (Adil Shahi, 1489-1686) and Golconda in 1687 (Qutb Shahi, 1518-1687), but the Marathas never accept Mughal authority. About 40 years of war drain the treasury and cripple the Mansabdars, who must maintain armies; by 1705 the Marathas are winning.
    → But war was not his only mistake: he also lost the people.
  3. The people turn away. Aurangzeb 58-07 follows an orthodox Islam in his governance. He re-introduces Jizya in 1679 (Akbar 56-05 had abolished it in 1564) and demolishes the Kashi Vishwanath temple in 1669 (his court record, the Maasir-i-Alamgiri, documents it). He executes Guru Tegh Bahadur in 1675 and Sambhaji 80-89 in 1689; both refuse to convert and become martyrs, hardening Sikh and Maratha feeling.
    → Meanwhile the nobles who run his army are being squeezed too.
  4. The bargain breaks. The Mansabdari system runs on personal loyalty to the emperor and a give-and-take: Mansabs and Jagirs for military service. A strong emperor keeps even an unhappy Mansabdar quiet through faith in fairness or through fear. A weak emperor cannot, so the Mansabdar acts on his disloyalty: lobbying, backbiting, a takhtapalat to bring his favourite to the throne.
    → Nobles busy in court politics are not busy with the army.
  5. A paper army. Europeans win with smaller numbers using file firing, grapeshot and the bayonet on the Brown Bess musket; Haider Ali 61-82 reforms on a European model, but the Mughals, paralysed by factionalism, reform nothing. Commanders are chosen by court politics, not merit, and Mansabdars keep far fewer cavalry than their Sawar rank requires.
    → And the money problem explains why they cheat.
  6. Too many Mansabdars, too little land. The Jagirdari crisis is like a water crisis: the number of Mansabdars goes up and the land goes down. After 1686 and 1687 Aurangzeb 58-07 makes most Bijapur and Golconda land Khalisa yet absorbs the Deccani nobles; later Mughals over-recruit to buy loyalty; land shrinks as the Marathas win. Then 5% of Mansabdars control 61% of Jagir revenue, and luxury inflation hurts the rest. The teacher calls its impact the most important in the decline.
    → Insecure tenure turns the Jagirdars into looters.
  7. Kal ho na ho Jagir. Jagirs are shuffled to satisfy disgruntled Naqdi Mansabdars, so no Jagirdar knows how long he holds his land. He extracts the maximum in the minimum time, Ijaradari spreads, and Zamindar-led revolts follow: the Jat Kingdom grows from the 1695 revolt of Churaman 95-21 Jat. Military corruption brings victories to rebel states (Sikh, Marathas, Afghans) and to an outsider like Nadir Shah.
    → At court, the factions now fight for the throne itself.
  8. Five factions, three rounds. The court splits into Irani, Turani, Hindustani, Hindu and Deccani groups. Bahadur Shah 07-12 fights his brothers; Jahandar Shah 12-13 rules with Zulfiqar Khan and is executed by Farukhsiyar 13-19, who rises with the Sayyid Brothers. He refuses to be their puppet, so the Sayyids ally with the Marathas (15,000 cavalry in Delhi, 1719), kill him and install Muhammad Shah 19-48. Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48 beats the Sayyids with the Irani group, then takes Hyderabad in 1724.
    → The Marathas watch Delhi's weakness.
  9. The Marathas test Delhi. From 1705 the Marathas defeat Mughal forces; by the mid-18th century they recover Aurangzeb 58-07's gains and take Akbar 56-05's Gujarat, and only Hyderabad holds out. In 1737 Peshwa Bajirao 20-40 plunders Delhi and briefly holds Md Shah prisoner. In 1738 they beat the army led by Nizam, and the Treaty of Bhopal gives them the Chambal-Narmada lands and Malwa.
    → Then a bigger predator arrives from Iran.
  10. Nadir Shah, the lowest point (1739). Nadir Shah wants plunder, not annexation. At Karnal the Mughal army is routed and Sadat Khan 22-39 is captured; Nizam, now Mir Bakshi, buys peace for Rs 50 lakh. But Sadat Khan, passed over for that post, goads Nadir Shah to attack Delhi: he rules for 60 days, about 2 lakh civilians die, about 100 crore is looted, including the Peacock Throne with the Kohinoor. The Marathas, home in the South after Bhopal, simply wait.
    → After this blow, the emperor needs a protector.
  11. Protector and puppet (1752-54). Nadir Shah's victory encourages Abdali's first invasion of 1748. In 1752 the emperor comes under Maratha protection for fear of Afghan invasions, and in 1754 the Marathas install Alamgir II 54-59 as their puppet. By the mid-18th century there are many States, not one: successors (Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad), rebels (Jats, Marathas, Rohilkhand, Farukhabad) and the already autonomous (Mysore, Rajputs, Travancore), with the Marathas as No. 1 power. The emperor keeps symbolic sovereignty until 1858.
    → Now: how will the exam ask this?
  12. How the exam asks it. Prelims 2003: Shah Alam II 60-06 stayed away from Delhi in his early years for fear of his Wazir Imad-ul-Mulk, not a frontier invasion; read A and R separately, then think. Mains 2017 asked about a fragmented polity: find the protagonist and weight 30% Mughal decline and 70% regional powers, and flip the weights if the Mughal Empire itself is the subject.

In simple words#

  • MIH starts here. The Mughal State had its greatest extent under Aurangzeb 58-07 in 1707, yet by the 1750s the emperor ruled little beyond Delhi-Agra under Maratha protection.
  • Decline is a five-way weakening: Political, Economic, Military, Social and Technological power fell together. Use vertical (power moves down to Mansabdars) and horizontal (one entity replaces another) lenses.
  • Aurangzeb's long wars, religious policy and executions drained the treasury and the people's goodwill
  • The Jagirdari crisis (too many Mansabdars, too little land) broke the nobles
  • Weak emperors could not keep the Mansabdari bargain, so nobles turned to factionalism, the army rotted (corruption, no reform) and Marathas and Nadir Shah (1739) exposed it.
  • For exams: hold the Wazir fact (Prelims 2003), the Assertion-Reason method, and the 30/70 split for the Mains 2017 fragmented-polity question.

1 · Framework: how to analyse the decline#

Story link

Story so far: a State at its peak in 1707. Because the decline hit five kinds of power at once, learn the five-part equation and the two lenses first. Ask: which lens fits each event, vertical or horizontal?

  • This is where Modern Indian History (MIH) starts.
  • The Mughal State reached its greatest extent under Aurangzeb 58-07 and began collapsing right after.
  • Within about four decades it was a small area around Delhi–Agra under Maratha protection.
Teacher's board
  • MIH starts here. Later Mughals are weak, from Bahadur Shah (1707-12) in the 1700s to Zafar (1837-57) in the 1800s.
  • Power moves from the Mughal down to the Mansabdars.
  • The foundation of decline was laid at Aurangzeb 58-07.
  • After him, the weak emperors either could not revive the State or contributed to its decline.

The five-part equation

  • State power = Political + Economic + Military + Social + Technological.
  • The Mughal decline is a simultaneous weakening across all five.
Teacher's board
  • By 1707 the Mughal empire reached its peak (territory), but not its economic power peak, and not its social power peak.
  • Travancore was never part of the Mughal State. Assam also not.

Two analytical tools (apply to every decline question)

ToolMeaningMughal example
Vertical power shift = decentralisationThe emperor weakens and power moves downwardAfter 1707 the emperors were weak personalities, so the Mansabdars under them became the real centre of power
Horizontal power shift = replacementOne entity replaces another at the same levelDelhi Sultanate replaced by the Mughals at Panipat 1526; British super-power replaced by US-USSR at the end of WW2 (1945)
Handout note

The handout says to apply both lenses to every exam question on decline.

The teacher's components board (what each form of power means)

  • Political power = the emperor's word is rule ("vachan hi hai shasan": decision + rule).
  • Teacher: political power is proportional to the territory of the State.
  • Economic power = the treasury, or public exchequer = the name of the bank account of the State.
  • Military power has four parts:
    • Number of soldiers / size of the army
    • Technology
    • No military corruption
    • Strong military leadership.
  • The teacher's World War examples:
    • 1945, USSR had the largest army
    • 1945, atomic bomb (USA).
Teacher's board
  • Social power = awe / love in people's hearts.
  • The common man does not want to be an MP, MLA or minister
  • He or she wants security (economic, physical, socio-cultural)
  • If social power falls, the chance of revolt rises
  • If social power rises, the chance of revolt falls.
  • Example: 1971, Indira Gandhi, and the film song of the time.

The teacher's list of factors

  • 1. Role of Aurangzeb 58-07: wars, religious policy.
  • 2. Weak emperors post-1707.
  • 3. Military weakened: external (Nadir Shah) and internal (rebel states, e.g. the Marathas).
  • 4. Institutional decline: disloyalty of Mansabdars, military corruption.
  • 5. Jagirdari crisis of the 18th century.
  • 6. Lack of reform (tech and organisation), all ending in territorial decline.

2 · Why did Aurangzeb 58-07's wars sow the seeds of decline?#

Story link

Because the peak hid weak economic and social power, the story begins with Aurangzeb's wars. Ask: was the return on investment positive, and did peace follow?

  • Aurangzeb 58-07 came to power in 1658 after a brutal succession war (he killed his elder brother Dara Shikoh).
  • He governed as an empire-builder on a mission: to conquer all of India, particularly the Deccan.
  • War helps a State only if ROI (return on investment) is positive and a period of peace follows the conquest.
  • If wars never stop and territory won is eventually lost, it becomes an economic disaster.
Teacher's board
  • War = rapid investment of money: recruit more men and horses, produce more weapons, logistics (costs of movement of men, food, weapons), attrition of soldiers, horses and elephants.
  • Domestic economy suffers if the war is fought in the homeland.
  • War victory → ROI is long and slow.
  • The gain of territory brings more LR, ports and more trade, and geopolitical gains such as a natural frontier.
  • Example: Hitler wanted territory up to the Urals.

What he won, and what it cost

  • Bijapur (annexed 1686; Adil Shahi dynasty 1489–1686) and Golconda (annexed 1687; Qutb Shahi dynasty 1518–1687).
  • Both were successor kingdoms of the Bahmani Sultanate (1347–1527).
  • In Bijapur it was a three-way fight: Mughals, Bijapur, and the Marathas, who never accepted Mughal authority.
  • By 1705 the Marathas were winning
  • By the 1750s they had reversed most of Aurangzeb 58-07's gains and some of Akbar 56-05's as well.
  • 40 years of warfare drained the treasury (the government's bank account), broke the Mansabdars financially (they had to maintain armies), and translated directly into later military corruption (Mansabdars not maintaining the army as per Sawar).
Teacher's board

Role of Aurangzeb 58-07 (about 50 years), versus Akbar 56-05 (about 50 years).

3 · Religious policies and the social foundation#

Story link

Because the wars cost money, the emperor also lost the people. Ask: which of the four dates hit social power, and who became a martyr?

  • State power needs social support. Aurangzeb 58-07 followed an orthodox interpretation of Islam in his personal life and his governance, systematically alienating non-Muslim subjects.

3.1 Jizya (1679) and its long history

  • The most damaging measure was the re-introduction of Jizya in 1679 (abolished in 1564): an additional tax on non-Muslim adult males.
  • Beyond the money, the manner of collection made people feel religiously discriminated against, and resentment ran deep.
  • Qutb-ud-din Aibak 06-10 first introduced Jizya in an institutionalised manner.
  • He was the first Sultan of the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) and founder of the Mamluk dynasty (1206–1290; Mamluk = military slave).
  • Babur 26-30 and Humayun 30-40 continued it.
  • Akbar 56-05 abolished it in 1564 as part of his Sulh-i-Kul (Universal Peace) policy.
  • Aurangzeb 58-07 reintroduced it in 1679.
  • Zulfiqar Khan (Wazir of Jahandar Shah 12-13 abolished it again in 1712.
  • The handout says Farukhsiyar (1713–19) re-introduced it in 1717.
  • As the empire weakened, Jizya faded with it.

  • Both agree on the direction: abolished 1712, re-imposed by Farukhsiyar, gone by 1719.

3.2 Temple policy: Kashi Vishwanath (1669)

  • Aurangzeb 58-07 refused temple repairs and reduced their land grants.
  • More significantly he demolished important temples.
  • Best-known case: the Kashi Vishwanath temple at Varanasi, demolished 1669, replaced on the same site by the Gyanvapi Mosque.
  • His own court record, the Maasir-i-Alamgiri, documents his order.
  • The temple's troubled history: razed earlier by Qutb-ud-din Aibak 06-10 (1194, then slave-general of Md Ghori, before he became first Sultan in 1206) and Sikandar Lodi (1490).
  • Ahilyabai Holkar of Indore rebuilt it in 1780 near the original site.

3.3 Executions that backfired

  • 1675: Guru Tegh Bahadur (GTB), the 9th Sikh Guru. 1689: Chhatrapati Sambhaji 80-89, elder son of Shivaji 74-80, after weeks of torture.
  • Both were offered convert-or-die
  • Both refused
  • The executions did not suppress resistance: they created martyrs, hardening anti-Mughal sentiment among Sikhs and Marathas.
Teacher's board

Both GTB (1675) and Sambhaji 80-89 (1689) are boxed on the board as the "execution of leaders" line, under social power sad.

4 · Why did the Mansabdari system turn against the empire?#

Story link

Because the treasury and the people were weaker, the nobles who served the emperor came under strain. Ask: what is different for an unhappy Mansabdar under a strong and a weak emperor?

  • The Mansabdari system ran on personal loyalty to the emperor as an individual, not impersonal loyalty to the empire as an institution.
  • It was a give-and-take:
    • The emperor granted Mansabs and Jagirs
    • Nobles gave military service and loyalty
  • Strong emperors made this work
  • Weak emperors could not.
  • After 1707 the Later Mughals could not control powerful Mansabdars.
  • When the emperor could not grant the desired Mansabs and Jagirs, the Mansabdars became disloyal and acted upon that disloyalty (earlier, either fear or belief in the fairness of a strong emperor kept them in line): they engaged in court politics for the best Mansabs and Jagirs, or tried replacing the emperor with their favourite from the royal family.
  • Court politics crippled governance. Those who should have led reforms (economic, administrative and military) were consumed by factional rivalry.
Why the Mansabdari bargain failed
Teacher's board
  • Busy in court politics and not on work.
  • That is the teacher's one-line reason why reform slowed down.

Why the Mansabdari bargain failed

  • Mansabdar does not get the desired Mansab and Jagir
  • Feeling of disloyalty
  • Strong emperor:
    • Good strong = faith in future fairness
    • Bad strong = fear
    • So no action by the Mansabdar
  • Weak emperor: the Mansabdar acts on his feeling of disloyalty
  • Factionalism = group politics = court politics: lobbying the emperor, backbiting, plot/coup (takhtapalat), bringing the favourite as emperor

5 · Why did the Mughal military weaken?#

Story link

Because nobles were busy in court politics, the army was left unreformed. Ask: which of the three problems is a matter of people, and which of method?

  • Three interconnected problems:

5.1 No technological or organisational reform

  • European powers succeeded with smaller numbers because of better command, tighter discipline, and tactical innovations: File firing: rotating lines for continuous fire. Grapeshot artillery: multiple bullets in one shot
  • Lethal at close range. Bayonet (a knife) on the Brown Bess musket (so a rifle became a sword at close range)
  • Haider Ali 61-82, Mysore) reorganised his military on the European model and stayed formidable.
  • The Mughals, paralysed by factionalism, reformed nothing.
Teacher's board
  • No reform while Indian rivals upgraded the military with European help.
  • Example in the handout: Haider Ali 61-82 reorganised on the French model (the slide says "on the French model"; the handout says "European model").

5.2 Weak war commanders

  • Under the Later Mughals (1707–1858), court politics, not merit, decided military appointments.

5.3 Military corruption

  • Under weak supervision Mansabdars maintained far fewer cavalry than their Sawar rank required, pocketing the difference.
  • Aurangzeb 58-07's war drain and the Jagirdari crisis compounded this.
  • By the early 18th century the army was corrupt, under-equipped, poorly led and technologically frozen.

7 · The Jagirdari crisis: why did it hit so hard?#

Story link

Because there were more claimants than land, tenure became unsafe and everything below it cracked. Ask: why does the teacher call this the most important cause?

  • The Jagirdari crisis was a structural mismatch: supply of Jagirs and demand for them fell dangerously out of balance.
Teacher's board
  • Water-crisis analogy: number of people up, water down.
  • Jagirdari crisis: number of Mansabdars up + land down.
Teacher's board
  • What? (1) Over-recruitment (number of Mansabdars rises).
  • (2) Land is the same in the kingdom
  • land shrunk because of loss of territory in wars (post-1705, the Marathas winning)

  • (b) Post-1707 factionalism: either to be in power or to come to power, the emperors over-recruited.

The Jagirdari spiral

  • Frequent transfers: Jagirs shuffled to satisfy disgruntled Naqdi Mansabdars
  • Tenure insecurity: no Jagirdar knew how long he would hold the land (kal ho na ho Jagir)
  • Ruthless extraction:
    • Maximum in minimum time
    • No incentive to invest in the land
  • Ijaradari spreads: revenue-farming strips collection from Zamindars, the rural stabilisers
  • Peasant revolts: over-extraction leads to Zamindar-led revolts (Jat Kingdom from the 1695 revolt under Churaman 95-21 Jat)
  • Effect on the peasantry: over-extraction, stagnation, revolts led by alienated Zamindars.
  • Direct example: the Jat Kingdom emerged from a 1695 peasant revolt under Churaman 95-21 Jat, a Zamindar.
  • Meanwhile Mansabdars did not maintain the army as per Sawar, to maximise their earnings, as no one knew when the emperor would transfer the Jagir (kal ho na ho Jagir).
  • The teacher ties the military consequences together: military corruption → victories for rebel states (Sikh, Marathas, Afghans) + an external power (Nadir Shah) → loss of territory.

9 · How the Marathas exposed the military collapse#

Story link

Because Delhi was weak inside, the Marathas could measure it from outside. Ask: what did each step, 1737 and 1738, take from Mughal prestige?

  • The military decline is best measured by what the Marathas achieved.
  • From 1705 they systematically defeated Mughal forces.
  • By the mid-18th century they had not only recovered Aurangzeb 58-07's Deccan gains but taken lands annexed under Akbar 56-05, including Gujarat.
  • Only Hyderabad (formerly Golconda) stayed outside Maratha control, held by Nizam as a successor State.
  • 1737: Peshwa Bajirao 20-40 marched to Delhi, plundered the capital, and briefly held Md Shah prisoner.
  • 1738: the Marathas defeated a large Mughal army commanded by Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48.
  • Formalised in the Treaty of Bhopal (1738): the Marathas received legal recognition of control over all lands between the Chambal and Narmada and the Subah of Malwa.
  • Imagine the Mughal emperor became a hostage in his own capital.

10 · Nadir Shah's invasion (1738–40): the five-pillar blow#

Story link

Because the Marathas had gone south, a Persian raider met Delhi alone. Ask: how did one snub undo a 50 lakh deal, and which pillars did he hit?

Teacher's board
  • A State has a territory motive, but does a cost-benefit analysis: (+) own resources (economic, military) and (+) decides on the basis of geography.
  • Example:
    • Ruling territory = a cost
    • Maintaining territory = a cost.
  • War aims: annexation, or less than annexation, e.g. plunder (attack and loot). Nadir Shah's aim was plunder.
  • Nadir Shah ruled a Persian empire stretching into Afghanistan (Mughal-claimed territory) and desired India's wealth.
  • His victory was easier because of the weakened Mughal empire plus factionalism.

What happened, step by step

Karnal to the fall of Delhi (1739)

  • Battle of Karnal:
    • Mughal army routed
    • Sadat Khan 22-39 taken prisoner
    • The Mir Bakshi died fighting
  • Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48, now Mir Bakshi, negotiates: Rs 50 lakh and Nadir Shah does not enter Delhi
  • Factionalism: snubbed over the Mir Bakshi post, Sadat Khan 22-39 goads Nadir Shah to attack Delhi for bigger plunder
  • Delhi falls
  • Md Shah imprisoned
  • Nadir Shah rules as emperor of Delhi for 60 days
  • Sadat Khan 22-39, insulted by Nadir Shah after the victory instead of being rewarded, takes his own life
Karnal to the fall of Delhi (1739)

11 · India by the mid-18th century#

Story link

Because the centre collapsed, power moved to many hands. Ask: which regional powers are successors, rebels or already autonomous, and who is No. 1?

  • 1752: the Mughal emperor came under Maratha protection, accepting Maratha military guardianship against further Afghan invasions. The protector had become the protected.
  • 1754: the Marathas installed Alamgir II 54-59 as their puppet emperor, a Mughal ruler chosen by the very people the Mughals had spent decades trying to suppress.
  • By the mid-18th c the Mughal Empire as a functional State had ceased to exist.
  • What remained was a collection of regional powers, each with its own territory, army and foreign policy.
  • The Maratha State stood at the top.

Teacher's pun on Nadir Shah?Nadir of Mughal power: the lowest point.
What happened in 1752 and 1754?1752: the emperor came under Maratha protection (fear of Abdali); 1754: the Marathas installed Alamgir II 54-59 as puppet.
Who was the No. 1 power by the mid-18th century?The Marathas (teacher).
Until when did the emperor have symbolic sovereignty?1858, though he ruled little beyond Delhi-Agra.
Prelims 2003 fact to pin?Shah Alam II 60-06 stayed away from Delhi early on for fear of his Wazir Imad-ul-Mulk.
