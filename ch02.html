1 · Framework: how to analyse the decline#

This is where Modern Indian History (MIH) starts. The Mughal State reached its greatest extent under Aurangzeb 58-07 and began collapsing right after. Within about four decades it was a small area around Delhi–Agra under Maratha protection.

Teacher's board

MIH starts here. Later Mughals are weak, from Bahadur Shah (1707-12) in the 1700s to Zafar (1837-57) in the 1800s. Power moves from the Mughal down to the Mansabdars. The foundation of decline was laid at Aurangzeb 58-07. After him, the weak emperors either could not revive the State or contributed to its decline.

The five-part equation

State power = Political + Economic + Military + Social + Technological. The Mughal decline is a simultaneous weakening across all five.

Memory hook

Five parts of State power: P-E-M-S-T.

"People Eat Many Sweet Treats."

Decode: Political, Economic, Military, Social, Technological. Mughal decline hit all five at once.

Why it sticks: if you can taste five sweets at once, you can feel five powers weaken at once.

Teacher's board

By 1707 the Mughal empire reached its peak (territory), but not its economic power peak, and not its social power peak. Travancore was never part of the Mughal State. Assam also not.

Two analytical tools (apply to every decline question)

ToolMeaningMughal example
Vertical power shift = decentralisationThe emperor weakens and power moves downwardAfter 1707 the emperors were weak personalities, so the Mansabdars under them became the real centre of power
Horizontal power shift = replacementOne entity replaces another at the same levelDelhi Sultanate replaced by the Mughals at Panipat 1526; British super-power replaced by US-USSR at the end of WW2 (1945)
Note

The handout says to apply both lenses to every exam question on decline.

Memory hook

Vertical drops, horizontal swaps.

"Vertical = Vacate downward; Horizontal = Hand over sideways."

Decode: Vertical = decentralisation: after 1707 power moved from the weak emperors down to the Mansabdars. Horizontal = replacement at the same level: Delhi Sultanate replaced by the Mughals at Panipat 1526; British super-power replaced by US-USSR at the end of WW2 (1945).

Why it sticks: use both lenses on every decline question: one for who lost power downward, one for who took the seat.

The teacher's components board (what each form of power means)

  • Political power = the emperor's word is rule ("vachan hi hai shasan": decision + rule). Teacher: political power is proportional to the territory of the State.
  • Economic power = the treasury, or public exchequer = the name of the bank account of the State.
  • Military power has four parts: number of soldiers / size of the army; technology; no military corruption; strong military leadership. The teacher's World War examples: 1945, USSR had the largest army; 1945, atomic bomb (USA).
Memory hook

Military power's four parts: S-T-C-C.

"Sena, Takneek, Clean hands, Commander."

Decode: Size of the army; Technology; no military Corruption; strong military Commander (leadership). Teacher's examples: 1945, USSR had the largest army; 1945, atomic bomb (USA) = technology.

Why it sticks: two numbers-and-guns items (Sena, Takneek), two people items (Clean, Commander); the Mughals later failed all four.

Teacher's board

Social power = awe / love in people's hearts. The common man does not want to be an MP, MLA or minister; he or she wants security (economic, physical, socio-cultural). If social power falls, the chance of revolt rises; if social power rises, the chance of revolt falls. Example: 1971, Indira Gandhi, and the film song of the time.

The teacher's list of factors

  1. Role of Aurangzeb 58-07: wars, religious policy.
  2. Weak emperors post-1707.
  3. Military weakened: external (Nadir Shah) and internal (rebel states, e.g. the Marathas).
  4. Institutional decline: disloyalty of Mansabdars, military corruption.
  5. Jagirdari crisis of the 18th century.
  6. Lack of reform (tech and organisation), all ending in territorial decline.
Memory hook

Six factors: "A Weak Mughal Is Just Lost".

A = Aurangzeb 58-07 (wars, religious policy); W = Weak emperors post-1707; M = Military weakened (Nadir Shah, Marathas); I = Institutional decline (disloyal Mansabdars, military corruption); J = Jagirdari crisis; L = Lack of reform.

Decode: the letters follow the teacher's numbered list 1 to 6.

Why it sticks: the sentence tells the whole result: the Mughal was lost.

Teacher's board

"We have covered the concepts of decline of the Mughals. Now let's look at factual data and events to support the concepts." Concepts first, then facts. (L4 slide 27 and L5 slide 2 show this two-layer approach: org = concept + fact.)

Teacher's board

Concept summary: Factionalism (Royal vs Royal, Royal vs Noble, Noble vs Noble); Weak personality (Rangila, 1719-48 = Muhammad Shah 19-48); Military weakness plus shrinking of territory (external: Nadir Shah 1738-40; internal: Marathas).

2 · Why did Aurangzeb 58-07's wars sow the seeds of decline?#

  • Aurangzeb 58-07 came to power in 1658 after a brutal succession war (he killed his elder brother Dara Shikoh). He governed as an empire-builder on a mission: to conquer all of India, particularly the Deccan.
  • War helps a State only if ROI (return on investment) is positive and a period of peace follows the conquest. If wars never stop and territory won is eventually lost, it becomes an economic disaster.
Memory hook

War is a sprint to spend, a marathon to earn.

Money leaves fast (men, horses, weapons, logistics, attrition); returns arrive slowly (territory, LR, ports, trade, a natural frontier).

Decode: war = rapid investment; victory = long, slow ROI. A war helps only if ROI is positive and peace follows. Aurangzeb 58-07's wars never stopped, so territory won was eventually lost: an economic disaster.

Why it sticks: sprint out, marathon back, and he never reached the finish line.

Teacher's board

War = rapid investment of money: recruit more men and horses, produce more weapons, logistics (costs of movement of men, food, weapons), attrition of soldiers, horses and elephants. Domestic economy suffers if the war is fought in the homeland. War victory → ROI is long and slow. The gain of territory brings more LR, ports and more trade, and geopolitical gains such as a natural frontier. Example: Hitler wanted territory up to the Urals.

What he won, and what it cost

  • Bijapur (annexed 1686; Adil Shahi dynasty 1489–1686) and Golconda (annexed 1687; Qutb Shahi dynasty 1518–1687). Both were successor kingdoms of the Bahmani Sultanate (1347–1527).
  • In Bijapur it was a three-way fight: Mughals, Bijapur, and the Marathas, who never accepted Mughal authority. By 1705 the Marathas were winning; by the 1750s they had reversed most of Aurangzeb 58-07's gains and some of Akbar 56-05's as well.
  • 40 years of warfare drained the treasury (the government's bank account), broke the Mansabdars financially (they had to maintain armies), and translated directly into later military corruption (Mansabdars not maintaining the army as per Sawar).
Memory hook

Bijapur then Golconda: B before G, 86 before 87.

Dynasty ends = annexation year: Adil Shahi 1489–1686 (Bijapur); Qutb Shahi 1518–1687 (Golconda).

Decode: Bijapur annexed 1686, Golconda 1687. Adil (A) is older (1489) and Qutb (Q) younger (1518). Both were successors of the Bahmani Sultanate (1347–1527). Bijapur was a three-way fight: Mughals, Bijapur, Marathas.

Why it sticks: alphabetical order matches chronological order, both times (A then Q; B then G).

Teacher's board

Role of Aurangzeb 58-07 (about 50 years), versus Akbar 56-05 (about 50 years).

Teacher's board

Aurangzeb 58-07, four lines. (1) Religious orthodoxy: Jizya (1679), temple policy → social power sad. (2) Execution of leaders: GTB (1675), Sambhaji 80-89 (1689) → social power sad. (3) Contribution to the Jagirdari crisis. (4) Continuous wars of about 45 years → economy sad (high LR demand; military corruption → weak military; wars proved useless ultimately; future emperors inherit a weak treasury).

Teacher's board

Akbar 56-05 = foundation of rise. Universal peace, syncretic policies, religious harmony → social power happy. LR system fair, no Ijaradari, economic security. Strong state machinery (Mansabdari system) → military happy.

Memory hook

"Rigid Emperor Jams Wallets."

Decode: Religious orthodoxy (Jizya 1679, temple policy); Executions (GTB 1675, Sambhaji 80-89 in 1689); Jagirdari crisis; continuous Wars (about 45 years). First two make social power sad; wars make the economy sad.

Why it sticks: four words, four lines of the teacher's board.

Akbar 56-05's three smiles: social (universal peace, religious harmony), economic (fair LR, no Ijaradari), military (Mansabdari machinery).

Decode: both ruled about 50 years. Akbar 56-05 = foundation of rise; Aurangzeb 58-07 = foundation of decline (social sad, economy sad, weak military, future emperors inherit a weak treasury).

Why it sticks: same length of reign, opposite faces.

Teacher's board

The Deccan triangle: Bijapur (1686), Golconda (1687), Goa, and the old Vijayanagara (1336–1646) region below them. The fight triangle = Aurangzeb 58-07 (the external power) + Marathas (domestic rebels) + Bijapur (the existing State).

3 · Religious policies and the social foundation#

State power needs social support. Aurangzeb 58-07 followed an orthodox interpretation of Islam in his personal life and his governance, systematically alienating non-Muslim subjects.

3.1 Jizya (1679) and its long history

  • The most damaging measure was the re-introduction of Jizya in 1679 (abolished in 1564): an additional tax on non-Muslim adult males. Beyond the money, the manner of collection made people feel religiously discriminated against, and resentment ran deep.
  • Qutb-ud-din Aibak 06-10 first introduced Jizya in an institutionalised manner. He was the first Sultan of the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) and founder of the Mamluk dynasty (1206–1290; Mamluk = military slave). Babur 26-30 and Humayun 30-40 continued it. Akbar 56-05 abolished it in 1564 as part of his Sulh-i-Kul (Universal Peace) policy. Aurangzeb 58-07 reintroduced it in 1679.
  • Zulfiqar Khan (Wazir of Jahandar Shah 12-13 abolished it again in 1712. The handout says Farukhsiyar (1713–19) re-introduced it in 1717. As the empire weakened, Jizya faded with it.
Handout alert

Who first introduced Jizya? Muhammad bin Qasim introduced it in 712 CE after conquering Sindh. By paying it, the non-Muslim population was granted the status of Zimmis (Dhimmis), "protected people", allowed to practise their religion and exempted from military service. (Handout aside: is this a gain or a loss of employment opportunity for the masses?) Who first institutionalised Jizya? Qutb-ud-din Aibak 06-10.

Memory hook

First vs formal: Qasim is Quick, Aibak is Administrative.

"Qasim Quick (712 CE, Sindh); Aibak = Administration (institutionalised)."

Decode: Muhammad bin Qasim introduced it first, after conquering Sindh in 712 CE; payers became Zimmis (protected people), allowed their religion and exempt from military service. Aibak 06-10 first institutionalised it.

Why it sticks: two different verbs, two different men: "introduced" vs "institutionalised".

Note

Discrepancy to check. The handout text says Farukhsiyar re-introduced Jizya in 1717. The teacher's slide says Farukhsiyar continued the abolition but later re-imposed Jizya, and that Jizya fades from 1719. Both agree on the direction: abolished 1712, re-imposed by Farukhsiyar, gone by 1719.

Memory hook

Jizya's light switch: ON, ON, OFF, ON, OFF, ON, fade.

Qasim 712 ON; Aibak 06-10 ON (institutional); Akbar 56-05 OFF 1564; Aurangzeb 58-07 ON 1679; Zulfiqar Khan OFF 1712; Farukhsiyar 13-19 ON 1717; fades 1719.

Decode: Babur 26-30 and Humayun 30-40 continued it. Off 64, on 79, off 12, on 17. Handout says Farukhsiyar re-introduced it in 1717; the teacher's slide says he continued the abolition but later re-imposed it. Both agree: abolished 1712, re-imposed by Farukhsiyar, gone by 1719.

Why it sticks: three flips back and forth, then the bulb dies with the empire.

3.2 Temple policy: Kashi Vishwanath (1669)

  • Aurangzeb 58-07 refused temple repairs and reduced their land grants. More significantly he demolished important temples.
  • Best-known case: the Kashi Vishwanath temple at Varanasi, demolished 1669, replaced on the same site by the Gyanvapi Mosque. His own court record, the Maasir-i-Alamgiri, documents his order.
  • The temple's troubled history: razed earlier by Qutb-ud-din Aibak 06-10 (1194, then slave-general of Md Ghori, before he became first Sultan in 1206) and Sikandar Lodi (1490). Ahilyabai Holkar of Indore rebuilt it in 1780 near the original site.
Memory hook

Kashi Vishwanath: three break, one builds (11, 14, 16, 17).

"Aibak Sikandar Aurangzeb break; Ahilyabai builds": 1194, 1490, 1669, 1780.

Decode: Aibak 06-10 razed it in 1194 (then Ghori's slave-general); Sikandar Lodi in 1490; Aurangzeb 58-07 demolished it in 1669 (Gyanvapi Mosque on the site; court record Maasir-i-Alamgiri); Ahilyabai Holkar of Indore rebuilt it in 1780 near the original site.

Why it sticks: the first two digits climb 11 → 14 → 16 → 17, and the builder comes last.

3.3 Executions that backfired

  • 1675: Guru Tegh Bahadur (GTB), the 9th Sikh Guru. 1689: Chhatrapati Sambhaji 80-89, elder son of Shivaji 74-80, after weeks of torture. Both were offered convert-or-die; both refused.
  • The executions did not suppress resistance: they created martyrs, hardening anti-Mughal sentiment among Sikhs and Marathas.
Memory hook

Martyr codes: Sambhaji 80-89 ends where he was executed.

GTB = 9th Sikh Guru, 1675. Sambhaji 80-89, elder son of Shivaji 74-80, executed 1689: his code ends in 89.

Decode: both were offered convert-or-die, both refused; Sambhaji 80-89 after weeks of torture. Executions created martyrs and hardened Sikh and Maratha anti-Mughal feeling.

Why it sticks: the end of his code is the year of his death.

Teacher's board

Both GTB (1675) and Sambhaji 80-89 (1689) are boxed on the board as the "execution of leaders" line, under social power sad.

Memory hook

Aurangzeb 58-07's four black dates: 69, 75, 79, 89.

"Kashi, Guru, Jizya, Sambhaji" = 1669, 1675, 1679, 1689.

Decode: Kashi Vishwanath demolished 1669; Guru Tegh Bahadur executed 1675; Jizya re-introduced 1679; Sambhaji 80-89 executed 1689. All four hit social power.

Why it sticks: steps of +6, +4, +10, and the rhythm "sixty-nine, seventy-five, seventy-nine, eighty-nine".

4 · Why did the Mansabdari system turn against the empire?#

  • The Mansabdari system ran on personal loyalty to the emperor as an individual, not impersonal loyalty to the empire as an institution.
  • It was a give-and-take: the emperor granted Mansabs and Jagirs; nobles gave military service and loyalty. Strong emperors made this work; weak emperors could not.
  • After 1707 the Later Mughals could not control powerful Mansabdars. When the emperor could not grant the desired Mansabs and Jagirs, the Mansabdars became disloyal and acted upon that disloyalty (earlier, either fear or belief in the fairness of a strong emperor kept them in line): they engaged in court politics for the best Mansabs and Jagirs, or tried replacing the emperor with their favourite from the royal family.
  • Court politics crippled governance. Those who should have led reforms (economic, administrative and military) were consumed by factional rivalry.
Teacher's board

Busy in court politics and not on work. That is the teacher's one-line reason why reform slowed down.

Why the Mansabdari bargain failed

Memory hook

Strong king = Freeze, weak king = Free-for-all.

Strong emperor: Faith (good strong) or Fear (bad strong) makes the unhappy Mansabdar freeze. Weak emperor: the Mansabdar is free to act.

Decode: an unhappy Mansabdar (no desired Mansab or Jagir) acts only under a weak emperor: lobbying, backbiting, takhtapalat (coup) to bring his favourite from the royal family. Result: busy in court politics, not on work.

Why it sticks: the same feeling of disloyalty exists in both cases; only the king's strength decides whether it acts.

5 · Why did the Mughal military weaken?#

Three interconnected problems:

5.1 No technological or organisational reform

  • European powers succeeded with smaller numbers because of better command, tighter discipline, and tactical innovations:
  • File firing: rotating lines for continuous fire.
  • Grapeshot artillery: multiple bullets in one shot; lethal at close range.
  • Bayonet (a knife) on the Brown Bess musket (so a rifle became a sword at close range).
  • Haider Ali 61-82, Mysore) reorganised his military on the European model and stayed formidable. The Mughals, paralysed by factionalism, reformed nothing.
Memory hook

European tricks: File, Grape, Bayonet.

A file of men fire in turns; the shot sprays like grapes; the Brown Bess grows a knife, the bayonet.

Decode: file firing = rotating lines for continuous fire; grapeshot = many bullets in one shot, lethal at close range; bayonet on the Brown Bess musket turns a rifle into a sword. Haider Ali 61-82 (Mysore) reorganised on a European model (the slide says the French model, the handout says European) and stayed formidable.

Why it sticks: F-G-B runs from long range to close range to hand-to-hand.

Teacher's board

No reform while Indian rivals upgraded the military with European help. Example in the handout: Haider Ali 61-82 reorganised on the French model (the slide says "on the French model"; the handout says "European model").

5.2 Weak war commanders

Under the Later Mughals (1707–1858), court politics, not merit, decided military appointments.

5.3 Military corruption

  • Under weak supervision Mansabdars maintained far fewer cavalry than their Sawar rank required, pocketing the difference. Aurangzeb 58-07's war drain and the Jagirdari crisis compounded this.
  • By the early 18th century the army was corrupt, under-equipped, poorly led and technologically frozen.
Memory hook

Three military problems: N-W-C.

"No reform, Weak commanders, Corruption."

Decode: no technological or organisational reform; weak war commanders (court politics, not merit); military corruption (Mansabdars kept far fewer cavalry than their Sawar rank, pocketing the difference).

Why it sticks: the paper army and the real army do not match: Sawar on paper, fewer horses in the field.

Teacher's board

The Jagirdari crisis increased the chances of military corruption (written at the bottom of the slide).

Factionalism = Royal vs Royal + Royal vs Noble + Noble vs Noble. It is group politics.

Memory hook

Five factions: "It's The Hot Hyderabadi Dum".

Irani, Turani, Hindustani, Hindu, Deccani.

Decode: Irani = Shia Persian, Zulfiqar Khan. Turani = Sunni Turkish-Uzbek (like the Mughals), Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48. Hindustani = converted Indian Muslims, Sayyid Brothers (brought Farukhsiyar 13-19 to power). Hindu = Rajputs and Jats, Sawai Jai Singh 99-43. Deccani = Bijapur and Golconda nobles (no example given).

Why it sticks: "Z-N-S-J" gives the four examples in order: Zulfiqar, Nizam, Sayyids, Jai Singh 99-43.

  • After 1707 weak emperors could not control these groups. Mansabdars competed to dominate the sitting emperor or install their preferred candidate: whoever controlled the emperor controlled Mansabs and Jagirs.
  • No clear succession rule: every emperor's death was potentially a new power struggle.
  • Some Mansabdars who could have stabilised the centre chose not to, and instead used the chaos to carve out their own principalities (the successor states).

7 · The Jagirdari crisis: why did it hit so hard?#

The Jagirdari crisis was a structural mismatch: supply of Jagirs and demand for them fell dangerously out of balance.

Teacher's board

Water-crisis analogy: number of people up, water down. Jagirdari crisis: number of Mansabdars up + land down.

Teacher's board

What? (1) Over-recruitment (number of Mansabdars rises). (2) Land is the same in the kingdom; land shrunk because of loss of territory in wars (post-1705, the Marathas winning).

Teacher's board

Why over-recruitment? (a) Role of Aurangzeb 58-07: he declared most lands of Bijapur 1686 and Golconda 1687 as Khalisa lands (LR to the King), but the Deccani group came in as Mansabdars. (b) Post-1707 factionalism: either to be in power or to come to power, the emperors over-recruited.

Jagirdari crisis = water crisis.

More mouths, less water: more Mansabdars, less land.

Decode: demand-supply mismatch. Land shrank through territory loss (post-1705, Marathas winning); over-recruitment came from Aurangzeb 58-07's Deccani nobles and from post-1707 factionalism. The teacher: the impact of the Jagirdari crisis was the most important in the decline of the Mughals.

Why it sticks: you know the water crisis already; just swap people for Mansabdars and water for Jagirs.

Memory hook

Four causes: K-O-I-L (the Koel).

"Khalisa grab, Over-recruitment, Inequality, Luxury inflation."

Decode: after Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687) Aurangzeb 58-07 declared most of their lands Khalisa; later Mughals over-recruited to buy loyalty; 5% of Mansabdars controlled 61% of Jagir revenue; luxury inflation (partly from EIC exports).

Why it sticks: the Koel's nest is crowded and unequal, and "5 and 61" = 5% of Mansabdars hold 61% of the Jagir revenue.

Teacher's board

9 · How the Marathas exposed the military collapse#

The military decline is best measured by what the Marathas achieved.

  • From 1705 they systematically defeated Mughal forces. By the mid-18th century they had not only recovered Aurangzeb 58-07's Deccan gains but taken lands annexed under Akbar 56-05, including Gujarat. Only Hyderabad (formerly Golconda) stayed outside Maratha control, held by Nizam as a successor State.
  • 1737: Peshwa Bajirao 20-40 marched to Delhi, plundered the capital, and briefly held Md Shah prisoner.
  • 1738: the Marathas defeated a large Mughal army commanded by Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48. Formalised in the Treaty of Bhopal (1738): the Marathas received legal recognition of control over all lands between the Chambal and Narmada and the Subah of Malwa.
  • Imagine the Mughal emperor became a hostage in his own capital.

10 · Nadir Shah's invasion (1738–40): the five-pillar blow#

Teacher's board

A State has a territory motive, but does a cost-benefit analysis: (+) own resources (economic, military) and (+) decides on the basis of geography. Example: ruling territory = a cost; maintaining territory = a cost. War aims: annexation, or less than annexation, e.g. plunder (attack and loot). Nadir Shah's aim was plunder.

  • Nadir Shah ruled a Persian empire stretching into Afghanistan (Mughal-claimed territory) and desired India's wealth. His victory was easier because of the weakened Mughal empire plus factionalism.

PoliticalMd Shah (1719–48) was publicly humiliated and did not stand up for his own dignity. For Mansabdars watching, an emperor who could not stand for his dignity wasn't worth defending.
SocialNadir Shah's forces massacred roughly two lakh civilians of Delhi. Whatever social legitimacy remained evaporated in the streets of the capital.

11 · India by the mid-18th century#

  • 1752: the Mughal emperor came under Maratha protection, accepting Maratha military guardianship against further Afghan invasions. The protector had become the protected.
  • 1754: the Marathas installed Alamgir II 54-59 as their puppet emperor, a Mughal ruler chosen by the very people the Mughals had spent decades trying to suppress.
  • By the mid-18th c the Mughal Empire as a functional State had ceased to exist. What remained was a collection of regional powers, each with its own territory, army and foreign policy. The Maratha State stood at the top.
  • The emperor continued to sit in Delhi, technically retaining symbolic sovereignty until 1858, but his actual authority extended little beyond Delhi and Agra. A ceremonial figurehead kept alive because his symbolic legitimacy was useful to others.
Teacher's board

Multiple regional powers = Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad (successor states); Jats, Marathas, Rohilkhand and Farukhabad (rebel states; the Afghans); Mysore, Rajputs, Travancore (already autonomous). No. 1 power = Marathas. The emperor's symbolic sovereignty and the regional powers are both boxed.

Memory hook

Three kinds of regional power: BAH, JMRF, MRT.

Successors BAH: Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad ("Bah!"). Rebels JMRF: Jats, Marathas, Rohilkhand, Farukhabad ("Just Make Rotis Fresh"). Already autonomous MRT: Mysore, Rajputs, Travancore.

Decode: the teacher's classification of the mid-18th-century map; Rohilkhand and Farukhabad are the Afghans. No. 1 power = Marathas.

Why it sticks: three groups of 3, 4, 3: successors, rebels, autonomous.

Teacher's board

Method: read as independent statements. (i) Read A and mark T/F. (ii) Read R and mark T/F. (iii) If both are True, THEN THINK: is R the reason for A?
