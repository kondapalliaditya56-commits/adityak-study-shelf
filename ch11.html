The Subsidiary Alliance was control without annexation: Indian States kept the flag, the EIC took the army and Foreign Policy.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Money rules the chapter: high war expenditure produced the ban on conquest (1784), Wellesley 98-05 got round the ban with the Napoleon threat and the Subsidiary Alliance (control without annexation), and high war expenditure again got him recalled.

  1. Three brothers, one mission. Richard Wellesley 98-05 becomes Governor General of Bengal in 1798, an empire builder who knows expansion raises his profile with a nationalistic British public. His brothers join him: Arthur, a Royal Army officer commanding the 33rd Regiment of Foot (not an EIC officer), and Henry, who handles the administration and diplomacy of the EIC. In 1800 Richard founds Fort William College, Calcutta, to train British civil servants in Indian languages, laws and customs.
    → But Richard has a legal problem: conquest is banned.
  2. The money rule. GBF 1770 leads to the EIC's bankruptcy by 1772; the British State lends money but brings the EIC under supervision through the Regulating Act 1773, so it will not waste taxpayers' money on wars. Then the 1st Anglo-Maratha War (1775-82) and the 2nd Anglo-Mysore War (1780-84) mean high war expenditure, and Pitt's India Act 1784 bans conquest: the Governor General may fight only in defence, otherwise he needs the Court of Directors or the Board of Control.
    → So Richard needs a threat big enough to justify fighting.
  3. Napoleon knocks at Egypt. In 1798 Napoleon attacks Egypt to control the Middle East and hurt British trade, and he is in contact with Tipu 82-99 of Mysore. Wellesley 98-05 overhypes a Napoleon and Tipu 82-99 alliance. The threat was there but not real: Britain's navy was superior, and the British had already burnt French ships in the Mediterranean.
    → But the British State agrees anyway, and wants a cheaper path.
  4. The middle path. Protecting India from European rivals is the primary goal of British foreign policy, so the State does not take risks. Wellesley 98-05 offers the Subsidiary Alliance: political domination without the cost, administrative burden and symbolism of full annexation. The teacher's line: SA = control without annexation. The idea is older: Dupleix 42-54 at Hyderabad in 1751 and Clive 57-67 at Awadh in 1765 (a mini SA).
    → Now Wellesley 98-05 turns the idea into a policy, written down.
  5. The model treaty. Wellesley 98-05 formalises the relationship in a written treaty, takes over Foreign Policy and disbands the State's army, State by State. The EIC gets five things: a Resident, a military alliance, the State's army disbanded and replaced, an annual subsidy and Foreign Policy. The ruler gets two promises: protection, and non-interference in succession, Diwani and Nizamat (minus military). Taking Foreign Policy closes a loophole: the army was paid by the State's subsidy, so only war and peace in EIC hands gave the EIC the right to deploy it.
    → So why would any ruler sign?
  6. Why rulers walked in. Five arguments: protection against internal revolts, protection against external threats (Chauth raids, Mysore, Rohillas), a subsidy cheaper than annexation, royal dignity preserved, and help in succession disputes and against neighbours. The EIC made military help conditional on signing. The teacher tags the fifth argument FACTIONALISM.
    → But once signed, the chain starts to bite.
  7. The vicious chain. The State's army is disbanded and Mansabdars lose their military role; the EIC army grows free of cost; the subsidy is high and rising (Drain of Wealth); taxes rise and peasants revolt; the EIC charges maladministration though the ruler has no power; territory is taken in lieu of cash (half of Awadh 1801, Berar 1853); and no alliances against the EIC remain possible. The teacher compares it to Bali of Ramayana: a military power shift.
    → Let us see who signed, and why.
  8. The signing spree, 1798-1805. Hyderabad signs first in 1798, for protection against the Marathas and Mysore. Mysore signs in 1799 after Tipu 82-99 is killed at Seringapatam. Awadh in 1801: Sadat Ali Khan II had offered Rs 76 lakh in 1798 for John Shore 93-98 removing his nephew Wazir Ali (1797-98); he defaulted and Henry Wellesley imposed a full SA. The Gaikwad signs at Cambay in June 1802, the Peshwa Bajirao II 96-18 at Bassein in December 1802 after Yashwantrao Holkar defeats him, Scindia in 1803, the Jats, Rajputs and Bundelas in 1803-05, and Travancore in 1805 after a Nair mutiny.
    → But some land was not left to alliances at all.
  9. Land taken outright. Wellesley 98-05 also annexes directly: Mysore territory in 1799 (to the Madras Presidency), half of Awadh in 1801 (the Doab, Gorakhpur, Rohilkhand: revenue twice the Rs 76 lakh subsidy, later the Ceded Districts, NWP 1836), and the Carnatic in 1801 (maladministration on record, the Nawab's many loans as context). In the 2nd Anglo-Maratha War (1803-05) Bhonsle loses Orissa and Scindia loses Gujarat and everything north of the Yamuna, including Delhi.
    → But every war costs money.
  10. The bill, and the recall. Wars are expensive for both winner and loser. The board repeats: loan and Regulating Act 1773, but still wars; Pitt's India Act 1784, but still wars. Under Wellesley 98-05 the 4th Anglo-Mysore War 1799 and the 2nd Anglo-Maratha War 1803-05 again mean high war expenditure, and he is recalled to London. Travancore in 1805 is the last SA of his term.
    → The last two signatories come under his successor.
  11. The last two of ten. Under Francis Hastings 13-23, the Bhonsle of Nagpur signs in 1816 (Appa Sahib wins the Nagpur regency fight with EIC backing) and Holkar of Indore signs at Mandsaur in 1818 after defeat in the 3rd Anglo-Maratha War. The teacher's count: ten SAs in 20 years, eight under Wellesley 98-05 and two under Francis Hastings 13-23. Factionalism brought the Peshwa, Bhonsle and Gaikwad; war brought Scindia and Holkar.

In simple words#

  • Richard Wellesley 98-05 was joined in India by his two brothers: Arthur (Royal Army, not an EIC officer) and Henry (administration and diplomacy).
  • Pitt's India Act 1784 had banned conquest because of high war expenditure
  • Wellesley 98-05 got round it by overhyping the Napoleon and Tipu 82-99 threat, which was there but not real
  • His middle path was the Subsidiary Alliance:
    • control without annexation. The EIC got a Resident, an army, a subsidy and Foreign Policy
    • The ruler got protection and a promise of non-interference
  • The seven-link vicious chain (unemployment, free army, Drain of Wealth, tax hikes, maladministration charge, territory for cash, no alliances) left every signatory annexed or dependent.
  • Ten SAs in 20 years (1798 to 1818): eight under Wellesley 98-05 and two under Francis Hastings 13-23. Wellesley 98-05 was recalled because his wars again meant high war expenditure.

1.1 Arthur Wellesley: a Royal Army officer, not an EIC officer

  • Arthur Wellesley was a military commander, but he was NOT an EIC officer.
  • He was a commissioned officer in the British Royal Army, commanding the 33rd Regiment of Foot (that is, infantry).
  • He led his Royal regiment alongside EIC forces in the 4th Anglo-Mysore War (1798–99), including the Siege of Seringapatam (1799), and in the 2nd Anglo-Maratha War (1803–05), including the Battle of Assaye (1803).
  • He also fought Napoleon's army between 1808 and 1815.
Teacher's board
  • "Br State helped EIC in India, e.g. 3rd CW 1756-63
  • Therefore the EIC GOT HELP of the Br State in Empire BUILDING in India." Arthur shows that the British State's own army fought the EIC's wars.
  • Royal help goes back to the 3rd Carnatic War (1756-63).
  • The empire was built by the Company and the British State together, not by a private company alone.

1.2 Two European armies in India (3rd Carnatic War 1756–63 till 1858)

  • From the 3rd Carnatic War (1756-63) till 1858, two separate European military forces operated in India side by side:

Handout note
  • The handout's note: the Company State in India received direct military support from the British Crown.
  • Hence the British State cannot escape responsibility for empire building in India.
  • Key phrase: "EIC had to reimburse the British State" (the Crown lent its regiments, the Company paid the bill).

1.3 Arthur's own verdict on the Marathas

  • Arthur Wellesley on record stated that the Marathas were the most formidable enemy he ever faced, and that the Battle of Assaye (1803) against them was the toughest fight of his entire military career.
  • The Marathas were harder to defeat militarily.
Key takeaway
  • The handout's conclusion: had India not been a fragmented polity, that is, had there been one State ruling the whole of India, India would not have been colonised.
  • This is a ready Mains line on why the EIC won.

  • Dupleix 42-54 had discovered the template: an external army at the Indian State's cost, with a foreign adviser at court who would not just advise but establish indirect rule.
  • Clive 57-67's mini SA at Awadh was the British version, very close to the French template that Dupleix 42-54 had pioneered.

3.1 Wellesley 98-05's step: from a device to a policy

  • Wellesley 98-05 took the next step. He formalised the relationship in a written treaty, took over the Indian State's Foreign Policy explicitly, and disbanded its army.
  • The Subsidiary Alliance was NOT a Wellesley 98-05 invention.
  • BUT the Policy of Subsidiary Alliance was initiated by him.
  • Why "policy": a policy means a pre-meditated, formalised plan of action with a pre-decided intent, which is reflected in a series of events executed in line with that policy.
  • Wellesley 98-05 formalised the SA into a policy, added the takeover of Foreign Policy and the disbanding of the Indian State's army, and implemented this policy State by State across India.
Key takeaway
  • Invention versus policy. The idea is older: Dupleix 42-54 (Hyderabad, 1751) and Clive 57-67 (Awadh, ToA 1765).
  • The policy is Wellesley 98-05's: a written treaty, Foreign Policy taken over, the army disbanded, applied State by State.
  • If a statement says "Wellesley 98-05 invented the Subsidiary Alliance", it is wrong
  • If it says he "initiated the Policy of Subsidiary Alliance", it is right

From proto-SA to the full Policy of Subsidiary Alliance

  • 1751, Hyderabad (Dupleix 42-54):
    • French force under Bussy stationed, paid from the Northern Sarkars
    • A foreign adviser at court
  • 1765, Awadh (Clive 57-67, ToA 1765)

5.1 Links 1 and 2: unemployment and the EIC's free army

  • Link 1. Disbanding Indian State armies caused mass unemployment for military men.
  • Mansabdars lost their positions
  • Sepoys lost their livelihoods.

  • Since it had also taken over Foreign Policy, it could deploy these armies for its own wars elsewhere.
  • Hence the EIC could now maintain a large army at the expense of Indian States.
  • The handout's note: EIC Armies = the army of the Indian State maintained by the EIC using the Indian State's subsidy + the EIC's own army maintained from its own money.
  • Both were available to the EIC for its own wars.

5.2 Links 3 and 4: Drain of Wealth and tax hikes

  • Instead there was Drain of Wealth.

5.3 Links 5 and 6: maladministration and territory in lieu of cash

  • Link 5. The EIC then accused Indian rulers of maladministration.
  • In reality the ruler had no real power left.
  • The British Resident had in many cases established Indirect Rule illegally, in direct breach of the treaty's own promise of non-interference.
  • The ruler had no power, but all the responsibility.
  • The most stark example was Nawab Wajid Ali Shah of Awadh: accused of maladministration, his State annexed in 1856.
  • Link 6. When the subsidy could not be paid in cash, the EIC simply took territory as payment. In 1801, half of Awadh was annexed as permanent subsidy payment. In 1853, Hyderabad was made to give Berar.
  • The handout's aside: the EIC can say "Thanks, Dupleix 42-54".
  • He gave this idea when he took the Northern Sarkars from Hyderabad in 1751.
Teacher's board
  • Under "Territory in lieu of cash": "Subsidy = TOOL of empire building." The subsidy was set high, kept rising, and on default the EIC took land: the subsidy itself turned an "alliance" into annexation.
  • Two examples to memorise:
    • 1801, half of Awadh
    • 1853, Berar from Hyderabad.

5.4 Link 7 and the takeaway

  • Link 7. By losing Foreign Policy and military control, Indian States permanently lost the ability to form alliances against the EIC.
  • Hence the Subsidiary Alliance was, in effect, a sophisticated Divide and Rule instrument at the level of an entire subcontinent.
Key takeaway

Did Wellesley 98-05 invent the SA?No. He initiated it as a policy: written treaty, Foreign Policy taken over, army disbanded, applied State by State.
What is a "policy" in the handout's words?A pre-meditated formalised plan of action with pre-decided intent, reflected in a series of events executed in line with it.
The five terms the EIC got?British Resident; formal military alliance; State's army disbanded and replaced by an EIC army; annual subsidy; Foreign Policy.
The two terms the Indian ruler got?EIC protection against neighbours and domestic threats; a promise of non-interference in succession, Diwani and Nizamat (minus military).
What did "Foreign Policy" cover?War and peace, diplomacy, defence budget and foreign trade; GG's consent before any European at court; consult the GG before negotiating with any State or power.
The board's split of sovereignty under the SA?External functions (Defence, Communications, Foreign Affairs) taken by the EIC; domestic functions and symbolic sovereignty with the Indian king.
The legal logic of taking Foreign Policy?The army was paid by the State's subsidy, so its deployment could legally rest with the ruler; taking war and peace closed that loophole.
The PoSA formula?PoIR (informally) + PoRF + EIC military replacing the State's military + annual subsidy + loss of Foreign Policy.
Why is "a fixed income for the Company" not a feature of the SA?The subsidy was earmarked only for maintaining the army; it was not surplus income.
Why did the EIC recruit sepoys from outside the host State?To cut any loyalty to the Nawab if he revolted: Divide and Rule.
What does the Bali analogy stand for?"SA led to military power shift": every SA increased the EIC's army free of cost while the Indian State lost its own.
Starkest example of the maladministration link?Nawab Wajid Ali Shah of Awadh: accused of maladministration, State annexed in 1856.
Which territories made up "half of Awadh" in 1801?The Doab, Gorakhpur and Rohilkhand: the Ceded Districts, later the North-West Provinces (NWP 1836).
How many SAs did the teacher count, and what did he strike?Ten (struck Nine and 9), with 8 under Wellesley 98-05 (struck seven) and 2 under Francis Hastings 13-23; Jats-Rajputs-Bundelas is no. 7.
The teacher's tag for argument 5, winning succession disputes?FACTIONALISM: rival groups fight over the throne or regency and the side seeking EIC help has to sign the SA.
The teacher's lesson written under the Hyderabad and Mysore rows?Had India had one common centre (one State ruling all), this would not have happened; "Don't lose wars" (Mysore signed after losing the 4th Anglo-Mysore War).
The teacher's FACTIONALISM or WAR tags for the Marathas?Factionalism: Peshwa, Bhonsle, Gaikwad. War: Scindia and Holkar.
The Awadh oval on the board?Left half 1801 (arrow to NWP 1836), right half 1856; "1877: Both merged".
Awadh's Nawabs on the margin list?Sadat 22-39, Safdar Jung 39-53, Shuja 54-75, Asaf-ud-Dowla 75-97, Wazir Ali [1797-98], then his uncle Sadat II Ali Khan [1798+].
Scindia's date of signing as per the teacher?1803 or 1804, both inside the 2nd Anglo-Maratha War (1803-05).
The teacher's list of empire builders?Clive 57-67 and Wellesley 98-05 (18th c); F. Hastings 13-23 and Dalhousie 48-56 (19th c).
Carnatic 1801: recorded reason and the teacher's context?Recorded: maladministration, six years after Md Ali 52-95 died. Context: the Nawab had taken many loans from the EIC and its officers (financial maladministration).
Where did Mysore's territory go in 1799?To the Madras Presidency.
Why was Wellesley 98-05 recalled, as the teacher's board puts it?Wars are expensive for both winner and loser: the 4th Anglo-Mysore War 1799 and the 2nd Anglo-Maratha War 1803-05 ran up high war expenditure ("but still wars" after the 1773 loan and the 1784 ban).
