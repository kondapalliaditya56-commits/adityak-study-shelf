The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: The peasant now used the master's law and the educated gave him a voice, so each revolt (Indigo, Pabna, Deccan, Punjab) ended in a legal relief and left behind organisation and voice: the rehearsal, and the soil, for the national movement.

  1. 1857 changes the way of resisting. After 1857 the peasants did not just pick up weapons: they filed court cases and organised themselves, using the colonial legal system against colonial rule. The Western Educated Middle Class (WEMC) began to speak for the ryot in newspapers, courts and associations. The teacher's ladder says modern politics tries non-violent methods first: courts, press, peaceful protest.
    → So the first great test came in Bengal, over a blue dye.
  2. 1788: a trap that looked like a contract. The EIC began promoting indigo in Bengal in 1788 with loans to ten European planters, preferring them to zamindars. Nij farming (the planter buys land) was costly, so planters moved to the Ryoti system: lease land from zamindars, then contract with ryots to grow indigo on at least 25% of their land, with low-interest loans, seeds and seed drills, and the planter buying the crop.
    → But the contract turned into a noose.
  3. The squeeze. Indigo ruined the soil, the price was not remunerative, and the ryot borrowed one loan to repay another, tied by force of finance. A Stock Exchange crisis then cut global indigo prices, and a sympathetic Lieutenant Governor of Bengal told Collectors to side with ryots against forced cultivation.
    → So the ryots finally had room to act.
  4. The ryots go to court. They refused new loans and indigo, boycotted the planters' Indian agents, and began a no-rent campaign when planters sued (the teacher: the contract was legal). When planters tried eviction, the ryots organised and went to court under the Rent Act of 1859. This was Non-Cooperation decades before Gandhi.
    → Then the educated took up their pens.
  5. The educated join. DeenBandhu Mitra's play Neel Darpan (1860) shocked the public; Michael Madhusudhan Datta translated it and James Long published it, and for that Long was convicted of libel, fined Rs 1,000 (paid by Kaliprasanna Sinha) and jailed a month. Hindu Patriot and Somprakash ran editorials, and the British Indian Association (1851) of educated zamindars backed the ryots, partly from self-interest.
    → Now the system had to give way, but not all at once.
  6. Indigo ends slowly. The Indigo Commission (1860) said honour old contracts, refuse new ones. By 1863 indigo moved to Bihar as Tinkathia (3/20th of land). The long-term death blow was BASF's synthetic indigo in 1897 (Prelims 2020), with a short WWI revival, and Champaran (1917) and the Act of 1918 ended Tinkathia.
    → Meanwhile in Bengal another villain appeared: not a European, but an Indian zamindar.
  7. Pabna, 1873: a league and a split. Zamindars violated the occupancy rights of the Rent Act 1859, raised rents illegally and collected Abwabs. The ryots formed Agrarian Leagues, pooled money for court, and Ishan Chandra Roy spread them. The WEMC split: Hindu Patriot and Amrita Bazar Patrika for the zamindars, Bengalee (SNB) and the Anglo-Indian press for the ryots. Ripon 80-84's Rent Commission (1880) led to the Bengal Tenancy Act 1885: occupancy rights after more than twelve years.
    → Now across to the Deccan, where the villain was the moneylender.
  8. The Deccan's bond trap. No-tax came in 1873-74 against a Land Revenue hike. The Limitation Law 1859 allowed interest for three years only, so moneylenders made a fresh bond with the interest added to the principal. The Poona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870) gathered 17,000 signatures; moneylenders refused loans; in 1875 the ryots burned debt bonds, not lives. The Relief Act came in 1879, and a second no-tax campaign in 1896-97 over refused remission.
    → Then to Punjab, where religion and canals shaped the revolt.
  9. Punjab: a sect and a canal bill. The Namdhari (Kuka) sect under Baba Ram Singh turned from violence against cow slaughterers and priests to violence against the British, and he was exiled to Burma in 1872 (the teacher: not imp). In 1907 a bill to control inheritance and raise water rates in the canal colonies brought the Lahore Indian Association, the Arya Samaj and the Singh Sabha into a no-tax campaign. The British repressed, then quietly withdrew it, fearing the Army, recruited heavily from Punjab, might follow.
    → Put the pieces together and a pattern shows.
  10. The rehearsal. Across Bengal, Maharashtra and Punjab, peasants organised, used the colonial legal system and found the WEMC lending its voice. It was not yet the national movement but the soil in which it would grow: non-cooperation, no-tax, boycott and legal challenge were all rehearsed here before Champaran (1917) and Non-Cooperation (1920).

In simple words#

  • After 1857 peasants did not just fight: they went to court and organised, and the Western Educated Middle Class (WEMC) spoke for them in plays, papers and associations.
  • In the Indigo Revolt (1859-63) Bengal's ryots refused indigo, boycotted the planters' agents, stopped paying rent and used the Rent Act 1859
  • The Indigo Commission 1860 and, later, BASF's synthetic dye (1897) ended the system
  • Pabna (1873) shows organisation (Agrarian Leagues) and a split WEMC
  • The Bengal Tenancy Act 1885 gave occupancy rights after twelve years
  • In Maharashtra the moneylenders' debt-bond trap led to the Deccan Riots (1875) and the Relief Act (1879)
  • Punjab saw the Kuka Revolt and the 1907 Canal Colony agitation, which the British withdrew out of fear of a 1857 repeat
  • Together these revolts are the rehearsal: not yet the national movement, but its soil.

1 · What changed after 1857#

Story link

Because 1857 failed as a war, resistance changed its tools. Ask: what do the peasants now use (law, organisation) and who joins them (the WEMC)?

  • The Revolt of 1857 was a turning point not just in British policy but also in the character of Indian resistance.
  • The peasant revolts after 1857 differed from earlier uprisings in two ways:
  • First, peasants now had a greater awareness of British laws and institutions.
  • They did not just pick up weapons: they filed court cases and they organised themselves.
  • They used the legal system that colonial rule had imposed on them as a tool against that very rule.
  • Second, the Western Educated Middle Class (WEMC) began to take up peasant causes.
  • English-educated lawyers, writers, journalists and reformers spoke for the ryot in English newspapers, in courts and in public associations.
  • The peasant was no longer alone.

2 · The Indigo Revolt (1859-63)#

Story link

Because the new tools needed a first test, Bengal's indigo ryots go first. Follow the chain: contract, trap, squeeze, court, voice, end.

2.1 Context: how did indigo cultivation begin?

  • In 1788 the East India Company (EIC) began promoting Indigo cultivation in Bengal by giving loans to ten European planters.
  • The EIC could have given the same loans to local zamindars but chose European planters: preferential treatment from day one.
  • The teacher dates this under Cornwallis 86-93 (his board: "CW 86-93" under "Origin 1788"): 1788 falls inside those years.
Teacher's board
  • Two systems, and the money chain of the second.
  • Nij:
    • The planter buys land directly and cultivates indigo himself
    • this was expensive and complicated (buying large tracts, organising labour, arranging bullocks and ploughs)
  • The scribble beside Nij reads like "Nij = Ryt" (the exact words are unclear).
  • Ryoti (Bengal): the planter leases land from zamindars and contracts with ryots.
  • His drawing shows who pays whom: the ryot pays rent to the European planter, the planter pays a lease amount to the zamindar (the circled Z; across this chapter the teacher's circled Z means the zamindar), and the zamindar pays Land Revenue (LR) to the State.
  • The circle cut into four with "Indigo" in one quarter is the 25% condition: one quarter of the ryot's land goes to indigo.
  • The planters moved from Nij to Ryoti because Nij was costly and complicated.
  • After the Permanent Settlement of 1793 zamindars were large landowners and land had become a commodity that could be sold, rented, leased or mortgaged, so a planter could simply lease land from a zamindar.
  • Under the Ryoti system the planter signed contracts with tenant ryots with three key conditions:
  • (i) The ryot had to grow indigo on at least 25% of the land he occupied.
  • (ii) The planter gave loans at low interest and also sold indigo seeds and seed drills to the ryot.
  • (iii) The planter then purchased the cultivated indigo from the ryot.
  • On paper this looked like a commercial arrangement.

2.2 What were the grievances of the ryots?

  • The ryots had real and compounding grievances:
  • Indigo destroyed soil fertility. Once a plot was used for indigo, its productivity for food crops fell sharply, hurting the ryot's overall income.
  • The price the planter paid was not remunerative.
  • Low fertility plus low prices meant the ryot was perpetually in debt, even though the loan rates were low.
  • To pay back one loan, the ryot took another.
  • Since he could not repay, the planter had grounds to remove him from his occupied land (during the lease, the planter was effectively the de-facto zamindar for that ryot).
  • The cycle of debt kept the ryot tied to indigo "not by force of arms, but by force of finance."
  • Then came two triggers:
  • An international trigger: indigo prices on global markets fell, partly due to a financial crisis on the Stock Exchange (a crisis in financial markets caused a general slowdown in the British economy: people lost money, their buying power fell and demand for goods, including indigo, dropped).
  • Planters then offered the ryots even less. The squeeze became unbearable.
  • An administrative trigger: a sympathetic Lieutenant Governor of Bengal directed District Collectors to take the side of ryots in disputes, on the ground that forced cultivation was against the spirit of capitalism and free-market economics.
  • (The post of Lieutenant Governor of Bengal was created in 1854 to reduce the Governor-General's workload, a change made possible by the Charter Act of 1853.)

2.3 What did the ryots do?

  • The ryots responded with a sophisticated, multi-pronged campaign:
  • (i) They refused to accept new loans and refused to cultivate indigo.
  • (ii) They organised a social boycott of the Indian agents of the planters.
  • This was effectively Non-Cooperation, decades before Mahatma Gandhi.
  • (iii) When planters went to court to enforce contracts, ryots began a no-rent campaign. (Again, Non-Cooperation before Gandhi's era.)
  • (iv) When planters attempted eviction, ryots went to court to enforce their occupancy rights under the Rent Act of 1859.

2.4 The role of the Western Educated Middle Class (WEMC)

Handout note
  • The handout opens this section with a bracket: "The spirit of Fraternity at work here: a key element of Modern Nationalism." The educated class standing up for the oppressed peasant was a new phenomenon in Indian public life.
  • The slide title repeats it: "(spirit of Fraternity)."
  • The WEMC played a decisive role in amplifying the ryots' cause:
  • 1860: Neel Darpan (Blue Mirror). The playwright DeenBandhu Mitra wrote this powerful play depicting the oppression of ryots by indigo planters. It created enormous public awareness.
  • The Bengali poet Michael Madhusudhan Datta translated Neel Darpan into English, and the Christian missionary James Long published it, bringing the issue to the attention of audiences in England.
  • The planters filed a case of libel (maan-haani) against Long.
  • He was convicted, fined Rs 1,000 (paid by Kaliprasanna Sinha) and sentenced to one month's imprisonment.
  • Hindu Patriot (English) and Somprakash (Bengali) published strong editorials in favour of the ryots.
  • Even the British Indian Association (1851), an organisation of educated zamindars, supported the ryots.
  • Their motivation was partly self-interest: they resented the dominance of European planters over the rural economy.

2.5 Result

  • The Indigo Commission (1860) was set up.
  • It ruled that ryots must honour existing contracts but were free to refuse future ones.
  • By 1863 indigo cultivation had largely shifted from Bengal to Bihar, where it operated as the Tinkathia System: essentially the same as the Ryoti System, but here the ryot was forced to grow indigo on 3/20th (three-twentieths) of the land he occupied.
  • The long-term death blow to natural indigo came not from peasant resistance but from chemistry.
  • In 1897 the German company BASF developed synthetic indigo, cheaper and more consistent than the natural product.
  • As synthetic indigo flooded global markets, demand for natural indigo collapsed, destroying whatever economic logic remained in the plantation system. (Prelims 2020.)
  • The Bihar chapter closed only in 1917, when Mahatma Gandhi (MG) launched the Champaran Satyagraha, leading to the Champaran Agrarian Act of 1918, which brought the Tinkathia System to its end.

3 · Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal#

Story link

Because the Rent Act 1859 gave rights that zamindars then broke, a new villain appears. Ask: why does the WEMC split here but not in indigo?

  • The ryots of Pabna district in Bengal had a different grievance.
  • The Rent Act of 1859 had granted them occupancy rights, the right not to be arbitrarily evicted.
  • But zamindars were routinely violating these rights.
  • On top of that, rents were being raised illegally, and illegal extra cesses called Abwabs were being collected.
Teacher's board
  • A different villain, and an extra trick.
  • The teacher heads the slide "Ryot vs Indian Z":
    • In Indigo the enemy was a European planter
    • in Pabna it is an Indian zamindar (circled Z)
  • He adds a (+) point: zamindars evicted tenants before 12 years to prevent the occupancy right from arising (this is why the twelve years in the Bengal Tenancy Act of 1885, below, matters).
  • Above the title: "L ORG [* e.g. LoN 1920]" (a League is an organisation; his example is the League of Nations of 1920; the reading is slightly unclear).
  • The ryots organised themselves into Agrarian Leagues, a formal structure of collective action.
  • They pooled money to fight their cases in court.
  • A leader called Ishan Chandra Roy then replicated the movement in neighbouring districts.
  • (Organisation and legal awareness: the two new features of post-1857 revolts, clearly on display here.)
Teacher's board

Beside Organisation the teacher starred "Modern Politics": an organisation with a name (the Agrarian League) is exactly what the pre-1857 revolts did not have. He underlined Ishan Chandra Roy.

4.2 Bombay Deccan Riots (1875)

Teacher's board
  • A cotton boom and bust behind the riots (not in the handout). 1861-65 US Civil War: supply of raw cotton from the US fell (the US supplied Britain), so the price of cotton rose (underlined), so demand for Indian raw cotton rose (a circled plus), so the ryot was happy (the smiley face).
  • When the civil war ended (underlined), the arrows are read as supply from the US up, price down, demand for Indian cotton down (the three arrows) and the ryot was sad (the sad face).
  • The funnel marks the Bombay Deccan as the raw-cotton region feeding Britain.
  • Boom borrowing followed by bust is the setting for the debt that exploded in 1875.
Handout alert

Reading of slide 51. The three small arrows after "Civil war ended" (up, down, down) and the funnel sketch are not labelled in full. The reading above (US supply up, price down, demand for Indian cotton down) is the natural reverse of the boom column beside it. Check your notebook.

  • The grievances: high Land Revenue under the Ryotwari system, oppression by moneylenders, and loss of land.
  • Background: the Limitation Law of 1859 in Bombay.
  • It ruled that interest on a loan could be applied only for three years, regardless of when the ryot repaid.
  • Moneylenders found a workaround: they made the ryot sign a fresh debt bond after three years, with the accumulated interest now added to the principal.
  • The debt could never truly be paid down.
Teacher's board
  • The arithmetic on the right margin:
    • Rs 100 = loan
    • Three years' interest = the new principal (the fresh bond starts again from the bigger sum)
  • Each bond is bigger than the last, so the debt grows even though the law limits interest to three years.
  • Action: the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870), associated with Ranade (M.G.), collected 17,000 signatures on powers-of-attorney to represent ryots.
  • It successfully convinced the government to declare that confiscation of land should be the last resort in recovering Land Revenue arrears.
Teacher's board
  • Why this body matters: "Regional / for all ppl / ORG" and "= 1st Regional Pol Party of WEMC": the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha was a regional organisation for all the people (that is what "Sarvajanik" says), and the first regional political party of the WEMC.
  • Compare the Lahore Indian Association in section 5.2, which he also calls a "Regional Pol Party of WEMC".
  • Retaliation: moneylenders refused to extend any further loans, leaving ryots unable even to pay their Land Revenue.
  • Violence broke out. The ryots did not attack moneylenders' lives. They burned debt bonds.
  • (An awareness of the instrument of oppression, not just its wielder.)
Teacher's board

Under "burned debt bonds" he wrote "L Proof of Loans": the debt bond is the proof of the loan, so destroying it destroys the moneylender's claim.

  • Result: the Deccan Agriculturists Relief Act of 1879 was passed.
  • It gave protection against loss of land due to indebtedness and regulated interest rates.

5.2 Punjab Canal Colony agitations (1907)

  • In west Punjab, from 1885 to 1940, the British built canals in semi-desert areas to convert them into fertile lands for land revenue income.
  • These areas were called Canal Colonies, and lands were allocated to farmers from other parts of Punjab at no or nominal cost, on the condition that they bring the land under cultivation and pay water rates and land revenue.
  • Some lands were given as reward for civil and military service, while some large plots were sold to large investors.
  • In 1907 the British government introduced a bill that would allow the government to:
  • Control inheritance of land in the canal colonies, and
  • Increase water rates and fines for violations of canal colony regulations.
  • Three organisations mobilised the peasantry: the Lahore Indian Association (1877), the Arya Samaj (1875) and the Singh Sabha (1873).
  • A no-tax campaign began, which later escalated into riots.
Teacher's board

  • So the leadership of the Canal Colony agitation was both religious bodies and a WEMC political body.
  • His drawing shows the canal feeding the fields and a box "Water rates [up]": the colonists' fields depend on the canal, so a rise in water rates hits every farmer.

6 · A thread running through all these revolts#

Story link

Because every region ticked the same boxes, the chapter closes on the pattern. Remember ROLE: rehearsal, organised, law, educated.

  • Look across Bengal, Maharashtra and Punjab and a pattern emerges. In each case:
  • Peasants were not reacting with blind rage.
  • They were organising: forming leagues, pooling resources, filing cases.
  • They were using the tools of the colonial legal system against colonial exploitation.
  • Sections of the WEMC (journalists, lawyers, reformers) were lending their voice.
  • This is not yet the Indian National Movement.
  • But it is the soil in which that movement would grow.

Why did the Maharashtra no-tax campaign of 1873-74 happen?A hike in Land Revenue (the teacher: revised after 30 years in the Ryotwari system in Bombay).
What was the Limitation Law 1859 trap?Interest only for three years, so moneylenders made a fresh bond with the interest added to the principal: the debt never fell.
What did the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870) do?Under M.G. Ranade collected 17,000 signatures on powers-of-attorney; won a ruling that land confiscation be the last resort for Land Revenue arrears; the teacher: the first regional political party of the WEMC.
What did the Deccan rioters burn and why?Debt bonds, the proof of loans, not the moneylenders' lives: awareness of the instrument of oppression.
What was the result of the Bombay Deccan Riots?The Deccan Agriculturists Relief Act 1879: protection against loss of land due to indebtedness and regulated interest rates.
Why did the 1896-97 no-tax campaign happen?The government refused any remission in Land Revenue despite widespread crop failure (the teacher: in Ryotwari, lower LR if bad harvest).
Who were the Kukas?The Namdhari Sikh sect led by Baba Ram Singh: living Guru and Guru Granth Sahib, women's equality, mantras only (Kuka = reciter), anti-priestly, pro-cow; exiled to Burma 1872. The teacher: not imp.
What was the 1907 Canal Colony bill?It would control inheritance of land in canal colonies and raise water rates and fines; Lahore Indian Association, Arya Samaj and Singh Sabha mobilised a no-tax campaign.
Why did the British withdraw the bill?The Army was heavily recruited from Punjab (Sikhs) after 1857; peasant disaffection could spread to it: the teacher's "fear of 1857 Repeat".
Who is a "zamindar" in Punjab (teacher)?A landowner who is a ryot (Mahalwari: most landowners not super rich); Zamin = land, Dar = holder.
What is the common thread of all these revolts?Peasants organised, used the colonial legal system, and the WEMC lent its voice: the rehearsal and soil of the INM, not yet the INM.
Mains practice: "Peasant revolts after 1857 were the rehearsal for the INM." Skeleton?Intro: post-1857 peasants used law and organised, the WEMC helped; soil of the INM. Body: non-cooperation, no-tax, boycott, legal challenge, each tied to Indigo, Maharashtra, Punjab, Pabna, Poona; relief laws 1860, 1879, 1885. Closing: the INM scaled these methods (Champaran 1917, Non-Cooperation 1920); regional bodies as WEMC parties.
Mains practice: "The WEMC's role in peasant causes: examine." Skeleton?Intro: fraternity at work, voice. Body: Indigo (Neel Darpan, Datta, Long, Hindu Patriot, Somprakash, BIA); Pabna split by class (zamindars' sons, selfish interest); Poona Sarvajanik Sabha. Closing: voice and organisation, limited by class interest.
