1995PYQ 3(Prelims 1995)

Answer: (C)

(C) ✓ — Only statement 3 (All India Forward Bloc) is correct. AIFB, founded by Subhas Chandra Bose in 1939 after his break from INC, was an ECI-recognised national-level political party at the time of the 1995 UPSC examination. Statement 1 (Muslim League) — by 1995 the surviving Indian Union Muslim League (IUML) was a state-recognised party in Kerala only.

Statement 2 (Revolutionary Socialist Party) was a state-recognised party in West Bengal and Kerala. Statement 4 (Peasants & Workers Party) was a state-recognised party in Maharashtra only. Therefore only AIFB qualified as a national party. 1.10.

Wrong options:

(A) ✗ — Includes the Muslim League and the Revolutionary Socialist Party, but by 1995 the IUML survived as a state party in Kerala and the RSP as a state-level party (see the ✓ entry above) — only the Forward Bloc carried national status.

(B) ✗ — The RSP and the Peasants and Workers Party were both state-level parties (✓ entry above) — and this code misses the one genuinely national party, the All India Forward Bloc.

(D) ✗ — Collects the three state-level parties and drops the only national one — the All India Forward Bloc (✓ entry above). (Note: These all parties were regional level political parties during pre-independence time in India. However only Indian National congress which had least level presence almost entire nation, can be considered a national party.)

(D) ✗ — A is not false: outside moments like the Sholapur strikes, working-class participation in the early-1930s upsurge remained limited. (Note: The labour leaders were influenced by communist ideology and they looked Congress as a party of bourgeois and their ideology as reactionary. FYQ 1 Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words The INM, while presenting itself as a national movement, marginalised the voices of workers, capitalists, Dalits & women in different ways. Examine.

Demand of the question: "Examine" + "marginalised in different ways" demands tracing how each group was excluded from INC's core agenda.

Suggested intro (~25 words): The INC's mass strategy was built on coalition-management. To hold the coalition together, it consistently subordinated class, caste & gender questions to the national-political question. Body guidance — 4 themes:

1. Workers (see 'The Working Class Movement'). Trade unions were tactically supported (AITUC 1920, Lajpat Rai presidency) but never made central to INC's economic platform. Gandhi's Trusteeship framework rejected class struggle. The INC stayed close to capitalists; trade union autonomy under N.M. Joshi & later CPI was tolerated, not integrated.

2. Capitalists (see 'The Role of Indian Capitalists in the INM'). Funded the INC (FICCI 1927, Bombay Plan 1944) but were marginalised in INC's public ideology — Gandhi's anti-modern, anti-industrial framing was uncomfortable for capitalists. Their political voice was muted publicly, even as their financial role grew.

3. Dalits (see 'The Voice of Dalits'). Ambedkar's Mahad Satyagraha 1927, Round Table Conference advocacy, & 1932 Communal Award protest revealed that INC's framework could not accommodate Depressed Classes as autonomous political actors. Poona Pact 1932 ended separate electorates but left reserved-seats-within-Hindu-electorate as second-best compromise. Ambedkar's 1956 conversion to Buddhism expressed his judgment that Hinduism could not be reformed from within.

4. Women (see 'The Voice of Women'). AIWC 1927 led on women's issues. INC mobilised women for civil disobedience (1930 salt march, 1942 QIM) but did not promote them to organisational or parliamentary leadership — Sarala Debi Chaudhrani's "lawbreakers not lawmakers" line captured this.

Closing verdict: The INC's "Many Voices" framing was strategic absorption, not genuine representation. Each constituency — labour, capital, Dalit, women — found INC useful as a vehicle for some demands but had to develop separate organisational forms for autonomous representation. The post-1947 Indian state inherited this incomplete integration as its structural problem.

Revise: 'Working Class' + 'Capitalists' + 'Dalits' + 'Women'.

FYQ 2 Mains-FYQ · 10 marks · 150 words Examine the historical significance of the Ambedkar-Gandhi confrontation over the Communal Award (1932) for the politics of Dalit representation.

Demand of the question: "Examine the significance" demands tracing the structural meaning of the conflict.

Suggested intro (~25 words): The Ambedkar-Gandhi confrontation over the Communal Award (1932) was not just about electorates — it was about whether Dalits could be politically autonomous from the Hindu fold. Body guidance — 3 themes (see 'The Voice of Dalits'):

1. The Award's framing. Communal Award (August 1932) by Ramsay MacDonald provided separate electorates for Depressed Classes — meaning Dalits could elect their own representatives from their own electorate, NOT from a combined Hindu electorate. This was Ambedkar's position: Dalits as an autonomous political community.

2. Gandhi's Fast Unto Death. Gandhi opposed separate electorates because they would split the "Hindu" fold permanently. His fast unto death at Yerwada Jail forced Ambedkar to accept the Poona Pact compromise (24 September 1932) — reserved seats within joint Hindu electorates. Ambedkar agreed under moral pressure, not conviction.

3. The long shadow. The compromise meant Dalits remained politically attached to the Hindu electorate; their representatives needed Hindu votes to win. Ambedkar saw this as second-best; his later trajectory (1936 Independent Labour Party, 1942 Scheduled Castes Federation, 1956 Buddhist conversion) showed his rejection of the assumption that Dalits could be represented through Hindu-mediated structures.

Closing verdict: The 1932 confrontation was the foundational debate of Dalit political autonomy. Gandhi won the immediate constitutional battle; Ambedkar's long-term diagnosis — that Hindu structures cannot represent Dalits — has shaped Indian politics ever since.

Revise: 'Voice of Dalits — Ambedkar + Poona Pact'.
