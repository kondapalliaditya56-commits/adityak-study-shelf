In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • The Constitution was adopted on 26 Nov 1949 and enforced on 26 Jan 1950. It is the lengthiest written Constitution: 395 Articles, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules at the start, 470, 25 and 12 today.
  • The handout lists 12 salient features: Westminster-style parliamentary government, a blend of rigid and flexible, Fundamental Rights, DPSP, Fundamental Duties, a federation with a strong Centre, adult suffrage, an independent judiciary, a secular State, single citizenship and a flexible separation of powers.
  • Amendment rules depend on the type of majority: simple, effective, absolute and three special majorities. Learn which motion needs which.
  • Critics made eight charges (borrowed, carbon copy of 1935, un-Indian, un-Gandhian, elephantine, lawyers' paradise, inconsistent, Ambedkar's warnings), and each has a reply.
  • The big answer: the framers adapted, not copied, and Ambedkar warned that political democracy needs social and economic democracy.

Key terms

Westminster model
The parliamentary system India chose: real power with the PM and Council of Ministers, responsible to the Lok Sabha.
Effective strength
Total membership minus vacant seats; the base for an effective majority.
Special majority, Type I
Majority of total membership of each House and two-thirds of those present and voting; used for amendment (Art. 368).
Judicial review
The courts' power under Arts. 13, 32 and 226 to check laws and executive acts; makes the judiciary guardian of the Constitution.
Quasi-federal
K.C. Wheare's label: structure federal but tilted to the Centre for unity.
Separation of powers
Montesquieu's idea; in India it is functional and flexible, with deliberate overlaps and checks.
Sarva Dharma Sambhava
Equal respect for all religions; India's model of secularism, with State reform where needed.
Constitutional morality
Ambedkar's reply to his critics: respect for constitutional methods, not mass protests or blind hero-worship.
Remember it as

Count: 395/22/8 to 470/25/12. Rights side: FR (Part III), DPSP (Part IV), FD (Part IVA, 1976; 11th duty 2002). Voting age 21 to 18 (61st, 1988).

1 · The twelve features at a glance#

  • The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and enforced on 26 January 1950. The framers studied many countries' Constitutions and the working of the Government of India Act, 1935, then took what suited India.
  • Memory aid (All twelve in one sentence):
    • "Long Parliaments Blend Fine Debates, Duties
    • Federal Unions Judge Secular Single Separations." Order: Lengthiest, Parliamentary, Blend of rigid/flexible, Fundamental Rights, DPSP, Duties, Federation, Universal suffrage, Judiciary, Secular, Single citizenship, Separation of powers
  • Shortcut: Four clusters. Size and form (1-3), Rights side (4-6), Federal side (7, 11), People and law (8-10, 12). Sort each feature into its cluster and the list stops feeling long.

2 · 1. The lengthiest written Constitution#

  • It is the most detailed written Constitution in the world (written, like the American; unlike the unwritten British).

Size then and now

At adoptionToday (handout)
Articles395470
Parts2225
Schedules812
  • Amendments deleted about 20 Articles and one Part (Part VII) and added nearly 95 new Articles, four Parts (IVA, IXA, IXB, XIVA) and four Schedules (9, 10, 11, 12).
  • Memory aid (Four new Parts): IVA, IXA, IXB, XIVA. Chant it as "IV-A, IX-A, IX-B, XIV-A": the A, A, B, A pattern is all you need.

Why so long (four reasons)

  • Vast geography and diversity needing special provisions.
  • Influence of the lengthy Government of India Act, 1935.
  • One Constitution for both Union and States, unlike federations such as the USA.
  • A lawyer-dominated Constituent Assembly that preferred detailed drafting.
  • Subjects that other democracies leave to convention or ordinary law (electoral system, administrative machinery) are written into this Constitution.

3 · 2. Parliamentary form of government#

  • India took the Westminster model, not the American presidential system, as better suited to its social diversity and democratic traditions. It runs on cooperation between legislature and executive, not strict separation.

Who holds power

  • President: Nominal head of State
  • Prime Minister and Council of Ministers:
    • Real authority
    • Collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha
  • No-confidence motion: Can remove the government
Who holds power

India versus Britain

  • Differences from the British model:
    • Parliament is not sovereign: bound by a written Constitution and judicial review
    • Head of State is an elected republican, not a hereditary monarch
  • Criticisms:
    • Instability in coalition politics
    • Executive dominance under majority rule
    • Growth of Prime Ministerial Government
  • The framers chose it deliberately for its balance of stability with accountability.

Also in the handout

  • The framers of the Constitution thoughtfully opted for this model to promote unity in a society marked by deep linguistic, regional, and cultural diversity.
  • They claimed it was too Western in inspiration and unsuited to Indian traditions.

4 · 3. Blend of rigidity and flexibility#

  • Neither wholly rigid nor wholly flexible.

Two kinds of provision

  • Rigid:
    • Federal structure, judiciary, fundamental rights
    • Special majority of Parliament plus ratification by half the states (Art. 368)
  • Flexible:
    • Formation of new states, change of names or boundaries
    • Simple majority like ordinary laws

Types of majority (Additional information, redrawn)

Six majorities and where each is used

TypeMeaningUsed for
1 Simple (ordinary or functional)More than 50% of members present and voting; default rule (Art. 100)Ordinary, Money and Financial Bills; motions (confidence, no-confidence, censure, adjournment, thanks); President's Rule (Art. 356); Financial Emergency (Art. 360); Speaker/Deputy Speaker election (Art. 93); Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha (Art. 89); disapproval of National Emergency (Art. 352)

5 · 4. Fundamental Rights#

  • Part III, the cornerstone of democracy, guarantee civil and political liberties and check arbitrary State power. They are justiciable.

The six categories

RightArticlesCore idea
Equality14-18Equality before law, no discrimination, abolition of untouchability and titles
Freedom19-22Speech, association, movement, residence, profession; protection on conviction and personal liberty
Against exploitation23-24Bans trafficking, forced labour, child labour
Freedom of religion25-28Conscience; profess, practise, propagate
Cultural and educational29-30Language, culture, minorities' right to run educational institutions
Constitutional remedies32Move the Supreme Court; Ambedkar: "heart and soul"
  • Memory aid (Six rights): Equality, Freedom, Exploitation, Religion, Cultural-educational, Constitutional remedies: "Every Fine Engine Runs Cool Completely." Originally seven rights. The Right to Property (Art. 31) was removed by the 44th Amendment, 1978 and made a legal right under Art. 300-A (Part XII).

Also in the handout

  • They ensure equality, freedom, and dignity, while safeguarding minority and cultural rights, thus laying the foundation of political democracy.

6 · 5. Directive Principles of State Policy#

  • Part IV: non-justiciable guidelines for a welfare State and socio-economic democracy. Ambedkar: a "novel feature".

Three categories

CategoryExamples in the handout
SocialistRight to work, education, livelihood
GandhianPanchayati Raj, village industries, prohibition of intoxicants
Liberal-intellectualUniform civil code, protection of environment, promotion of international peace
  • They are not enforceable but fundamental in governance, and influenced land reforms, MGNREGA and the Right to Education Act.
  • Memory aid (Three types): Socialist, Gandhian, Liberal-intellectual: "See Gandhi Lead".

Also in the handout

  • Though courts cannot enforce them, DPSPs are considered fundamental in the governance of the country and have significantly influenced progressive policies like land reforms, MGNREGA, and the Right to Education Act, thereby complementing Fundamental Rights in building a just social order.

7 · 6. Fundamental Duties#

  • Inspired by the Constitution of the USSR. They instil civic responsibility and balance rights with obligations.

How the duties came

  • 1976: 42nd Amendment places them in Part IVA, Art. 51A: 10 duties
  • 2002: 86th Amendment adds the 11th: parents or guardians to provide educational opportunities to children aged 6 to 14 (Art. 51A(k))
How the duties came
  • They are non-justiciable: a moral code guiding national unity, integrity and constitutional values.

8 · 7. Federation with a strong centralising tendency#

  • Art. 1 calls India a "Union of States". States cannot secede: the Union is indestructible.

Federal against unitary

  • Federal features:
    • Dual polity
    • Division of powers
    • Written Constitution
    • Independent judiciary
    • Bicameralism at the Centre
  • Tilt to the Centre:
    • Most important subjects with the Union
    • Emergency takeover
    • One Constitution
    • One citizenship
  • K.C. Wheare called it "quasi-federal": structurally tilted to the Centre for unity and stability.

Also in the handout

  • Still, there is a clear tilt towards central power: most important subjects are controlled by the Union government, during emergencies it can take over wide powers, and the whole country shares one Constitution and one citizenship. Owing to this unique balance, constitutional scholar K.C.

9 · 8. Universal adult suffrage#

  • Art. 326: every citizen aged 18+ votes in Parliament and State Legislature elections. Voting age was 21, reduced to 18 by the 61st Amendment (1988). Equal votes regardless of caste, religion, gender, education or economic status. Over 900 million eligible voters make India the world's largest democracy.

10 · 9. Independent judiciary#

  • An integrated judiciary:
    • Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts, subordinate courts
    • One system enforces both Central and State laws. Judicial review under Arts. 13, 32 and 226 makes it the guardian of the Constitution and Fundamental Rights, and it decides Union-State disputes. Independence rests on security of tenure, fixed service conditions and a rigorous impeachment process

11 · 10. Secular State#

  • No State religion
  • Arts. 25-28 guarantee the right to profess, practise and propagate any faith, or none. The 42nd Amendment (1976) inserted "Secular" in the Preamble. Unlike Western absolute separation, India follows Sarva Dharma Sambhava: equal respect for all religions, with State intervention for reform (banning untouchability, gender equality in places of worship) while staying neutral

Also in the handout

  • This framework allows the State to intervene in religious matters to uphold social reform and constitutional values, such as banning untouchability or ensuring gender equality in places of worship, while maintaining neutrality among different faiths.

12 · 11. Single citizenship#

  • Unlike the US (dual citizenship), India has one citizenship. Every Indian enjoys the same political and civil rights (vote, movement, equality before law) irrespective of state or union territory. It prevents parochial loyalties and promotes national integration and fraternity.

Also in the handout

  • This prevents parochial or sectional loyalties, reduces the scope for discrimination on regional lines, and strengthens the feeling of belonging to one nation. Thus, single citizenship serves as a vital instrument for fostering national integration and fraternity.

13 · 12. Separation of powers#

  • Montesquieu's doctrine: legislature makes laws, executive implements, judiciary interprets. In India it is functional and flexible, not rigid.

Where the powers deliberately overlap

OverlapExample in the handout
Executive in legislatureMinisters are drawn from the legislature; accountable through Question Hour, no-confidence motions, committee scrutiny
Judiciary over othersJudicial review of legislative enactments and executive decisions
Legislature in judiciaryRole in impeachment of judges
Executive in judiciaryParticipation in judicial appointments
  • Result: a dynamic system of checks and balances, not watertight compartments.

Also in the handout

  • This framework establishes not watertight compartments but a dynamic system of checks and balances, aimed at preventing concentration of power and preserving democratic accountability in line with the spirit of the Constitution.

14 · Critique: the eight charges and the replies#

  • Critics inside and outside the Assembly called it borrowed, bulky and over-legalistic. The framers defended each choice by India's diversity, history and governance needs.
  • Memory aid (Eight charges): Borrowed, Carbon copy, Un-Indian, Un-Gandhian, Elephantine, Paradise of lawyers, Lacks consistency, Ambedkar's warnings: "Bad Copies Usually Upset Every Proud Lawyer, Ambedkar warned."

Also in the handout

  • It is a very comprehensive, elaborate and detailed document.
  • These rights are justiciable, empowering citizens to seek judicial redress, and remain vital in upholding liberty, equality, and the rule of law.
  • These mechanisms insulate the judiciary from political pressures, enabling it to act as a neutral arbiter and upholder of constitutional supremacy.
  • Yet, critics both within and outside the Assembly argued that it was a borrowed, bulky, and excessively legalistic document, lacking originality or indigenous character. While such criticisms hold some ground, the framers justified their choices in light of India’s diversity, historical context, and governance needs.
  • A Borrowed Constitution A major criticism has been that the Indian Constitution is a “bag of borrowings”, as it drew heavily from other constitutions and global experiences. Critics argued that this made it unoriginal and derivative rather than rooted in India’s traditions. Constitutional experts like Sir Ivor Jennings described it as a “patchwork” and even went as far as to call it a “lawyer’s paradise.” Similarly, many members of the Constituent Assembly themselves worried that it lacked novelty.
  • A Carbon Copy of the 1935 Act Another frequent criticism is that the Constitution is simply a continuation of the Government of India Act, 1935. Large portions dealing with administration, federal structure, and bureaucracy were retained, giving the impression of colonial continuity.
  • Ambedkar, however, justified this borrowing. He argued that since India was already functioning under the 1935 Act, and the machinery was familiar, it was practical to retain its administrative provisions.
  • Gandhi envisioned a polity rooted in villages, decentralisation, and self-reliance, but the Constitution gave primacy to a centralised parliamentary democracy.
  • Elephantine Size The Indian Constitution is often criticised for being excessively bulky.
  • Detailed drafting reduced ambiguities, prevented litigation, and ensured clarity in governance.
  • Paradise of the Lawyers Because of the dominance of legal experts in the Constituent Assembly, the Constitution is sometimes described as overly legalistic and complex, filled with technical jargon.
  • Maheswari worried it would make Indians “more litigious” and less inclined towards truth and non-violence.
  • Complex provisions were necessary to safeguard rights, balance federalism, and ensure clarity in judicial interpretation.
  • Lacks Theoretical Consistency The Constitution has also been criticised for lacking coherence.
  • It reflected India’s plural society and allowed space for consensus. By leaving some questions open-ended, the framers ensured the Constitution could evolve with changing times.
  • Ambedkar himself expressed concerns about the Constitution’s future.
  • His warnings remain relevant in assessing whether India has lived up to the constitutional spirit.

From Lakshmikanth · Indian Polity, 8th ed. Ch 4, Salient Features of the Constitution
  • The 42nd Amendment (1976) is called the 'Mini-Constitution'
  • The 7th, 42nd, 44th, 73rd, 74th, 97th and 101st Amendments changed many original features
  • No other Constitution has so many Articles and Schedules. The American Constitution originally had 7 Articles, Australian 128, Chinese 138 and Canadian 147.
  • Part VII was deleted by the 7th Amendment (1956). Parts IVA and XIVA came by the 42nd, IXA by the 74th and IXB by the 97th
  • Part IX by the 73rd
  • Art. 370 special status of Jammu and Kashmir ended in 2019
  • The Reorganisation Act 2019 made two Union territories, Jammu and Kashmir, and Ladakh
  • Ambedkar said the Constitution was framed after 'ransacking all the known Constitutions'. Its structural part is from the 1935 Act, the philosophical part (FR, DPSP) from the US and Ireland, the political part from Britain.
  • Art. 1 'Union of States' means the federation is not the result of an agreement among States and no State can secede. Other labels: Morris Jones 'bargaining federalism', Granville Austin 'co-operative federalism', Jennings 'centralising tendency'.
  • Indian judicial review is narrower than the US because Art. 21 says 'procedure established by law', not 'due process'. So the framers blended parliamentary sovereignty with judicial supremacy.
  • Judges' independence: expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund, no discussion of their conduct in legislatures, ban on practice after retirement, and power to punish contempt.
  • Fundamental Rights can be suspended in a National Emergency except Arts. 20 and 21. Emergency types: Art. 352 (war, external aggression or armed rebellion), Art. 356 and 365, and Art. 360.
  • Other features LK adds: independent bodies (Election Commission, CAG, UPSC, State PSCs), three-tier government (73rd and 74th, 1992), and co-operative societies (97th, 2011: Art. 19 right, Art. 43B, Part IXB).

"We wanted the music of Veena or Sitar, but here we have the music of an English band."
What was Ambedkar's reply to the "carbon copy" charge?
India already ran under the 1935 Act, so its machinery was practical to retain; democracy's soul, adult franchise and fundamental rights were new.
Ambedkar's warning about democracy?
Political democracy cannot last without social and economic democracy; an "era of contradictions"; avoid hero-worship.

Traps#

Trap 1 · Article count mismatch The handout says 470 Articles under Feature 1 but 'now over 450' in the Critique. Rule: quote 470 and 25 Parts and 12 Schedules; note the mismatch.
Trap 2 · Majority look-alikes Art. 352 appears twice (disapproval is Simple, approval is Special I). The Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha is Art. 89 (Simple, election) and Art. 90 (Effective, removal). Impeachment of the President (Art. 61) is Special Type II. Rule: Simple = present and voting; Effective = minus vacancies; Special II = two-thirds of all, only for the President.
Trap 3 · Amendment and date pairs Fundamental Duties came in 1976 (42nd, 10 duties, Part IVA) and the 11th in 2002 (86th, ages 6 to 14). Right to Property left Part III in 1978 (44th) for Art. 300-A. Voting age fell to 18 by the 61st (1988). Rule: pair each change with its Amendment number and year.
Trap 4 · Federal and separation labels The handout calls India 'quasi-federal' (Wheare), a Union of States whose States cannot secede; separation of powers is functional, not rigid. Single citizenship is the contrast with the US. Rule: federal with a strong Centre, never purely federal or purely unitary.
