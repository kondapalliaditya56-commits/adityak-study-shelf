The INC was not a British invention: decades of regional campaigns and all-India attempts came first, and Hume was the bridge and the umbrella, not the architect.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Decades of regional campaigns and attempts at all-India bodies prepared the ground; Hume, a Liberal retired civil servant, bridged Bombay, Calcutta and Madras and gave the INC protective cover, so the British 'Safety Valve' story is a friend's legend while the 'Lightning Conductor' reading is the sound one.

  1. Small bodies, big complaints. Long before 1885 regional associations protested the Lex Loci Act 1850 and the Income Tax of 1868, campaigned for Indianisation of the Civil Services (1877), against the Afghan War and the Vernacular Press Act (1878), for the Ilbert Bill (1883-84), and demanded the right to join the Volunteer Corps. None of their demands was met.
    → So the next logical step was a body that spoke for all of India.
  2. The first attempts. The British India Association (1851) tried branches in Bombay and Madras for a joint petition on the Charter renewal of 1853; journalists at the Imperial Darbar formed the Native Press Association (1877) with Banerjea as President; the Indian Association (1876) launched the Indian National Conference (1883); Madras hosted the Theosophical Society convention (1884); a National Fund followed in 1885.
    → But the regional bodies competed for leadership, and someone had to bring them to one table.
  3. The retired civil servant. A.O. Hume, a Liberal retired British civil servant, had worked under Mayo 69-72 (1871), was dismissed for opposing Lytton 76-80's Afghan War spending, and backed Ripon 80-84's reforms. He wanted the INC to be 'His Majesty's Opposition': a strong opposition party so that the government is accountable, with good governance and a responsive government.
    → And so in December 1885 the delegates met.
  4. Bombay, December 1885. The INC was founded in Bombay in December 1885 with W.C. Bonnerjee as 1st President. The teacher calls it the 1st all India ORG but not an all Indian one, since its founder was a Briton; and the President is not from the region of the session. Hume served as General Secretary, never as President.
    → The sessions then moved city to city, and the Presidents became the exam's favourite list.
  5. Presidents and Firsts. Naoroji 1886 (Calcutta), Tyabji 1887 (1st Muslim, Madras), Yule 1888 (1st non-Indian, Allahabad), Wedderburn 1889 and 1910, Webb 1894, Besant 1917 (1st woman), Das 1922 (from prison, Gaya), Naidu 1925 (2nd woman). Calcutta hosted the 2nd session, not the 1st; Tyabji died in August 1906, before the League of December 1906.
    → But why did this British friend help at all? One story says the British planned it.
  6. The Safety Valve story. Wedderburn's 1913 biography says Hume received seven volumes of secret reports in 1878 describing a conspiracy of the masses, met Dufferin 84-88, and together they founded the INC as a safety valve; R.P. Dutt agreed. The teacher draws a pressure cooker: Indians as steam, oppression as flame, the INC as the whistle.
    → But the story does not survive a check.
  7. Why the story fails. The seven volumes were never found in any archive; the only source is a friend's book written nearly four decades later. Dufferin 84-88 called the Moderates a 'microscopic minority of Babus working for self-interest', and quietly told the Governor of Bombay to be alert, fearing an Irish Home Rule League. The teacher: never in exam, always on YouTube.
    → So what was Hume really good for?
  8. The bridge and the umbrella. Wedderburn wrote to make his friend Hume a patriot. Hume's real usefulness was practical: a mediator who got Bombay, Calcutta and Madras to sit together, and, as Gokhale put it in 1913, a British founder gave protective cover because the British would never allow an all-India body under an Indian leader. If the British used the INC as a safety valve, Indians used Hume as a lightning conductor.
    → Hume had friends, and the handout names them.
  9. The English friends. Wedderburn (co-founder, 2nd non-Indian President 1889 and 1910, MP, with W.S. Caine set up the Indian Parliamentary Committee in 1893 for reforms in the Commons); Jardine (Judge, MP 1906-1918); Yule (1st non-Indian President, Andrew Yule and Company, an Agency House, 400 tigers); Webb (Irish MP, Madras 1894, invited by Naoroji, against racism and the opium trade).
    → One last check of how the exam turned the pieces into questions.
  10. What UPSC asked. Curzon 99-05 made the 'peaceful demise' remark (1998); the 1998 pairs trap is Tyabji and the Muslim League; 2000 asked Naidu, Das, Hume, Webb; 2004 and 2008 asked the first session (Bombay); 2011 the Wedderburn and Caine committee; 2015 the first woman and first Muslim Presidents. The teacher writes Mains - No Q, PRELIMS ticked.

In simple words#

  • The INC was founded in Bombay in December 1885 with W.C. Bonnerjee as 1st President
  • A.O. Hume, a retired British civil servant and Liberal, was its Founder and General Secretary, never President
  • The Safety Valve Theory (from Wedderburn's 1913 biography of Hume) says the INC was a British device to prevent a revolt, but the seven volumes were never found, and Dufferin 84-88 mocked the Moderates and watched the INC.
  • Before 1885 regional bodies already ran seven campaigns (Lex Loci 1850 to the 1885 appeal to British voters) and tried all-India bodies (BIA 1851, Native Press Association 1877, Conference 1883, National Fund 1885).
  • So Hume's real use was mediator between Bombay, Calcutta and Madras and a protective cover (Gokhale 1913): Indians used Hume as a lightning conductor.
  • The English friends are Hume, Wedderburn (2nd non-Indian President, Indian Parliamentary Committee 1893), Jardine, Yule (1st non-Indian President, Agency House) and Webb (Irish, Madras 1894).
  • The exam asks Presidents and Firsts, the first session, Curzon 99-05's remark and the 1893 committee
  • The teacher says the Safety Valve is never in exam

3 · The INC as a British "Safety Valve": the debate#

Story link

Because Hume was British, a story says the British planned it. Ask: what are the three flaws (Babus, Archive, Home Rule)?

3.1 What does the Safety Valve Theory say?

  • The theory says Hume founded the INC not out of genuine sympathy for Indians but as a deliberate British strategy: a platform where the educated Indian elite could air grievances peacefully, so preventing a violent mass uprising against British rule.
  • Origin: a biography of Hume written by William Wedderburn in 1913.
  • Per Wedderburn, in 1878 Hume came across seven volumes of secret reports compiled by religious gurus.
  • They allegedly described a conspiracy by the lower classes to overthrow British rule.
  • Alarmed, Hume met Viceroy Dufferin 84-88 (Lord Dufferin, Viceroy 1884-1888).
  • Together they brought educated Indians together to form the INC as a "safety valve" to vent grievances and prevent revolt.
  • R.P. Dutt, the noted (communist) historian, also supported this reading.
Teacher's board
  • "Think:
    • Whistle, lid, cooker." Indians = the steam inside a pressure cooker
    • the burner is British oppression (labels "By Rule" and "OPPRESSION")
    • The whistle (safety valve) lets the steam out so the lid does not blow
  • That is the theory's logic: the INC = the whistle on the British cooker.
  • Exam verdict: "never in exam, always on YouTube."
Handout alert
  • A date puzzle in the handout. The handout says Hume found the seven volumes in 1878 and then met Dufferin 84-88, who became Viceroy only in 1884.
  • The meeting therefore came years after the reports
  • The PYQ answer places the meeting in 1885.
  • Use: reports 1878, meeting with Dufferin 84-88 (1884-88 term), INC December 1885.

3.2 Why is the Safety Valve Theory wrong?

  • The theory does not hold up, for three reasons:
  • (i) The seven volumes were never found in any archive.
  • There is no reference anywhere except in Wedderburn's biography, written by a close personal friend of Hume, nearly four decades after the events.
  • (ii) Dufferin 84-88 did not take Hume seriously.
  • Far from being a co-conspirator, he hated the Moderates and did not see them as leaders of the Indian masses.
  • He called them a "microscopic minority of Babus working for self-interest," hardly the language of a man who thought they could prevent a mass revolt.
  • (iii) Dufferin 84-88 did not welcome the INC.
  • He quietly asked the Governor of Bombay to be alert about it.
  • He feared it would grow into something like the Irish Home Rule League.
  • (The Irish Home Rule League, led by figures like Charles Parnell in the 1870s-80s, demanded Irish self-governance within the British Empire.
  • It became a powerful political force that consistently embarrassed and pressured the British government.
  • Dufferin 84-88's comparison was a warning, not a compliment.)
  • So the man the theory names as co-designer actually viewed the INC with suspicion.
  • The entire theory rests on a single unverifiable source, written by a friend, about events he did not witness.

4.3 Then why did Wedderburn write what he wrote?

  • Wedderburn was Hume's close friend. He wanted to portray Hume, a Briton working for Indian political rights, as a patriot: a man who sacrificed his career to save the British Empire from its own folly.
  • The Safety Valve narrative made Hume look prescient and heroic. But it was a story written for a friend's legacy, not as history.

4.4 So what was Hume's real usefulness?

  • His actual contribution was more practical than mythological.
  • Mediator: he helped bridge differences among the regional organisations that were competing for leadership of a future national body.
  • Getting Bombay, Calcutta and Madras to sit together was not simple
  • Hume helped make it happen.
  • Protective cover: Gokhale put it plainly in 1913: the British would never have allowed an all-India organisation to be formed under the leadership of an Indian.
  • A British founder gave the INC protective cover in its early years.
  • The two readings: if the British thought they were using the INC as a safety valve, Indians were using Hume as a lightning conductor, to channel the energy of political organisation past the British reflex to shut it down.

Story link

5.1 A.O. Hume: the founder

  • Hume's motivations were reformist, even if the Safety Valve story distorts them.
  • As Secretary of the Department of Revenue, Agriculture and Commerce in 1871, he worked under Viceroy Mayo 69-72 (Lord Mayo, 1869-1872) to improve agricultural yields.
  • He opposed Viceroy Lytton 76-80's heavy expenditure on the Afghan War (1878-80) and was dismissed from his post for it.
  • He supported Ripon 80-84's reforms, especially the Local Self-Government Resolution of 1882.
Mnemonic · Hume's career with three Viceroys, in order
HiredFiredAdmired
HMayo 69-72 hired him: Secretary, Revenue, Agriculture and Commerce, 1871FLytton 76-80 fired him: he opposed the Afghan War (1878-80) spendingARipon 80-84 admired: Hume backed his reforms, the Local Self-Government Resolution 1882
Order kept: Mayo 69-72, Lytton 76-80, Ripon 80-84.
  • He was a Liberal in the British political tradition, someone who believed in representative governance.
  • He wanted the INC to function like "His Majesty's Opposition": a formal, legitimate channel of political dissent, the way an opposition party functions in a democracy.
  • In 1885 he also published a direct appeal to British voters to elect pro-Indian candidates to Parliament.
  • He served as the General Secretary of the INC (the handout figure: till 1892): not its President, but arguably its most active organiser in the early years.
Teacher's board
  • Hume = LIBERAL, Retd ICS. Liberal explains why he backed Ripon 80-84 and wanted an opposition party
  • Retd ICS explains why a retired civil servant could get the leaders of the three Presidencies together.
  • The 1885 appeal to British voters was led by Dadabhai Naoroji and linked to the National Fund (Section 4.2).

5.2 William Wedderburn: the co-founder who became a biographer

  • William Wedderburn, a retired civil servant who had been ex-Secretary to the Bombay Government, was a co-founder of the INC in 1885.
  • He became the 2nd non-Indian President of the INC, serving in 1889 and again in 1910.
  • Like Hume, he supported Ripon 80-84's reforms.
  • He favoured Cooperative Banks to provide cheap loans to farmers.
  • His pro-Indian positions cost him: he was denied a judgeship at the Bombay High Court.
  • (High Courts were established under the Indian High Courts Act of 1861 and began functioning from 1862.)
  • Later, as a Member of Parliament in Britain, he supported INC demands through the 1890s, in close partnership with Gopal Krishna Gokhale.
  • With W.S. Caine he set up the Indian Parliamentary Committee in 1893 (Prelims 2011).
Handout alert
  • Prelims 2011: answer (a) To agitate for Indian political reforms in the House of Commons.
  • The Indian Parliamentary Committee (Wedderburn and Caine, 1893) was a pressure group of sympathetic British MPs that raised and pressed Indian political reforms in the Commons.
  • (b) the judiciary was never its brief (Wedderburn's own record cost him a Bombay High Court judgeship)
  • (c) in 1893 the language was reform within British rule, not independence
  • (d) it was a group of sympathetic MPs, not a vehicle for sending Indians into Parliament
  • Wrong options commonly picked: (b), (c), (d).
Teacher's board
  • Exam keywords: William Wedderburn, WS Caine, Indian Parliamentary Committee in 1893, and in the answer Indian political reforms, House of Commons.
  • Wedderburn = "Retd ICS", "Hume's follower" (retired, same Liberal line, backed Ripon 80-84).

5.3 Justice John Jardine: the Liberal lawyer

  • John Jardine was also a co-founder of the INC in 1885.
  • He had served as Chief Secretary of Bombay and as a Judge of the Bombay High Court.
  • He later became an MP (1906-1918) and was a Liberal.

5.4 George Yule: the merchant President

  • George Yule was the 1st non-Indian President of the INC, presiding over the Allahabad Session in 1888.
Mnemonic · The three non-Indian Presidents, in order

Order kept: 1888, 1889, 1894. The first Britisher was Yule, not Hume (Prelims 2000).
  • He was a merchant and businessman: founder of the Agency House "Andrew Yule and Company" in Calcutta.
  • He also served as President of the Indian Chamber of Commerce.
  • The fact that a man of his economic standing was willing to preside over the INC in 1888 was itself politically significant.
  • He also had the distinction of having killed 400 tigers, which says something about the social world of such colonial figures.

5.5 Alfred Webb: the Irish friend

  • Alfred Webb was an Irish politician who served as an MP in the 1890s and was the 3rd non-Indian President of the INC, presiding over the Madras Session of 1894.
  • He was invited personally by Dadabhai Naoroji, with whom he shared a close friendship.
  • He was strongly opposed to racism (he supported Britain's first anti-racist journal) and opposed the opium trade into China.
  • His Irish background (Ireland had been under British control for centuries and was still fighting for Home Rule while Webb presided in 1894) likely shaped his sympathy for people under imperial rule.
