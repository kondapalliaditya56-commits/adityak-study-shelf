2 · West Asia: nature and evolution#

Class + handout

“West Asia is part of our extended neighbourhood.” (S. Jaishankar, quoted.) West Asia is India’s energy, diaspora, connectivity and maritime-security corridor: it supplies oil, LNG and fertilisers, hosts millions of Indians and controls the chokepoints of Hormuz, Bab-el-Mandeb, the Red Sea and the Suez route.

3 · West Asia: three axes and the major powers#

Class + handout

The region is easiest to read through three axes: Israel (and the Palestine question), Iran, and the Gulf states, plus the competing interests of the US, China and Russia.

The three axes (slides 5 and 7, handout p. 79)
AxisBackground and present character
Israel and the Palestine questionAfter the First World War Palestine came under the British Mandate; the Balfour Declaration (1917) supported a Jewish national home. The UN proposed separate Arab and Jewish states in 1947; Israel declared independence in 1948, followed by the first Arab-Israeli war. The 1967 war brought the West Bank, East Jerusalem and Gaza under Israeli occupation. Unresolved: statehood, Jerusalem, settlements, refugees, Israeli security. The widely supported framework is a two-State solution. Israel has a close strategic relationship with the US. The Abraham Accords (2020) normalised ties with the UAE and Bahrain but did not resolve Palestine. The latest conflict began with the Hamas-led attack of 7 October 2023; despite a 2025 ceasefire, violence and the humanitarian crisis continued into 2026
IranA US partner under the Shah; the 1979 Islamic Revolution transformed its relations with the US and Israel. A major Shia power with influence through partners and armed groups in Iraq, Lebanon, Syria and Yemen. Its nuclear programme brought sanctions. The JCPOA (2015) limited it; the US withdrew in 2018. In 2026, direct conflict involving the US, Israel and Iran became the region’s most serious crisis and disrupted energy through Hormuz
Gulf statesThe GCC: Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Oman, Bahrain. Mostly Sunni-ruled monarchies with different foreign policies; major oil and gas; historically dependent on the US for security. With Iran: rivalry and selective engagement (Saudi-Iran competition; Oman and Qatar keep broader channels). On Israel: the UAE and Bahrain normalised in 2020; Saudi Arabia has not. Now diversifying towards India, China, Europe and others
What the major powers want (slide 8, handout pp. 79-80)
PowerInterests in West Asia
United States and EuropeThe most important external security actor: Israel’s security, Gulf bases and partnerships, preventing an Iranian nuclear weapon, energy and sea routes, supported the Abraham Accords; the 2026 conflict reinforced its military role. Several European states take stronger positions for Palestinian statehood and against settlements
ChinaEconomics, energy and diplomacy: oil and gas, trade and infrastructure with both Iran and the Gulf, BRI sea routes; relations with all camps; brokered the Saudi-Iran rapprochement (2023); supports a two-State solution while trading with Israel
RussiaStrategic influence, energy and security: close ties with Iran; military intervention in Syria from 2015; relations with Israel and the Gulf; OPEC+ with Gulf producers; supports Palestinian statehood
IndiaEnergy, diaspora, connectivity, defence, trade and maritime stability

Slide 8: “India’s approach differs: it seeks relations across competing camps while preserving strategic autonomy and freedom of navigation.”

Live in the news: the Gulf hedges

In the news · Aug 2026 CA

Saudi Arabia, Türkiye and Pakistan sign a mutual-defence pact

Why it matters
The Mecca Joint Defence Agreement treats an attack on one as an attack on all three. Trigger: the US-Israel war on Iran raised Gulf doubts about the US security umbrella. Washington welcomed it as burden-sharing. But there is no integrated command, no published text and no common enemy (Saudi Arabia looks at Iran, Türkiye at Israel, Pakistan at India).

A live example of the handout’s line that Gulf states are “diversifying both their economies and foreign relations”, and of the US-as-security-actor row in the major-powers table. For India it touches the Pakistan factor in West Asia.

7 · India-ASEAN: nature, evolution and convergence#

Class + handout

“ASEAN centrality is at the heart of India’s Act East Policy; this is the Asian century, and India and ASEAN will shape it together.” (PM Narendra Modi, 21st ASEAN-India Summit, Vientiane, 2024.) ASEAN is a regional organisation of 11 Southeast Asian countries. It is the anchor of Act East and a pillar of India’s Indo-Pacific strategy. India wants a free, open, inclusive Indo-Pacific built around ASEAN centrality, without being drawn into US-China rivalry or the South China Sea disputes.

After the Cold War, the 1991 liberalisation led India to look east towards Southeast Asia. In 2014 the Look East Policy became the Act East Policy.

Three phases
  • 1991-2010, economic: India became ASEAN’s Sectoral Partner (1992) and Dialogue Partner (1995); the first summit followed in 2002 and the goods agreement (AITIGA) in 2010.
  • 2014-19, strategic: Act East added security and maritime cooperation; the Indo-Pacific Oceans Initiative (2019) placed ASEAN centrality at its core.
  • 2022-26, comprehensive: a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership (2022), a Year of Maritime Cooperation (2026) and BrahMos for Indonesia.
Evaluation

Trade has reached US$128.38 billion, about 10% of India’s trade, and UPI-PayNow links people. But the deficit is about US$51.55 billion, India left RCEP, China deepened its ASEAN FTA, and Kaladan is delayed. Strategically the policy has matured; economically it is under-realised.

A balanced AITIGA review, joint value chains and finished corridors can move India from strategic convergence to real economic integration.
