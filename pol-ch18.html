In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • The parliamentary system (also cabinet, responsible or Westminster model) makes the executive answer to the legislature. India has it at the Centre (Arts. 74, 75) and in the states (Arts. 163, 164). Britain, India, Japan and Canada follow it
  • The US, Brazil, Russia and Sri Lanka follow the presidential model
  • Eight features: nominal (President) and real (PM) executive, majority-party rule, collective responsibility (Art. 75(3)), political cohesion, double membership (a non-member minister gets 6 months, Art. 75(5)), PM's leadership, dissolution of the lower house (Art. 85) and secrecy.
  • In the US one President is head of State and head of Government, elected by an electoral college for a fixed 4 years, removable only by impeachment, cannot dissolve the House, and works on separation of powers.
  • Merits: harmony, responsible government, check on despotism, ready alternative government, wide representation. Demerits: instability, no policy continuity, cabinet dominance, fusion of powers, non-experts, coalition and regionalism.
  • Why India chose it: familiarity (Acts of 1919 and 1935), Ambedkar's preference for responsibility over stability, avoiding deadlocks, diversity, flexibility, and the Swaran Singh Committee (1975) backing.
  • Unlike Britain: republic, constitutional supremacy, PM from either House, outsider minister for 6 months, no countersignature, no shadow cabinet, and a federal and plural setting.

1 · What the parliamentary system is#

  • The Constitution sets up a parliamentary government at the Union level (Arts. 74, 75) and the State level (Arts. 163, 164). Democracies are classed by the executive-legislature link.

Parliamentary against presidential

  • Parliamentary:
    • Executive is politically accountable to the legislature for decisions and actions
    • Also called cabinet government, responsible government, Westminster model
    • Origin in the United Kingdom
    • Followers: Britain, India, Japan, Canada
  • Presidential:
    • Executive works independently of the legislature, holds a fixed term, is not answerable to it
    • Also called fixed executive or non-responsible government
    • Followers: United States, Brazil, Russia, Sri Lanka
  • Ivor Jennings called it the cabinet system: the cabinet is the core of executive authority.
  • Real executive power lies with the council of ministers, which jointly makes policy and runs the administration.
  • The cabinet stays in office only while it has the confidence of the legislature.

The Prime Minister's position

  • Traditionally in Britain the PM was primus inter pares (first among equals).
  • Over time the post became centralised and dominant: Crossman and Mackintosh called it prime ministerial government.
  • In India a similar trend is seen: the PM coordinates policy, guides the cabinet, and represents the government in Parliament and abroad.
  • Memory aid (Names of the system): Cabinet government, Responsible government, Westminster model: "Cabinets Run Westminster".

Also in the handout

  • Modern democracies are broadly classified into parliamentary and presidential systems, depending on the relationship between the executive and the legislature.
  • However, over time, the Prime Minister’s position has evolved into a centralised and dominant role, exerting substantial influence over cabinet decisions and the administrative machinery.
  • The most prominent example of this system is the United States of America, whose Constitution (1787) laid down the model, which has influenced several other nations.

2 · Eight features in India#

  • The system fuses the executive and the legislature, decides collectively, and answers to the legislature.

The eight features

#FeatureContent
1Nominal and real executivePresident = nominal (de jure), ceremonial head of state. PM = real (de facto), heads the government. Art. 74: Council of Ministers led by the PM aids and advises the President, who is bound to act on this advice
2Rule of the majority partyGovernment is formed by the party or coalition with a majority in the Lok Sabha. President appoints the majority leader as PM; other ministers are appointed on the PM's advice. If no party has a majority, the President may invite a coalition
3Collective responsibilityArt. 75(3): the council of ministers headed by the PM is collectively accountable to the Lok Sabha. All ministers act as one unit; a vote of no-confidence makes the whole council resign
4Political cohesionMinisters usually belong to one party and share an ideology. In coalitions they are bound by mutual consensus
5Double membershipMinisters sit in both executive and legislature. A non-member can be a minister only if he becomes an MP within six months (Art. 75(5))
6Leadership of the PMPM is leader of the council of ministers, leader of the ruling party and leader of the legislature; coordinates policy, directs administration, represents the Union nationally and internationally
7Dissolution of the lower houseThe President can dissolve the Lok Sabha on the PM's advice (Art. 85), to seek fresh elections
8Principle of secrecyMinisters keep cabinet deliberations and decisions confidential; they take an oath of secrecy administered by the President before assuming office
  • Memory aid (Eight features): Nominal-real, Majority rule, Collective responsibility, Cohesion, Double membership, Leadership of PM, Dissolution, Secrecy: "No Minister Can Commit Double Life During Session".
  • Shortcut: Article shortcut. 74 = aid and advise (nominal vs real). 75(3) = collective responsibility. 75(5) = six months. 85 = dissolve Lok Sabha. 163-164 = the same system in the States.

How a government is formed and falls

  • Election: Party or coalition wins a majority in the Lok Sabha
  • President appoints:
    • The majority leader as PM
    • Other ministers on the PM's advice
  • Council aids and advises: Art. 74: the President is bound by this advice
  • Responsible to Lok Sabha: Art. 75(3): collective responsibility
  • No-confidence vote: Entire council of ministers resigns

Before assuming office, ministers take an oath of secrecy administered by the President, ensuring that sensitive government information remains protected and that policy formulation is conducted in a secure environment.

3 · The presidential system (USA)#

  • Here the executive and legislature work independently. The model comes from the US Constitution (1787) and is called non-responsible government: the executive is not answerable to the legislature for day-to-day administration.

Structure

  • Unified executive role: the President is both Head of State (ceremonial) and Head of Government (leads the executive, policy and implementation).
  • Fixed tenure:
    • Elected for a fixed term (four years in the US) by an electoral college
    • The legislature cannot remove him in this period except by impeachment for serious unconstitutional acts
  • Independent executive council:
    • Advisers such as a cabinet or the informal Kitchen Cabinet
    • Appointed solely by the President, responsible only to him, removable at his discretion, and need not be legislators

Key features

Six features of the presidential system

#FeatureContent
11Separation of powersLegislative, executive and judicial powers sit in independent organs; no branch dominates
12Non-responsibility to the legislaturePresident and cabinet are not accountable to Congress, do not attend its sessions and are not its members; independence, but less oversight of day-to-day administration
13Non-dissolutionThe President cannot dissolve the House of Representatives; the legislature keeps autonomy and its members a fixed term

4 · Advantages#

  • These merits come from joining the executive to the legislature, from collective responsibility and from representative governance (Arts. 74, 75, 163, 164).

Five merits

MeritContent
Harmony between legislature and executiveMinisters are MPs and draw legitimacy from Parliament, so fewer institutional conflicts; smoother policymaking, quicker laws, efficient governance, shared political mandate
Responsible governmentMinisters collectively answerable to the Lok Sabha (Art. 75) for acts of omission and commission; tools: question hour, debates, adjournment motions, no-confidence motion; transparency and no misuse of authority
Check on arbitrary authorityExecutive power is shared among ministers, so it is not concentrated in one person; a no-confidence motion is a continuous check against despotism
Ready alternative governmentIf the ruling party loses its majority, the President, acting under Art. 75, can invite another party or coalition with a majority; no fresh election needed. Jennings: the leader of the opposition is an alternative PM
Broad representationPM picks ministers from elected members, so leaders of different states, social groups and factions can be included: regional, gender and minority representation
  • Memory aid (Merits):
    • Harmony, Responsible, Check on arbitrary power, Alternative government, Broad representation: "How Really Can All Be heard?" In short: efficiency, accountability and inclusiveness
    • Smooth transfer of power
    • Suited to a diverse, plural society

5 · Limitations#

LimitationContent
Political instabilityMinisters hold office only while they have Lok Sabha confidence (Art. 75); no-confidence motions, defections or coalition disagreements can end a government. Short-lived governments: Morarji Desai, Charan Singh, V.P. Singh, Chandra Sekhar, Deva Gowda, I.K. Gujral
Lack of policy continuityUncertain tenure causes reversals. Example: the Janata Government (1977-79) under Morarji Desai reversed many policies of the earlier Congress government; Congress under Indira Gandhi brought back its own measures after returning in 1980
Concentration of power in the cabinetWith a strong majority, the cabinet and especially the PM can sideline scrutiny. Laski and Ramsay Muir warned of the dictatorship of the cabinet; seen in the eras of Indira Gandhi and Rajiv Gandhi
Fusion of powersPM and council lead the executive while answerable to the legislature. Bagehot: the cabinet is a hyphen that joins the buckle that binds the executive and legislative departments. Goes against strict separation of powers; legislature may be subordinate to the executive
Governance by non-expertsPM selects ministers from among Members of Parliament (Art. 75), limiting technocrats; ministers spend time on Parliament, party and cabinet meetings rather than technical administration
Coalition politics and regionalismPolicy compromises, inflated cabinets, decision delays; regional parties may put short-term regional gains over national priorities
  • The handout says the cure lies in mechanisms like anti-defection law, coalition consensus-building and institutional reforms.

6 · Why India chose it#

  • The Constituent Assembly debated parliamentary versus US-style presidential government and chose the parliamentary form at the Centre and in the States (Arts. 74-75; 163-164).

Six reasons

#ReasonContent
1Historical familiarityExperience under British rule, through the Government of India Acts (1919 and 1935) which brought responsible government at the provincial level. K.M. Munshi: India had decades of parliamentary responsibility; a novel US model could unsettle a new nation
2Responsible governmentAmbedkar: a democratic executive needs both stability and accountability, but no system gives both equally. US: stability (fixed tenure), less accountability. British model: more responsibility (Art. 75; no-confidence, Art. 75(3)). The Assembly preferred responsibility over rigid stability
3No legislative-executive deadlockUS Congress and President often clash, causing paralysis. India in 1947, new and diverse, could not risk deadlock; the executive drawn from the legislature gives cooperation
4Diverse societyMany languages, religions, castes, regions; ministerial representation of groups, coalition-building, national integration; regional parties get ministerial posts
5FlexibilityIf a government loses its majority, the President can invite the opposition or another coalition without dissolving the Lok Sabha
6Post-independence endorsementSwaran Singh Committee (1975), appointed by the Congress government, found the system working effectively and saw no need to replace it with a presidential model
  • Memory aid (Six reasons): History, Responsibility, Deadlock avoided, Diversity, Flexibility, Endorsement: "He Really Debated Deeply, Finally Endorsed".
  • Shortcut: Ambedkar's trade-off. Stability (presidential) versus responsibility (parliamentary): the framers picked responsibility.

Also in the handout

  • Harmonious Relationship Between Legislature and Executive One of the foremost benefits of the parliamentary system is that it fosters coordination and cooperation between the legislature and the executive.
  • ## Rationale Behind Adopting the Parliamentary System in India During the framing of the Indian Constitution, the choice between a parliamentary and a presidential system was a subject of extensive debate in the Constituent Assembly.
  • Introducing a completely novel system like the US presidential model could have created administrative and political unfamiliarity, potentially destabilizing the newly independent nation.
  • Prevention of Legislative–Executive Deadlocks The framers sought to minimise potential conflicts between the legislature and the executive, which are common in presidential systems.
  • India, being a newly independent and diverse nation in 1947, could not risk prolonged deadlocks that might obstruct policy-making or social development.
  • By allowing coalition-building and inclusive ministerial representation, this system fosters national integration and mitigates regional or communal alienation. For example, regional parties often secure ministerial positions in coalition governments, ensuring local interests are represented at the Centre.
  • It concluded that the system had been functioning effectively and there was no pressing need to replace it with a presidential model, reaffirming the validity of the original choice. This committee’s findings highlighted that, despite challenges like coalition politics, the parliamentary system remained the most suitable for India’s democratic and socio-cultural landscape.
  • By prioritizing responsibility and inclusivity over rigid stability, the framers created a flexible system capable of accommodating India’s complex political and social realities.

7 · India against Britain#

  • India draws on the Westminster model but is not a carbon copy.

Seven differences

#PointBritainIndia
1Head of StateHereditary, ceremonial monarchElected President (Art. 52) by an electoral college of MPs and State legislators: a republic
2SupremacyParliamentary sovereignty: Parliament is the supreme law-making bodyWritten Constitution supreme (Art. 13); Parliament limited by judicial review, Fundamental Rights (Part III) and federal division of powers (Seventh Schedule)
3PM's HousePM must be a member of the House of CommonsPM may be from either Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha (Arts. 75(5) and 164(1))
4MinistersOnly elected MPs can be ministersA non-member can be appointed if he becomes an MP within six months (Art. 75(5)); allows expert talent (finance, law, science and technology)
5Legal responsibilityMinisters countersign the monarch's official actsNo countersigning; the President acts on the binding advice (Art. 74(1)); responsibility lies collectively with the council
6Shadow cabinetThe opposition forms a shadow cabinetNo formal shadow cabinet; Leader of the Opposition (Arts. 100, 113 as cited) in both Houses has a partial role; committees like Public Accounts and Estimates oversee the government
7Federal and plural societyUnitary, largely centralisedUnion and States share an executive framework (Arts. 163-164); ministries reflect regional, linguistic and social diversity

Which Articles set up the parliamentary system at Union and State levels?
Arts. 74-75 (Union); Arts. 163-164 (States).
Another names for the parliamentary system?
Cabinet government, responsible government, Westminster model.
Who called it the cabinet system?
Ivor Jennings.
Who called the PM's role prime ministerial government?
Crossman and Mackintosh.
Which Article makes the council of ministers collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha?
Art. 75(3).
Which Article gives six months for a non-MP minister to become an MP?
Art. 75(5).
Which Article lets the President dissolve the Lok Sabha on the PM's advice?
Art. 85.
Who is the nominal and who the real executive in India?
President is nominal (de jure); PM is real (de facto).
What are the eight features?
Nominal-real executive; majority rule; collective responsibility; political cohesion; double membership; PM's leadership; dissolution of lower house; secrecy.
What is the Kitchen Cabinet?
An informal advisory body of the US President; appointed by him, responsible only to him, removable at will, not legislators.
What is the US presidential term and how is the President removed?
Four years, by electoral college; removal only by impeachment.
What does the "nominal" President's duty under Art. 74 require?
To act on the advice of the Council of Ministers led by the PM.
Five merits of the parliamentary system?
Harmony; responsible government; check on arbitrary authority; ready alternative government; broad representation.
Six limitations?
Instability; policy discontinuity; concentration of power in cabinet; fusion of powers; non-experts; coalition politics and regionalism.
Who warned of the dictatorship of the cabinet?
H.J. Laski and Ramsay Muir.
Which Governments are cited as short-lived?
Morarji Desai, Charan Singh, V.P. Singh, Chandra Sekhar, Deva Gowda, I.K. Gujral.
Who said the cabinet is a hyphen joining the buckle?
Walter Bagehot.
Why did the framers prefer the parliamentary system?
Familiarity (GoI Acts 1919, 1935), responsibility over stability (Ambedkar), no deadlock, diversity, flexibility, Swaran Singh Committee 1975.
What did Ambedkar say about stability and responsibility?
A democratic executive needs both, but no system guarantees both equally; the Assembly preferred responsibility.
Name the seven differences from the British model.
Republic (Art. 52); constitutional supremacy (Art. 13); PM from either House; non-member ministers for six months; no countersigning (Art. 74(1)); no formal shadow cabinet; federal and plural society.
Which committees oversee the government in India instead of a shadow cabinet?
Public Accounts Committee and Estimates Committee.
Mains 2024: “The growth of cabinet system has practically resulted in the marginalisation of the parliamentary supremacy:” Elucidate.
Intro: The cabinet leads the executive and is drawn from the legislature, so a strong majority can dominate Parliament. Body: Collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)) makes cabinet a team that carries the House; Fusion of powers: ministers sit in both organs, the cabinet a hyphen joining executive and legislature (Bagehot); With a strong majority the cabinet and PM can sideline legislative scrutiny: the dictatorship of the cabinet (Laski, Ramsay Muir), seen in the Indira Gandhi and Rajiv Gandhi eras; Prime ministerial government (Crossman, Mackintosh); Checks remain: no-confidence motion, question hour, debates, adjournment motions, Public Accounts and Estimates Committees; judicial review and Fundamental Rights limit Parliament. Close: Parliamentary supremacy needs real scrutiny of a strong cabinet, with the Constitution as the final limit.
Mains 2023: Compare and contrast the British and Indian approaches to Parliamentary sovereignty.
Intro: Britain follows parliamentary sovereignty; India follows constitutional supremacy. Body: Britain: Parliament is the supreme law-making body without constitutional constraint; India: written Constitution is supreme (Art. 13); Indian Parliament is limited by judicial review, Fundamental Rights (Part III) and the federal division of powers (Seventh Schedule); Britain is unitary, India federal and plural; Head of state: hereditary monarch in Britain, elected President (Art. 52) in India. Close: Both are Westminster-style, but India adapted the model to a written, supreme Constitution.

Traps#

Trap 1 · Answerable to Lok Sabha, not the whole Parliament Art. 75(3) makes the Council of Ministers collectively accountable to the Lok Sabha, and a no-confidence vote there removes the whole Council. Rule: collective responsibility = Lok Sabha only.
Trap 2 · Which House the PM may come from In Britain the PM must be in the House of Commons; in India the PM can belong to the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha. A non-member can be a minister for 6 months (Art. 75(5)); British ministers are MPs. Rule: India = either House + 6-month window; UK = lower house only.
Trap 3 · Countersignature and legal responsibility Britain makes ministers countersign the monarch's acts and bear legal responsibility. India has no such rule; the President acts on the binding advice of the Council (Art. 74(1)) and responsibility is collective. Rule: countersignature = Britain; collective responsibility = India.
Trap 4 · Who can dissolve and who can remove The Indian President can dissolve the Lok Sabha on the PM's advice (Art. 85) and the government falls by no-confidence. The US President cannot dissolve the House and is removed only by impeachment. Rule: parliamentary = dissolution + no-confidence; presidential = fixed term + impeachment.
