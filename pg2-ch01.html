1 · Constitutionalism · RULE OF LAW#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Polity handout pp. 3-4. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Constitutionalism means a government whose powers are limited by a supreme law. Rule of Law (Dicey) is one tool for that. Know the meaning, the six features, negative vs positive constitutionalism, why codification alone is not enough, Dicey's three principles and the exceptions to equality.

Constitutionalism: Meaning AND Features

Meaning and concept

  • Powers of the government are not absolute
  • They are limited by a body of laws or a supreme law called the Constitution
  • Government must subscribe to this body of laws as its legitimacy depends on it
  • Hence constitutionalism abhors absolutism
  • tools to limit government: Dicey's Rule of Law and Separation of Powers

Features of constitutionalism

  • Rule of Law
  • Separation of Powers
  • basic civil liberties and protection of people's rights
  • Democracy: temporariness of government through periodic free and fair elections
  • Independent Judiciary
  • Free Media
Memory aid · DR FISC

Six features: Democracy (periodic elections) · Rule of law · Free media · Independent judiciary · Separation of powers · Civil liberties. (own mnemonic)

Negative constitutionalism (prevent tyranny)

  • idea that the State can be a danger or source of oppression gained ground in the late medieval and early modern age
  • developed largely among Western thinkers to prevent tyranny by limiting the powers of the government after oppression by the ruling class

Positive constitutionalism

  • Zeal for limiting power may hinder the State from performing basic functions (e.g. public order), leading to anarchy
  • So the State must be empowered
  • government must have certain powers to perform its functions properly
  • balances power to prevent tyranny (excess power and abuse) and anarchy (inadequate power)
Memory aid · TA

Positive constitutionalism balances Tyranny (too much power) against Anarchy (too little). (own mnemonic)

Constitutionalism beyond the written Constitution

  • these principles are assimilated into the Indian Constitution to varying degrees
  • mere codification is not a sufficient condition: principles must be implemented and observable to citizens in daily life
  • United Kingdom: no single codified Constitution, yet constitutionalism exists
  • so the presence or absence of codification does not guarantee the presence or absence of constitutionalism

RULE OF LAW

Meaning and concept

  • many have contributed, but A. V. Dicey's contribution is the most seminal
  • basically the supremacy of law over arbitrary power: government power exercised according to law, and the law itself consistent with constitutional principles

Dicey's principles

  • Supremacy of law: rule by principles of law, not whims and fancies of the ruler, and the ruler is also subject to law. SC: however high a person may be, the law is above them. Upanishads express similar ideas (Dharma of the king, Lex is Rex). Corollary: arbitrary exercise of power is prohibited
  • Equality before law: all sections of society equally subject to the law of the land. Affirmative action and positive discrimination depart from strict formal equality but promote substantive equality
  • Equality of law administered through ordinary courts: today many forums have adjudicatory functions, e.g. quasi-judicial CAT, ITAT and other tribunals
Memory aid · SEE

Dicey: Supremacy of law · Equality before law · Equal administration through ordinary courts. (own mnemonic)

Exceptions TO Legal Equality

President and Governors: Article 361

  • while in office, no criminal proceedings can be instituted or continued against them
  • no civil proceedings for acts in personal capacity during tenure unless two months' prior notice is given
  • no arrest or imprisonment process from any court during the term of office
  • a departure from strict formal equality

A. G. Noorani's criticism

  • termed the criminal exemption a mindless borrowing from the West, based on British practice where courts cannot prosecute the ruler
  • In Britain courts are historically tied to the authority of the Crown and the sovereign enjoys constitutional immunity
  • In India both the President and the courts are creations of the Constitution, so similar provisions are questionable
  • can be abused: politicians facing criminal prosecution can be appointed Governors
  • if introduced today through a constitutional amendment, it would not get the approval of the public or the courts

Other exceptions

  • diplomatic immunity
  • parliamentary privileges:
    • Article 105(2): an MP is not liable to any court proceedings for anything said or any vote given in Parliament or any committee
    • Protection for speech inside the House, an exception even to defamation law
Memory aid · DPP

Exceptions to equality: Diplomats · Parliamentarians (Art 105(2)) · President and Governors (Art 361). (own mnemonic)

2 · Separation OF Powers · Checks AND Balances#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Polity handout pp. 5-8. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Power should not sit in one institution, so it is split into law-making, law execution and law adjudication. Perfect separation is impossible; India follows a middle path with checks and balances. Know the overlaps, why strictness fails, each check, and the India / USA / UK comparison.

Meaning, Functions, Benefits

Separation of Powers

  • power of government should not be concentrated in one institution but divided among them
  • Traditionally legislative, executive and judicial/adjudicatory
  • In medieval times kings held all, leading to absolutism
  • Montesquieu's name is indelibly associated with the concept
  • functions:
    • Law-making (legislative)
    • Law execution (executive)
    • Law adjudication (judicial)
    • Governance runs on consensus between the three organs, hence Checks and Balances

Benefits

  • preserves democracy and rights of the people
  • healthy division of labour, specialisation and efficiency
  • healthy debate and constructive friction, so deliberation leads to better outcomes

Functional Overlap Among Organs

Value addition

Even in the USA, the traditional home of rigid separation, there is dilution. Perfect separation is not possible in a modern State and is not followed anywhere in the world.

Westminster system

  • in a parliamentary system separation between legislature and executive is never complete
  • executive = permanent bureaucracy plus the Council of Ministers headed by the PM (political executive), who are generally MPs, so a minister is part of both legislature and executive
  • Article 75(5): a non-MP can become a minister but must become a member of either House within six consecutive months
  • in the USA a person cannot be in the Presidential Cabinet and Congress at once: the biggest qualification to be a minister in India is a disqualification in the USA

Delegated legislation

  • Law passed in a broad skeletal form
  • Detailed rules, regulations and bye-laws are framed by the executive
  • law-making is delegated to the executive, a subordinate legislature

Administrative adjudication

  • adjudication is traditionally a judicial function, but here the executive carries it out: e.g. district administration (revenue courts), tribunals like CAT and ITAT
  • technical matters of adjudication are better handled this way

Judge-made laws

  • where law is silent or ambiguous the court clarifies it by interpretation: a law-making function
  • where there is a legal vacuum the court gives guidelines that function as law, e.g. Vishakha Guidelines

Ordinances

  • urgent need for a law and Parliament not in session: the President can promulgate Ordinances under Article 123
  • function as laws passed by Parliament, subject to constitutional requirements

Regulatory bodies

  • emerged worldwide due to the complex modern economy
  • law-making, implementation and adjudication all vested in them:
    • Make rules, regulate the sector, penalise defaulters
    • E.g. SEBI, IRDAI

MPLADS and Article 372

  • MPLADS:
    • An MP's main job is legislating, but here MPs identify local needs and recommend works, a developmental (executive) function
    • Overlap of legislative representation and executive functions, though sanction and implementation stay with administrative authorities
  • Article 372:
    • Pre-Constitution laws could be aligned with the Constitution
    • This power was with the President for the first three years, so the executive performed legislative functions
Memory aid · MAJOR WAD

Eight overlaps: MPLADS · Art 372 · Judge-made law · Ordinances · Regulators · Westminster (minister is MP) · Administrative adjudication · Delegated legislation. (own mnemonic)

Limits OF Strict Separation

Why rigid separation is not maintainable

  • specialisation: traditional organs cannot handle all complex functions of modern administration
  • speed and efficiency: if all tribunal cases went to the judiciary it would be overwhelmed and could not perform its primary functions
  • public interest:
    • Power is not a property but a means to an end
    • If the end (serving people, their welfare) is better served by vesting power in an institution not meant to exercise it, so be it
Memory aid · SSP

Limits of strict separation: Specialisation · Speed · Public interest. (own mnemonic)

System OF Checks AND Balances

Note

India does not follow strict, rigid separation as in the USA but a system of checks and balances (term borrowed from the USA): functions are divided among branches and each acts as a check on the others to prevent abuse of power.

Executive checks the judiciary: appointment of judges

  • on paper the executive has a role (President appoints), but in India the process has come to be substantially controlled by the judiciary through the Collegium System
  • USA: President nominates, subject to Senate confirmation
  • executive participation helps ensure competent judges of integrity

Legislature checks the judiciary: removal of judges

  • Article 124(4):
    • SC judge removed by the President after an address by each House, supported by a majority of the total membership and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting
    • A corresponding process for High Court judges
  • so difficult that not one HC or SC judge has been removed through it to date

Judiciary checks the executive and legislature

  • strikes down unconstitutional laws and illegal or unconstitutional executive action
  • power to determine legality and constitutionality = Judicial Review

Legislature checks the executive

  • laws need enactment by the legislature
  • questions the executive via Question Hour, resolutions and debates
  • extreme case: No-Confidence Motion against the Council of Ministers in the Lok Sabha

Executive checks the judiciary: clemency

  • power to grant clemency for punishments after judicial proceedings: President (Art 72) and Governors (Art 161)
  • A distinct constitutional power, not an ordinary appeal
  • Meant for human errors and unduly harsh punishments
Memory aid · CRJ-NQ

Checks in action: Collegium / appointment · Removal Art 124(4) · Judicial review · No-confidence · Question Hour · clemency (Art 72, 161). (own mnemonic)

Separation OF Powers: India, USA, UK

CountryPosition (handout)
Indiaif the offices of both President and Vice-President are vacant, the CJI discharges the President's functions under the President (Discharge of Functions) Act, 1969; if the CJI is unavailable, the seniormost available SC judge. An overlap where the head of one branch temporarily performs another's functions
USAno such arrangement: more rigid separation than India
UKlooser separation than the USA; highest appellate judicial function used to sit in Parliament (House of Lords), changed by the UK Supreme Court in 2009
Way forward / key point

India follows a middle path between the rigid USA and the looser UK.

NEED FOR Greater Checks ON THE Executive

Why

  • historically more power gets vested in the executive
  • if judicial independence is compromised and the opposition is fragmented, executive rule goes unchecked
  • so a proactive legislature, discussion and an independent judiciary are needed, but in contemporary times both are less effective
  • Less discussion on Bills in Parliament
  • Separation exists on paper but may be weakened in a substantive sense

Institutional checks on executive power

  • CAG, ECI, UPSC and CVC enjoy varying degrees of institutional independence
  • CAG, ECI and UPSC are constitutional bodies
  • CVC is a statutory body
  • so there is no unbridled exercise of power by the political executive
