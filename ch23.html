Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • BrIA 1851 (Calcutta, Radhakanta Deb) as the first all-Indian organisation (no Briton in it) against the first all India INC 1885; the 1851-52 bodies and the Charter Act 1853 trigger; Chetty with the Madras Native Association, not the Madras Mahajan Sabha 1884 (2017)
  • The 'un-British' argument; Downward Filtration 1835; Census 1921 (8% and 2%); Persian background (Golconda 1686, Bijapur 1687); 400+ newspapers and 1.5 lakh readers by 1875
  • Black Acts and Lex Loci Act 1850; Income Tax 1860 and 1868; Great Madras Famine 1876-78; every Lytton 76-80 measure against every Ripon 80-84 measure; Factories Act 1881 = Ripon 80-84 (2007)
  • The Ilbert Bill: its aim, the mixed jury, and why it is not the press or arms law (2003, 2013)
  • The four WEMC associations and their founders; the 1875 petition (2002); the 1883 National Conference match (1996); SNB dismissed from the ICS, Tagore first, Dutt retired, Bose resigned (1999)

Mains hooks

  • Factors behind the rise of the INM: a product of colonial policy, good tools and a bad villain ('stronger the villain, stronger the hero')
  • Why Western education made the INM uneven; how the press, railways and telegraphs made regional leaders national
  • British racism as the accelerant: the Black Acts to Lytton 76-80 to the Ilbert Bill; Lytton against Ripon 80-84
  • Why organised WEMC associations replaced landed-class bodies and what they did (peasants, famine relief, labour, councils)

The landed class petitioned for itself; the Raj's own tools and insults built a class that organised for everyone, and five roots met in the INC of 1885.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Petition, then provoke, then organise, then converge: the landed class petitioned for itself and got nothing; the Raj's own tools (education, press, rails) built the WEMC and its racism provoked it; the WEMC organised for everyone from 1870; and five roots converged in the INC of 1885.

  1. The landlords petition (1851-52). The first organised political voice is not the middle class but the educated landed class. The British Indian Association (BrIA 1851) is set up in Calcutta with Radhakanta Deb as first president; the Bombay Association and the Madras Native Association follow in 1852. All three petition because Parliament is discussing the Charter Act of 1853, and they send three separate petitions, not one. They never oppose British rule: they call exploitation 'un-British'.
    → But nothing is granted.
  2. Nothing is granted. These conservatives have too much to lose: land revenue systems like the Permanent Settlement of 1793 created the landed class, the police protect them, and their status and security depend on the British. The teacher's board puts it as Of, By, For: their bodies were of, by and for the landed class, not for the people.
    → So a different class will have to carry the movement.
  3. The Raj builds a class (after 1857). Higher Western education after 1857 creates a politically conscious Western Educated Middle Class (WEMC), meant to be loyal Brown Sahibs. When it sees no real intent to share power, admiration turns into criticism and then a demand for Indian rights. But education is uneven (the Downward Filtration Theory of 1835 favoured Presidency Town elites; 1921 literacy is only 8%, female 2%), so the movement starts in Calcutta, Bombay and Madras.
    → Now this class needs tools.
  4. The tools: press, rails, wires. By 1875 India has over 400 newspapers and more than 1.5 lakh readers; the press turns regional leaders into national ones. From the 1850s railways and the telegraph let leaders from Bombay, Bengal, Madras and Punjab meet and coordinate. The infrastructure of empire becomes the infrastructure of anti-colonial politics.
    → But the Raj keeps handing out reasons for anger.
  5. The villain does its work. The 1850 Black Acts try to put Europeans in the mofussil under Indian judges and are suspended after a 'white rebellion'. The Lex Loci Act 1850 protects converts' inheritance over Indian opposition. Income Tax (1860, withdrawn, 1868) arrives during famines and draws 'No Taxation Without Representation'. In 1870 Bengal's higher-education budget is cut while military spending continues.
    → And then Lytton arrives.
  6. Lytton antagonises everyone (76-80). Lytton 76-80 cuts the ICS age from 21 to 19 (1876, exam only in London), holds the Delhi Durbar (1877) during the Great Madras Famine, passes the Vernacular Press Act and the Arms Act (1878), creates the nomination-based Statutory Civil Service, and fights the Second Afghan War (1878-80). The Amrita Bazar Patrika turns English overnight to escape the press law.
    → Then a liberal Viceroy is sent.
  7. Ripon, the liberal contrast (80-84). Gladstone's Liberals win in 1880 and send Ripon 80-84. The Rent Commission (1880) leads to the Bengal Tenancy Act 1885; the Factories Act 1881 bans labour under 7; the Vernacular Press Act is repealed and the Arms Act amended (1882); the ICS age goes back to 21. His key reform is Local Self-Government 1882: elected District Boards and municipalities with two-thirds elected. But Europeans in India oppose the simultaneous exam and it fails.
    → How far will Europeans let reform go?
  8. The Ilbert Bill (1883-84). Law Member C.P. Ilbert proposes that Indian district judges may try Britons in the mofussil. Europeans organise, lobby and protest, and the bill is withdrawn and amended to need a mixed jury. For the WEMC the last doubt is gone: the British will not treat Indians as equals. Its press campaigns are the biggest use of the press before 1885.
    → The WEMC decides to organise on its own.
  9. The WEMC organises (1870 onwards). The Poona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870, Ranade) petitions the House of Commons in 1875 for direct Indian representation. Surendranath Banerjee, who lost his ICS career over an age dispute, founds the Indian Association (1876); the Lahore Indian Association follows in 1877 and the Madras Mahajan Sabha in 1884. These bodies are of and by the WEMC but for all the people.
    → And they do real work.
  10. More than talking shops. The new associations lead peasants (Indigo Revolt 1859-63, Bombay Deccan Riots 1875, Punjab Canal Colony 1907), run famine relief and arbitration courts, protest the Plantation Labour Act of 1859 (serfdom), and demand Council seats, separation of powers, lower Home Charges and an extended Permanent Settlement. After the Indian Councils Act of 1892 they use the Councils themselves.
    → Now the roots meet.
  11. Five roots, one Congress (1885). Western education, a free press, racial provocation, organised WEMC associations, and railways and telegraphs all converge on the Indian National Congress at Bombay in 1885: the first all-India political party and the all-India culmination of the regional bodies.

In simple words#

  • The first organised political voice (1851-52) came from landed-class associations in Calcutta, Bombay and Madras. They sent three separate petitions, called exploitation 'un-British' and got nothing.
  • After 1857 the Raj built the tools of a movement: Western higher education (the WEMC), the press, railways and telegraphs. Then its racism and authoritarianism (Black Acts 1850, Income Tax, Lytton 76-80, the Ilbert Bill) supplied the anger: 'stronger the villain, stronger the hero'.
  • Lytton 76-80 antagonised everyone (ICS age cut, Durbar in a famine, Vernacular Press Act, Arms Act, Second Afghan War)
  • Ripon 80-84 was the liberal contrast (Local Self-Government 1882) but Europeans fought him, above all on the Ilbert Bill 1883-84
  • From 1870 the WEMC built its own organised bodies: Poona 1870, Indian Association 1876, Lahore 1877, Madras Mahajan Sabha 1884. They were of and by the WEMC, but for all the people.
  • They led peasants, ran famine relief, fought for labour and used the Councils. Western education, the press, racial provocation, organised associations and railways all converged on the INC at Bombay, 1885.

1 · How the Indian National Movement began#

Story link

Because the INC came late, the story opens before it: who spoke first? Ask: why was the first voice not the educated middle class?

  • The Indian National Movement (INM) was, in its mature form, led by the Western Educated Middle Class (WEMC).
  • But the first organised political voice in modern India was not theirs.
  • It belonged to a different group: the educated landed class.
  • Their regional associations were the earliest formal platforms for putting Indian political demands.
Key takeaway
  • Who led the INM and where did it begin? Mature INM = WEMC.
  • First organised voice = educated landed class, through regional associations (Section 2).

2 · The first associations (1851-52)#

Story link

Because the educated landed class had voice and money, it formed the first bodies (1851-52). Ask: what trigger sent three bodies to petition at once, and what does 'all-Indian' mean?

  • The first major voluntary organisation in India was the British Indian Association [BrIA 1851], set up in Calcutta in 1851.
  • Radhakanta Deb was its first president. Its members came from educated men of the landed class.
  • It was also the first all-Indian organisation in character, unlike Dwarkanath Tagore's Landholders' Society (1838), which had included many non-official Anglo-Indians.
  • (Before 1911, "Anglo-Indian" meant a European settled in India, not a person of mixed parentage.)
  • The same period saw the Bombay Association (1852) and the Madras Native Association (1852).
  • All three were educated, landed and conservative.
  • The immediate reason for all three: the British Parliament was deliberating on the Charter Act of 1853 and Indian elites wanted to put their demands before it.
Teacher's board
  • "All-Indian" is not "all India." BrIA 1851 = "[only Bengal]" and "all-Indian" = "[no Br Person in it]": all-Indian in membership (no Briton), unlike the Landholders' Society (1838), tagged "L ORG" (read as a landed ORG), which had non-official Anglo-Indians.
  • "imp": "1st all-Indian" versus "1st all India [INC 1885]": BrIA = first all-Indian ORG (Indians only), INC = first all-India ORG (whole territory).
  • Bombay row: "Dadabhai Naoroji in BoA 1852" (the same man appears in the 1876 revival, Section 13).

3 · Their demands#

Story link

Because they were landlords, they asked politely and for themselves. Ask: why did three petitions, called 'un-British', get nothing?

  • The three associations sent separate petitions, not a joint one.
  • They were not yet a single national fraternity, but three regional lobbies.
  • Their grievances fell into two kinds: Common grievances: high taxes, expensive and incompetent administration, neglect of education and public works. Elite personal interests: opposition to the salt and opium monopolies.
  • They did not oppose British rule itself.
  • They opposed what they called "un-British" rule: British governance was, by definition, just and fair, and exploitation was a departure from that standard.
  • This is "Brown Sahib" thinking: the phrase "un-British" assumes British rule is good by default.
  • They were not calling colonialism wrong
  • They were calling exploitation un-British
  • No demands were met. These conservatives had too much to lose: the colonial land revenue systems had directly benefited them, and their social standing depended on British patronage.
  • Framework to carry forward:
    • Conservatives want very little change
    • Moderates gradual reform
    • Extremists faster change
    • Revolutionaries immediate and fundamental transformation (the teacher's ladder, Section 0)

4 · The INM after 1857: a product of colonial policy#

Story link

Because the landlords failed, the movement needed a new class and new tools, and the Raj itself supplied both. Ask: which colonial policies were the tools and which the villain?

  • A deep irony: the British built many of the very tools that would later be used against them.
  • The growth of the INM after 1857 was in large part a consequence of colonial policies themselves.
Teacher's board
  • "FYQs:
    • Factors behind rise of INM"
    • "INM was a product of colonial policies." Policies were GOOD or BAD (= the villain).
  • GOOD = Press, Railways, Post and Telegraph, Western Education: "we got" the tools of the INM, modern by default: ideas of modern polity (fundamental rights, welfare State), vocabulary of modern politics, methods of modern politics (modern press, organisations), the press as a voice to demands, better communication (leader to leader, leader to people, people to people).
  • BAD = authoritarian and racist policies = anger, motivation, energy for the INM.
  • Rule:
    • "Stronger the villain, stronger the hero
    • Rise of villain = rise of hero."
Teacher's board
  • A class exists "when" three things hold, joined by AND:
    • (1) the number of members rises
    • (2) consciousness among members of common problems and solutions
    • (3) the members are organised (a small hierarchy)
  • So the WEMC became a class only when numerous, aware of common problems and solutions, and organised (Sections 5 and 13).

5 · Factor 1: Western education#

Story link

Because the Raj taught the educated, it created its own critics. Ask: why was the movement uneven, and who was left out?

  • The rapid expansion of Western higher education after 1857 created a new group: a politically conscious WEMC.
  • (It is specifically higher education that matters: colleges and universities are where people develop political ideology and a sense of public affairs; primary schooling alone does not produce political movements.)
  • The British meant this class to be loyal "Brown Sahibs." But as the WEMC realised the British had no genuine intention of sharing power, admiration turned to criticism, and criticism turned into a demand for Indian rights.
  • Western education did not spread evenly, so the INM grew unevenly.
  • The Downward Filtration Theory (1835) had focused education only on the elites of the Presidency Towns.
  • Mass education stayed neglected, shown by the Census of 1921: only 8% overall literacy and 2% female literacy, despite Wood's Dispatch (1854) and the Hunter Commission (1882-83) both calling for wider expansion.

  • Muslims were comparatively under-represented in early Western-educated politics: partly from a wish to preserve Persian literary culture, partly from the conservative influence of the Ulamas.

6 · Factor 2: the press#

Story link

Because the WEMC now had readers, the press carried its voice. Ask: how does a regional leader become a national one?

  • By 1875, India had over 400 newspapers in English and vernacular languages, with a combined readership of more than 1.5 lakh.
  • The press was the primary tool of the WEMC.
  • It built inter-regional awareness, spread nationalist ideas, and helped regional leaders grow into national ones.
  • When a regional leader begins writing and speaking on all-India issues (the budget, civil services, famine), he gradually becomes a national leader.
  • The press was the engine of that change.
Teacher's board
  • "Regional + all India": the press turns a regional leader into an all-India leader.

8 · Factor 4: British racism and authoritarianism#

Story link

Because the Raj kept insulting the educated, anger grew. Ask: which provocations are dated and which is a result?

Key takeaway
  • "If Western education was the slow fire that built the INM, British racism was the accelerant." Every act of racial discrimination reminded Indians of their subordinate status and added recruits to the movement.
  • It is the biggest single contributor to the movement's growth.
  • (Like a film villain, it gives the hero a reason to rise.)

8.1 1850: the Black Acts (Bethune's Bills)

  • Before 1850, a European charged with a crime in the mofussil (rural areas outside the Presidency Towns) could be tried only by the Supreme Court in a Presidency Town (Calcutta, Bombay or Madras).
  • That bypassed the local Company courts where Indian judges (Sadr Amins, Munsifs) sat.
  • This made justice for ordinary Indians nearly impossible: witnesses and complainants had to travel to a Presidency Town at great cost.
  • The Black Acts ended this privilege and brought Europeans in the mofussil under the criminal jurisdiction of the ordinary district courts, where Indians served as judges.
  • Europeans protested so fiercely in the press that it is called the "white rebellion," and the Acts had to be suspended.
  • Recall: the Charter Act of 1833 had promised racial equality.
  • The Black Acts tried to act on that promise.
  • The same principle returned in the Ilbert Bill of 1883 (Section 12).

11 · Local Self-Government (1882)#

Story link

Because Ripon wanted to train Indians in self-government, local bodies were elected. Ask: what did the government still control?

  • Ripon's most significant reform was the Local Self-Government Resolution/Act of 1882.
  • The stated goal was to train Indians in the practice of self-government.
  • Rural: elected District Boards across British India.
  • Members were to be elected by land revenue payers and rent payers, not nominated by officials.
  • Urban: municipalities were strengthened: at least two-thirds of members elected, and the chairperson a non-official member.
  • Charge: local bodies were given health, education, sanitation, roads and communications, with autonomy in day-to-day functioning.
  • The government kept the power to inspect and dissolve a local body (regulation, not total control).
  • The Hunter Education Commission (1882-83) followed: it recommended transfer of education to local bodies and a focus on mass education in vernacular languages, especially female education. Implementation remained poor.

12 · The Ilbert Bill controversy (1883-84)#

Story link

Because Europeans would not accept Indian judges over Britons, the bill was watered down. Ask: why did this episode radicalise the WEMC most?

Key takeaway

The single episode that most sharply exposed British racial attitudes and most decisively radicalised the WEMC was the Ilbert Bill controversy of 1883-84.

  • Drafted by Law Member C.P. Ilbert, the bill proposed to extend the jurisdiction of Indian district judges to try cases involving Britons in the mofussil (rural) areas.
  • It would end an existing privilege: a Briton in rural India could not be tried before an Indian judge.
  • The European community's reaction was immediate and furious: they organised, lobbied and protested, and the bill had to be withdrawn and amended.
  • The final version required a mixed jury in any case involving a Briton.
  • For the WEMC this settled any remaining doubt: the British would not treat Indians as equals in courts, in civil services or in political life.
  • The controversy triggered massive press campaigns across the country and is rightly called a major milestone in the growth of Indian political consciousness.

Recall sheet#

Read the question, answer in your head, then tap to check.

What does INC decode as on the teacher's board?I = Indian, N = Nation = Pol, C = ORG. A political party is an ORG with the motive of power over the State (here the colonial State), so the INC = the Indian people's ORG, the first all-India political party (1885).
What is "Of, By, For"?Landed-class bodies were of, by and for the landed class (sad: not for the people). WEMC bodies were of and by the WEMC but for all the people (landed, WEMC, peasants, workers = masses).
What is the teacher's ladder of methods?Conservatives (minimum change), Moderates (3P: press, petitions, prayers; FIR, court, elections), Besant and Malviya (3P, go to the people, no street protests), MG (non-violent street protest, no redressal channels), Extremists (violence if peaceful methods fail), Revolutionaries (press and guns).
Which was the first major voluntary organisation and why "all-Indian"?The British Indian Association [BrIA 1851], Calcutta, Radhakanta Deb first president. All-Indian = no Briton in it (but only Bengal); the INC (1885) is the first all India.
Why were the BrIA, Bombay Association and Madras Native Association founded?Parliament was deliberating on the Charter Act of 1853 and Indian elites wanted to present demands. They sent three separate petitions: three regional lobbies.
What were the early associations' grievances and stance?Common: high taxes, costly and incompetent administration, neglect of education and public works. Elite: salt and opium monopolies. Stance: not anti-British but "un-British" (Brown Sahib thinking). Nothing was granted.
Why was nothing granted?The conservatives had too much to lose: land revenue systems (PS 1793) created the landed class, the police protected them, and their status and security depended on the British.
Why was the INM a product of colonial policy?Good policies gave tools (press, railways, post and telegraph, Western education, modern political ideas and methods); bad ones (racism, authoritarianism) gave anger and energy: "stronger the villain, stronger the hero".
When does a group become a class (teacher)?When the numbers rise AND they are conscious of common problems and solutions AND they are organised.
How did Western education build and unbalance the INM?Post-1857 higher education created a WEMC (Brown Sahibs turned critics). Downward Filtration 1835 favoured Presidency Town elites, so the INM was uneven: Calcutta, Bombay, Madras first; 1921 census 8% literacy, 2% female.
Who led the early INM and why were Muslims under-represented?Upper-caste Marathis, Tamilians and Bengalis of the Presidency Towns (rich, UC Hindus). Muslims: wish to preserve Persian literary culture, Ulama conservatism (insecurity, getting hurt).
Why was Persian embedded in Muslim culture?Delhi Sultanate and Mughal court used Persian; Humayun 30-40 took Safavid (Shia) help against the Suris (1540-55); Awadh Nawabs were Shia; Golconda (1686) and Bijapur (1687) were Shia-ruled until Aurangzeb 58-07 annexed them.
How did the press and railways help the INM?By 1875, 400+ papers and 1.5 lakh readers turned regional leaders into national ones; railways and telegraphs (from the 1850s) let leaders from Bombay, Bengal, Madras and Punjab meet and coordinate.
What were the Black Acts (1850)?Bethune's Bills: Europeans in the mofussil to be tried in district courts with Indian judges. The Anglo-Indian "white rebellion" in the press got them suspended. They acted on the Charter Act 1833's racial-equality promise.
What was the Lex Loci Act 1850?The Removal of Religious/Caste Disabilities Act: inheritance rights for converts from any religion to any other; passed despite Indian opposition; seen as promoting conversion to Christianity.
What was the Income Tax story?Introduced 1860, withdrawn under press pressure, re-introduced 1868 during famines; slogan "No Taxation Without Representation".
What was the Great Madras Famine and why does it matter?1876-78, two years' drought, about 1 crore dead. The Delhi Durbar (1877) was held in the middle of it; Lytton's Afghan war also drained the treasury.
What were Lytton 76-80's six provocations?ICS age 21 to 19 (1876); Delhi Durbar 1877; Statutory Civil Service (nomination); Vernacular Press Act 1878; Arms Act 1878; Second Afghan War 1878-80. Mnemonic: DANGER.
What was the Vernacular Press Act 1878?A security bond forfeited for "objectionable" (anti-British) matter. Gladstone criticised it; the Amrita Bazar Patrika became English overnight; the teacher: "Financial Surgical Strike". Repealed 1882 by Ripon 80-84.
What was the Arms Act 1878 and what did Ripon 80-84 do?Indians needed a licence; Europeans and Eurasians were exempt (B.C. Pal campaigned). 1882: amended so everyone needs one (amended, not repealed).
What was the Statutory Civil Service?A service entered by nomination, not examination; only loyalists chosen (about 1%); colonial "lateral entry". Handout 1878, slide 1879; the slide ties it to the GOI Act 1870.
Why was the Second Afghan War criticised?A costly war from fear of Russia, with no real threat, while the treasury was strained by famine relief; the First Afghan War 1838-42 had failed for the same reason.
What did Ripon 80-84 do?Rent Commission 1880 (Bengal Tenancy Act 1885); Factories Act 1881; VPA repealed 1882; Arms Act amended 1882; ICS age back to 21; Local Self-Government 1882; Hunter Commission 1882-83. Mnemonic: LIBERAL.
What did the Factories Act 1881 do and who opposed it?Banned child labour under 7, capped hours for under 12 (9 hours), 4 monthly holidays. Moderate Indian leaders opposed it because it raised costs for struggling Indian capitalists. Prelims 2007: Ripon 80-84.
What were the features of Local Self-Government 1882?Elected District Boards (land revenue and rent payers); municipalities at least two-thirds elected with a non-official chair; charge of health, education, sanitation, roads, communications; the government could inspect and dissolve. Today: Part IX and IX-A (73rd and 74th Amendments 1992).
What was the Ilbert Bill and how did it end?Drafted by Law Member C.P. Ilbert: Indian district judges to try Britons in the mofussil. European agitation forced withdrawal and amendment: a mixed jury. It radicalised the WEMC and saw the biggest press use before 1885.
Name the four WEMC associations with founders.Poona Sarvajanik Sabha 1870 (Ranade); Indian Association 1876 (Surendranath Banerjee); Lahore Indian Association 1877 (Dyal Singh Majithia); Madras Mahajan Sabha 1884 (Veeraraghavachariar, Subramania Iyer, Anandacharlu).
What did the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha do in 1875?Petitioned the House of Commons for direct Indian representation (Prelims 2002); the teacher: Indians should vote in British elections.
What did SNB's ICS story show?An age (calendar) dispute cost him the service: dismissed (entered 1869, dismissed 1874 per the slide). That injustice became political fuel; he founded the Indian Association (1876). Prelims 1999.
What did the WEMC associations do?Led peasants (Indigo, Deccan Riots, Canal Colony); famine relief and arbitration courts; labour rights (Plantation Labour Act 1859: serfdom); political demands (Councils, separation of powers, Home Charges, PS 1793); later used the Councils after 1892.
What are the five roots of the INC?Western education, a free press, racial provocation, organised WEMC associations, railways and telegraphs, all converging on the INC at Bombay in 1885. Mnemonic: PAPER.
