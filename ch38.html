Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • Leader-to-movement pairs: Baba Ramchandra (Awadh Kisan Sabha 1920), Madari Pasi (Eka 1921), Vallabhbhai Patel (Borsad 1922-24 and Bardoli 1928).
  • UP Kisan Sabha, Feb 1918: Gauri Shankar Mishra, Indra Narayan Dwivedi, Madan Mohan Malaviya. Nehru was NOT associated (he came in June 1920).
  • Bardoli numbers: 30% rise in land revenue cut to 6.03% by an independent tribunal; title Sardar; Kaliparaj renamed Rani Paraj.
  • Mapilla 1921: Malabar, Hindu Jenmies, martial law. Akali: SGPC 1920, Gurudwara Reform Act 1925, Keys Affair, Guru Ka Bagh.
  • Five PYQs on the slides: Prelims 1996, 1997, 2003, 2005, 2026. Every one is a name/place/number pair.

Mains hooks

  • Why did peasants join the INM, and why were these movements still independent of INC? (symbiotic, not hierarchical)
  • Bardoli as the template of Gandhian method: constructive work, local leader, legislators resign, Gandhi arrives, tribunal.
  • Why Eka and Mapilla failed (violence, lost leadership) while Borsad and Bardoli succeeded.
  • Akali Movement: religious reform as a peasant and nationalist movement; positives and negatives for the INM.

Teacher marks this chapter important for both Prelims and Mains; learn the five movements as a ladder from disorganised unrest to disciplined satyagraha.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: After the Home Rule Leagues and the Non-Cooperation Movement taught villagers the language of rights and the tools of protest, peasants in Awadh, Malabar, Gujarat and Punjab organised around their own grievances: cesses, eviction, forced labour, rent and revenue. These movements were boosted by the INM but never owned by the INC. Those that dropped non-violence or lost their leaders (Eka, Mapilla) were crushed or derailed, while Borsad and Bardoli, run on Gandhian lines by Patel, won concrete outcomes. The Akalis fought a religious-reform battle that also politicised Punjab's peasantry.

  1. The countryside is educated. The Home Rule Leagues (1916-18) and NCM (1920-22) taught urban workers and rural ryots to take part in politics. Peasants learnt by doing and saw the use of satyagraha, boycott, civil disobedience and social boycott. This gave them a vocabulary of protest for their own fights.
    → So peasants began organising, but they had real grievances of their own.
  2. Awadh peasants are squeezed. Big landlords (zamindars and taluqdars) levied illegal abwabs and nazrana, evicted at will (bedakhli), and forced hari and begar. WWI inflation raised prices while rents and cesses did not fall.
    → So they set up their own Sabhas.
  3. Kisan Sabha 1918 and Awadh Kisan Sabha 1920. The UP Kisan Sabha was set up in Feb 1918 by Mishra and Dwivedi with Malaviya as senior patron. Malaviya was a Moderate and opposed the Non-Cooperation Movement, so Baba Ramchandra, with Nehru's support, founded the Awadh Kisan Sabha in 1920 for extra-constitutional struggle.
    → But the movement turned violent in 1921.
  4. Repression plus a concession. In 1921 the British used the Seditious Meetings Act (1911) to crush the agitation. They also passed the Awadh Rent Amendment Act (1921), which met some rent grievances and took the sting out of it.
    → Meanwhile a parallel Awadh movement rose under a new leader.
  5. Eka 1921: a lower-caste leader. The Eka Movement had the same grievances plus thikedars (rent collectors) and the same methods. Madari Pasi, from the lower castes, led it. It did not hold to non-violence, so the British crushed it on law-and-order grounds by 1922.
    → But Awadh was not the only region in revolt.
  6. Malabar 1921: Mapilla revolt. Muslim tenants, the Mapillas, were part of Khilafat and angry with Hindu Jenmies over rents and eviction. INC joined at first, but arrests of INC leaders left the revolt without guidance. It turned violent and communal, and the British declared martial law.
    → Then NCM itself was withdrawn, and the next fight was fought on pure Gandhian lines.
  7. Borsad 1922-24: dual tyranny. After NCM was withdrawn in 1922, Patel led Borsad against dacoits, police collusion and an additional police tax. Villagers refused the tax non-violently and volunteer corps warned them of police confiscation. The Governor of Bombay ordered an inquiry and withdrew the tax.
    → Patel's organising skill was now proven, and Bardoli would test it at scale.
  8. Bardoli 1928: the model. Bardoli had Kaliparaj and Ujaliparaj, the Hali system and a 30% revenue rise based on a miscalculation. Gandhi did constructive work from 1927, Patel led from 1928, Bombay legislators resigned, and Gandhi arrived. A tribunal cut the rise to 6.03%, and the women of Bardoli called Patel Sardar.
    → Meanwhile in Punjab a religious fight was becoming a peasant and national one.
  9. Punjab: Akalis versus Mahants. Udasi Mahants, enriched by Ranjit Singh RS01-39's land grants, controlled Gurudwaras and backed the British. They honoured General Dyer after Jallianwala Bagh, and the outrage launched the Akali Movement (1920-25). Nankana, the Keys Affair and Guru Ka Bagh followed.
    → The British finally gave way because they feared the Army.
  10. Outcome and lesson. The British passed the Gurudwara Reform Act, 1925, and the elected SGPC took all Gurudwaras. Gandhi had already withdrawn support over the Nabha issue. The chapter's lesson is that the INM gave peasants consciousness and language, and the peasants gave the INM its mass character.

In simple words#

  • Two-way street: INC took Swaraj to the villages, and peasants took land and rent into the INM.
  • These were not INC movements. They had their own grievances, leaders and organisations, and were only boosted by NCM, Khilafat and the Home Rule Leagues.
  • Awadh: cesses, eviction, forced labour and inflation. The Kisan Sabha (non-INC method: satyagraha) and the Eka (violent) were the two outlets.
  • Violence or lost leadership = defeat: Eka was crushed, and the Mapilla revolt turned communal after INC leaders were arrested.
  • Gujarat = Gandhian success: Borsad (no extra police tax) and Bardoli (30% hike cut to 6.03%), both led by Patel.
  • Akalis fought for Gurudwara control, joined NCM, and won the Gurudwara Reform Act 1925 because the British feared Sikh disaffection in the Army.
  • Trajectory to remember: Kisan Sabha 1918, Awadh Kisan Sabha 1920, Eka 1921, Borsad 1922-24, Bardoli 1928.

0 · The chapter on one board#

Story link

Because the national movement had reached the villages, the peasant had to fit into a bigger picture of strands. Ask: where do peasant movements sit among the strands of the INM?

Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: this chapter is important for both Prelims and Mains.
  • Chapter 39 (Revolutionaries Post 1922) comes next, and the teacher says to expect a FYQ in Mains on Revolutionaries.

0.1 Strands of the national movement

  • INM for nation state / Swaraj: the people's side has Moderates, Extremists and MG; the Revolutionaries are modern politics too. The 1857 Revolt is added as non-modern.
  • Class movements of the masses: Urban has the working class (WC); Rural has peasant movements, plus pre- and post-1857 peasant revolts. WC and peasant movements both feed into modern politics.
  • Social reforms (19th and 20th century): Dalits and Women. MG made the INC a social-reform organisation as well, e.g. Constructive Work (CW).
Teacher's board

Teacher's board: this chapter belongs to the rural class-movement strand. It sits between the pre- and post-1857 peasant revolts and the working-class movement.

1 · How did Peasant Movements connect with INM?#

Story link

Because the HRLs and NCM spread political awareness, peasants began to organise, yet their anger was about land and rent. Ask: who gave what to whom?

  • The peasant movements of the 1920s were heavily influenced by the INM: the energy of the Home Rule League Movement (1916-18), NCM (1920-22) and the Khilafat agitation.
  • These movements created political awareness among the rural masses and gave them a vocabulary of protest.
  • In turn, peasant movements merged with the INM by bringing the countryside into the national struggle.
  • A two-way street: INC raised the issue of Swaraj among peasants, and peasants raised land and rent within the INM.

1.1 What the INM gave the peasants

Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: HRLs (1916-18) plus NCM (1920-22) gave political education of the masses: urban WC and rural ryots became more politically aware, and rural areas became part of the INM.
  • Participation plus learning by doing taught the utility of tools: satyagraha, boycott, civil disobedience and social boycott. These were used later in peasant movements too.
  • Two enemies appear on the board: the INM in NCM was against the British, and peasant movements were also against lenders and the British GOI.
  • The Home Rule Leagues gave peasants confidence, so the Kisan Sabha demands are peasant demands.

1.2 What the peasants gave the INM

Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: if peasants join, the INM gets a big chunk of the nation, because in an agrarian economy the masses are the rural masses, that is the peasants.
  • If INM leaders raise peasant issues and make peasant demands part of INM demands, the peasant class movement gets leadership, organisation and strength.

1.3 Linked to INM but independent of INC

  • These peasant movements were not of INC and were independent of INC.
  • The peasantry had its own grievances (illegal cesses, forced labour, eviction), its own leaders and often its own methods.
  • INC's relationship was sometimes supportive, sometimes distant and sometimes uncomfortable, depending on how far the peasants were willing to go.
Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: the peasant class movement was linked to the INM but independent of it. The same holds for the WC movement.
  • Independent means they had their own leaders and organisations, which are not INM organisations or leaders. Peasant movements began independent of the INM.
Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: mostly there is a positive correlation. When the INM rises, the peasant class movement rises, and when the INM falls, it falls.
  • Later the INM correlates with the WC movement too. But in some cases where communism is inversely related to the INM, there is divergence between the INM and the WC and peasant movements (WC examples: CDM, and a second movement the board abbreviates; check your notebook).
  • Reason: communists placed INM or national interests below class interests.
Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: communism has an industrial stream (Marx, Stalin) and an agrarian stream (Mao, hence Maoism). Indian Naxalism was called Maoism.
  • No private ownership, state control and a one-party system make it the teacher's 'Bigger Hitler of the Left Wing'.
Key takeaway
  • INM gave consciousness and language; peasants gave the INM its mass character.
  • The relationship is symbiotic, not hierarchical.

2 · Kisan Sabha Movement (1918) and Awadh Kisan Sabha (1920)#

Story link

Because Awadh's landlords took more than the rent and the HRLs had given peasants a voice, they formed Sabhas. Ask: why did Baba Ramchandra start a second Sabha in 1920?

  • Awadh peasants faced strong oppression from big landlords (zamindars and taluqdars). Their grievances:
  • Illegal abwabs and nazrana: extra cesses beyond the legal rent, with no authorisation.

2.2 Role of the Home Rule Leagues

  • The Home Rule League Movement (1916-18) created political awareness among the masses.
  • This awareness inspired the beginning of the Kisan Sabha movement itself: peasants who had heard the language of rights and self-rule began to organise around their own demands.

2.3 The organisations

  • UP Kisan Sabha set up in February 1918, co-founded by Gauri Shankar Mishra, Indra Narayan Dwivedi and Madan Mohan Malaviya.
  • Mishra and Dwivedi were the operational organisers; Malaviya was the senior political patron lending his weight.
  • Jawaharlal Nehru was NOT associated with UPKS 1918. He engaged with the Kisan Sabha movement only from June 1920, when he visited Pratapgarh with Baba Ramchandra.
  • Kisan Sabha leaders attended INC sessions, linking the peasant cause to the national movement.
  • In 1920 Baba Ramchandra emerged as the leader of peasants in Awadh, and Nehru developed close contact with the movement.
Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: the Home Rule Leagues gave the peasants confidence.
  • Malaviya was a Moderate, therefore against the satyagraha method: no NC (Non-Cooperation) and no CD (Civil Disobedience).

  • Handout alert: the handout says UPKS was co-founded by all three (Mishra, Dwivedi, Malaviya). Use the handout wording for descriptive answers.

2.4 Why the Awadh Kisan Sabha formed, and its methods

  • Since Malaviya was a Moderate, he did not support NCM. So Baba Ramchandra (with Nehru's support) set up the Awadh Kisan Sabha in 1920 for extra-constitutional struggle: methods beyond petitions and prayers, towards active resistance.
  • Methods: resisting eviction, social boycott of those who did not join, and refusal of forced labour.
Teacher's board

Teacher's board: for the AKS 1920, 'extra-constitutional' means the method is satyagraha.

2.5 What happened next?

  • In 1921 the movement turned violent. The British suppressed it under the Seditious Meetings Act (1911).
  • The British also brought the Awadh Rent Amendment Act (1921), which met some rent grievances and took the sting out of the agitation.
  • The movement ended partly through repression and partly through a legislative concession.

3 · Eka Movement (1921) — Awadh#

Story link

Because the Awadh Kisan Sabha was being crushed, the same grievances found a second outlet under a new kind of leader. Ask: what did Madari Pasi's movement refuse to accept that Gandhi insisted on?

  • The Eka Movement arose in Awadh with the same grievances as the Kisan Sabha: landlord oppression plus the extra tyranny of thikedars, rent collectors who squeezed peasants harder than the zamindars.
  • The methods were the same: resistance, boycott, refusal of forced labour.
  • The key difference was leadership: Madari Pasi, a leader from the lower castes, emerged at the head. This shows the peasant struggle was not just upper-caste.
  • Unlike the Gandhian movements, which made non-violence non-negotiable, the Eka did not adhere to non-violence. This let the British repress it on law-and-order grounds, and it was crushed by 1922.
  • Eka shows a tension within the INM: Gandhi's method required strict non-violence, but many peasant leaders, especially of lower-caste background, saw non-violence as a restraint favouring the oppressor. Madari Pasi was the Extremist, not the Gandhian, of the peasant world.
Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: Eka methods were the same, plus violence if repressed.
  • Eka is not the MG way: the board calls its leader the Extremist.
Key takeaway
  • Eka = Awadh, 1921, Madari Pasi, lower castes, thikedars, not non-violent, crushed by 1922.

4 · Mapilla Revolt (1921) — Malabar#

Story link

Because Malabar's Muslim tenants had both a Khilafat cause and a landlord problem, their anger could turn either way. Ask: what turned it from agrarian to communal?

4.1 Background and grievance

  • In Malabar (present-day Kerala), Muslim tenants, called Mapillas or Moplahs, were part of the Khilafat movement.
  • The Mapillas had a long history of revolt: earlier uprisings in the 1840s and 1870s, driven by the same agrarian grievance.
  • Primary grievance: oppression by landlords, predominantly Hindu Jenmies (hereditary landowners) who controlled vast lands. Mapilla tenants suffered heavy rents and arbitrary eviction.

4.2 What happened?

  • INC joined and supported the movement initially.
  • When INC leaders were arrested, there was a lack of leadership. Without organised political guidance, the revolt turned violent and took a communal colour: anger against Jenmies as an economic class turned into Hindu-Muslim violence.
  • The British declared martial law and suppressed the revolt.
Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: the INC (Khilafat issue) had organised peasant ryots on the Khilafat issue, a positive for the INM.
  • The board also notes anger against the British next to the landlords (the rest of that line: check your notebook).
Key takeaway
  • The Mapilla Revolt is one of the saddest chapters of the period: a genuine agrarian grievance derailed into communal violence for lack of strong secular leadership.
  • It also hurt Hindu-Muslim unity within NCM.
Handout alert
  • Slide hook (Prelims 1997 explanation): the Moplah Revolt in Malabar, 1921, was led by Ali Musliyar and Varian Kunnath Haji. These names are not in the handout, so treat them as extra detail.

5 · Borsad Satyagraha (1922–24) — Gujarat (Post-NCM)#

Story link

Because NCM was withdrawn in 1922, Gujarat's peasants took a fresh route under Patel. Ask: what made Borsad a textbook satyagraha?

5.1 The grievance

  • After NCM was withdrawn in 1922, Gujarat saw a unique form of peasant mobilisation under Vallabhbhai Patel.
  • In Borsad there was monetary collusion between dacoits and the police: the police were in league with the dacoits and not protecting the people.
  • The Government also applied an additional tax on villagers to fund an additional police force against dacoity.
  • Dual tyranny: dacoits looted them, the police who should protect them colluded with dacoits, and the Government made peasants pay for an extra police force that was not doing its job.

5.2 The action

  • Patel asked villagers not to pay the additional tax, and not to resort to violence even if the Government confiscated movable property.
  • Volunteer corps were raised who alerted villagers when police came for confiscation, giving people time to run away, so the police found empty villages.
Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: a police vehicle goes village to village while lookouts warn each village. A small organisation can take several forms (Samiti, Society, Corps, Chhavnis), and the board marks Corps as the form used here.
  • Patel's organisational skill shows in the voluntary corps.

5.3 The result

  • The Governor of Bombay, upset over the accusation of police-dacoit collusion, ordered an inquiry.
  • This led to an immediate halt to confiscation and the withdrawal of the additional tax levied for the extra police force.
Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: Clear goal: no additional tax for the pay of the police force, and not a no-land-revenue campaign.
  • The concrete outcome was an inquiry for truth, and the organisational skill was the voluntary corps.
  • The slide's answer note: the Sarabandi (no-tax) campaign of 1922 was led by Vallabhbhai Patel, a great organiser like MG.
Key takeaway
  • Borsad is a small but textbook Gandhian satyagraha: specific grievance, non-violent resistance, moral pressure on the authorities, a concrete outcome. Patel's organisational skills were already visible.
Handout alert
  • The teacher's PYQ slide for Prelims 1996 adds: Patel emphasised the principal hardships and the need for complete unity and non-violence.

6 · Bardoli Satyagraha (1928) — Surat, Gujarat#

Story link

Because the INM was in a passive phase after 1922, Bardoli was the proving ground for CDM. Ask: what steps raised the tempo?

  • The Bardoli Satyagraha of 1928 was among the most significant peasant movements of the 1920s and a preparation ground for CDM (1930-34).
  • INC had originally planned CDM to begin from Bardoli itself.

6.1 Background

  • The people of Bardoli (Surat) were already politically organised, a consciousness built during NCM.
  • Two main caste groups: Kaliparaj (lower castes) and Ujaliparaj (upper castes), with a bonhomie between them and efforts to prepare for CDM.
  • Bhajan Mandalis were used to raise political awareness among the rural population.

6.2 The grievance

  • Two forms of oppression: hereditary forced labour and alcohol addiction leading to a debt trap, both tied to the Hali system.
  • On top, the Government imposed a 30% increase in land revenue assessment, unjust and based on a miscalculation.
Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: the Hali system is a social issue (an Indian issue), and the 30% hike is the fight against the British.
  • Chain: alcohol leads to debt, and debt leads to forced labour.
  • The miscalculation: the Government over-estimated the rise in the earnings of ryots, and on that basis raised land revenue 30%.

6.3 Constructive work and local leadership

  • Gandhi led a campaign through his newspapers Young India and Navjivan and visited Bardoli in 1927.
  • He worked to unite the low-caste Kaliparaj with the Ujaliparaj by opposing the Hali system, essential because caste divisions would have weakened any collective resistance.
  • Gandhi renamed the Kaliparaj as Rani Paraj, People of the Forests, giving them a dignified identity.
  • Vallabhbhai Patel took leadership in 1928.

6.4 Focus and organisation

  • The focus was kept limited to unjust aspects only; it was not a revolt against the entire revenue system.
  • Patel highlighted the miscalculation in the 30% rise and asked peasants to pay land revenue only at the old rates. He demanded an independent tribunal to examine the assessment.
  • Here Patel received the title Sardar from the women and ryots of Bardoli.
  • Organisation: Chhavnis as the basic unit, women included, social boycott of non-supporters, and the Bardoli Patrika newspaper to spread awareness of the miscalculated assessment.

6.5 Building pressure and the result

  • Incremental pressure: members of the Provincial Legislative Assembly (PLA) resigned, e.g. K.M. Munshi. Gandhi then reached Bardoli in 1928, adding his weight.
  • A truce followed. An independent tribunal was appointed and revised the increase down to 6.03%, from the original 30%.
  • MG style of arbitration: reveal economic truth and seek a middle path based on facts, not maximalist demands.

7 · Akali Movement (1920–25) — Punjab#

Story link

Because Mahants controlling Gurudwaras honoured Dyer, Sikh devotees would no longer tolerate them, so a religious reform fight became a peasant and national one. Ask: why did the British give way?

Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: the Akali Movement (1920-25) is a social reform movement. Goal: take control of Gurudwaras to the people from corrupt Mahants. It is also called a peasant movement, because the participants were ryots.
  • Synergy: the Akalis joined NCM. They supported the NCM goal (Swaraj, Khilafat wrongs and Punjab wrongs) and followed its method, satyagraha. INC supported the demands of the Akalis. (The board's NCM end year reads unclear: check your notebook; the handout says NCM 1920-22.)
  • Result: SGPC, Gurudwaras Reform Act 1925, and splits into the Unionist Party (moderate), Akali Dal (extremist) and Kirti Kisan Party (communist).
  • Positive for the INM: masses of Punjab entered the INM, and there was Hindu-Muslim-Sikh unity.
  • Teacher's remark: Dyer = Gadha (a fool), whose act ultimately hurt British national interests.

7.1 Background

  • The movement was a struggle for control of Gurudwaras, Sikh places of worship under Udasi Sikh Mahants since the 18th century.
  • Its origins go back to the Singh Sabha Movement of the 1880s, which first raised Sikh religious reform.
  • Mahants had control because they received rich lands from Ranjit Singh RS01-39, the ruler of the Sikh Empire. Over time they became corrupt and lived in luxury.
  • After the British annexation of Punjab in 1849, the British supported the Mahants against Sikh reformers. The priests of the Golden Temple also issued a hukamnama against the Ghadarites.
  • The Mahants honoured General Dyer after the Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 1919. This outrage laid the foundation of the Akali Movement.
Key takeaway
  • Jallianwala Bagh triggered the Akali Movement: Mahants honoured the very man who ordered the massacre of Sikhs at Amritsar.

7.2 Organisations

  • Core demand: control of Gurudwaras.
  • The British gave control of the Golden Temple easily, probably hoping to pacify the Akalis. This was the British policy of appeasing the moderates and suppressing the extremists among the Akalis.
  • SGPC formed in 1920 for organised management of the Golden Temple.
  • Shiromani Akali Dal (SAD) formed for further organised protests against Mahants, to win other Gurudwaras. The British supported the Mahants and tried to repress the movement.

7.3 Three important events

Teacher's board

Teacher's board: the three events (Nankana, Keys Affair, Guru Ka Bagh) are marked not important. Know them only in a line each.

EventWhat happened

7.4 Why the British agreed, and Gandhi's withdrawal

  • The British were anxious about disaffection in the Army, since Sikhs formed a significant part of the British Indian Army. They did not concede on principle but because they could not afford Sikh disaffection in the ranks.
  • Gandhi withdrew support when the Akalis protested for the restoration of the deposed Raja of Nabha (a Princely State). The movement turned violent, and Gandhi's support was conditional on non-violence.

7.5 Final outcome

  • To prevent disaffection in the Army, the British passed the Gurudwara Reform Act, 1925. It was a success: the SGPC, an elected body of Sikhs, got control of all Gurudwaras.

8 · A Note on Peasant Movements and INC#

Story link

Because each movement had its own leader and method, the INC's role differed every time. Ask: what is the single line that decides most exam questions?

  • Peasant movements of the 1920s were boosted by the INM: the Home Rule Movement, NCM and Khilafat. But they were not of INC and were independent of INC.
  • Peasants had their own grievances, leaders and often their own methods.
  • INC's engagement was real but strategic: the countryside gave the INM its mass base, but INC leadership was not always comfortable with where peasant anger could go.
  • The INM gave peasant movements political consciousness and language; peasant movements gave the INM its mass character. The relationship was symbiotic, not hierarchical.
Teacher's board
  • Teacher's board: UP has UPKS 1918, AKS 1920 and Eka 1921. Malabar (Madras) has the Moplah Revolt 1921. Then 1922 Chauri Chaura. Gujarat has Borsad 1922-24 and Bardoli 1928.
