Health Structure AND Status IN India

Key point

Health is the foundation of human capital and economic productivity. The WHO Constitution (1946) envisages the highest attainable standard of health as a fundamental right of every human being.

Constitutional foundation

  • The Supreme Court in Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal and other cases has affirmed the Right to Health as part of Article 21.
  • DPSP Article 39(e) (health of workers), Article 41 (assistance for sick and disabled), Article 42 (maternity benefits), Article 47 (nutrition and public health) and Article 48A (pollution-free environment) provide the constitutional foundation for State intervention in health.

Number card

  • Article 21 right to health
  • DPSP 39(e), 41, 42, 47, 48A
  • WHO Constitution 1946
  • Bhore Committee 1946.

Architecture OF THE Indian Healthcare System

Key point

The Indian healthcare system has evolved as a complex layered structure combining public, private and informal providers. India organises healthcare in three tiers based on the Bhore Committee (1946) model.

LevelFacilitiesPopulation coverage
Primary CareSub Centres, Primary Health Centres, Ayushman Arogya Mandirs3000 to 30000
Secondary CareCommunity Health Centres, Sub District Hospitals, District Hospitals80000 to 1.5 lakh
Tertiary CareMedical Colleges, AIIMS, Specialty HospitalsState and National level

What each level does

  • Primary:
    • First point of contact
    • Handles preventive care, immunisation, antenatal care and minor ailments
  • Secondary: specialised consultation and basic surgical care.
  • Tertiary: super-specialty treatment, largely concentrated in urban areas.

Major gaps in the architecture

  • India needs 30000 PHCs but has around 31000 with severe staff shortages.
  • CHCs face nearly 80% shortfall of specialists.
  • District hospitals are overburdened due to weak referral systems.
  • Tertiary care is urban-heavy: 67% doctors serve 34% urban population while 66% rural population has only 33% doctors.

Mixed public-private healthcare model

  • India follows a mixed model with both public and private providers operating in parallel.
  • 70% rural and 80% urban population uses private facilities (World Bank).
  • Private sector provides around 70% of outpatient and 60% of inpatient care.
  • Public sector remains the primary provider for maternal health, immunisation and basic preventive care.
  • Informal providers (RMPs) remain the first contact for many rural populations.
Way forward / key point

The mixed model creates choice but also creates inequity: the poor get pushed to overcrowded public facilities while paying out of pocket in private ones for quality care.

KEY Health Indicators: NFHS 6 Findings (2023-24)

Key point

The handout notes this section is data: do not memorise it by heart; get a general overview of what improved or worsened and use it to back arguments in comparison questions. NFHS 6 (released 2025) covered 6.79 lakh households, 7.16 lakh women and about 1.01 lakh men; results are provisional.

Demographic and household profile

  • Population aged 60 plus rose to 12.9% from 11.8% in NFHS 5, confirming rapid ageing.
  • Population below 15 years declined to 25.5%, showing demographic transition.
  • Households with health insurance jumped to 60.2% from 41%, reflecting PM-JAY expansion.
  • Female literacy (ever attended school) rose to 73.7% from 71.8%.
  • Households with improved drinking water at 96.5%.
  • Female members owning house or land at 18.8% up from 14%.

Marriage, fertility and family planning

  • TFR stable at 2.0 (Urban 1.6, Rural 2.1), confirming below-replacement fertility.
  • Women 20-24 married before 18 dropped to 20.1% from 23.3%, which is significant progress.
  • Adolescent pregnancy stable at 6.7%.
  • Use of any family planning method rose to 69.1% from 66.7%.
  • Modern method use dipped to 52.7% from 56.4% while traditional methods rose.
  • Unmet need for family planning fell to 8.5% from 9.4%.

Maternal and child health

  • Antenatal care visits (any) at 95.9% up from 92.6%.
  • First trimester antenatal check at 76.2% up from 70%.
  • Four plus ANC visits at 65.2% up from 58.5%.
  • Iron folic acid for 180 plus days rose to 37.8% from 26%.
  • Postnatal care within 2 days at 82.8%.
  • Full immunisation rose to 82.6% from 76.6%.
  • Rotavirus vaccine coverage jumped to 85.4% from 36.4%, a remarkable scale-up.

Nutrition status of children

  • Stunting declined to 29.3% from 35.5% (a major improvement).
  • Wasting at 19.0%, marginally down from 19.3%.
  • Underweight at 31.8%, down from 32.1%.
  • Severely wasted children dropped to 5.2% from 7.7%.
  • Exclusive breastfeeding under 6 months fell to 55.8% from 63.7%, which is worrying.
  • Children 6-23 months with adequate diet rose to 15.3% from 11%.

Adult nutrition and NCDs (the new challenge)

  • Women overweight or obese surged to 30.7% from 24%.
  • Men overweight or obese rose to 27.3% from 22.9%.
  • Women with high blood sugar at 17.8% up from 13.5%.
  • Men with high blood sugar at 20.9% up from 15.6%.
  • Women with elevated BP at 19.4%
  • Men at 22.1%

Women empowerment indicators

  • Internet use by women rose to 64.3% from 33.3% in NFHS 5.
  • Women with bank account at 89% up from 78.6%.
  • Women with mobile phone at 63.6% up from 53.9%.
  • Spousal violence dropped to 22.3% from 29.2%, a major improvement.
Way forward / key point

Key takeaway from NFHS 6: the dual transition. India is winning on undernutrition and infectious diseases but losing on lifestyle-related obesity, diabetes and hypertension. Anaemia continues to be a concern with no major improvement. Adult NCDs have emerged as the new health emergency.

National Health Accounts Estimates (NHA 2021-22)

Released by NHSRC; tracks total health expenditure in India

  • Total health expenditure as share of GDP around 3.8%.
  • Government health expenditure rose to 1.84% of GDP.
  • OOPE share declined to 39.4% of total health expenditure from 62.6% in 2014-15.
  • Social security expenditure on health rose to around 8.7%.
  • Per capita government health expenditure rose significantly.
Way forward / key point

The fall in OOPE share is a positive development driven by PM-JAY and CGHS expansion, but absolute OOPE in rupees continues to push nearly 4 crore Indians into poverty annually.

Health Insurance Coverage AND OOPE

Insurance penetration

  • 60.2% of households have at least one member covered by health insurance (NFHS 6) compared to 41% in NFHS 5.
  • PM-JAY covers 55 crore beneficiaries with Rs 5 lakh per family per year.
  • Missing middle of around 40 crore Indians lacks any financial protection (NITI Aayog Report).
  • 39.4% of health expenditure was still OOPE in 2021-22 against global average of 18%.
  • Around 80% of population lacks formal health insurance with adequate cover (FICCI 2022).
Way forward / key point

The biggest gap is in outpatient care, diagnostics and medicines, which are not covered by most insurance products yet contribute the bulk of OOPE. The sharp rise in household coverage to 60.2% in NFHS 6 is a significant policy success of PM-JAY and State schemes.

2 · Public Health System AND THE State · Marketisation · Missing Middle · Inter-state Variations · Safety OF Doctors · Patient Rights#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2024 (State role against marketisation), 2015 (can private sector bridge UHC gap) · Health chapter pp. 36-38. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Marketisation brings capacity and technology but also overcharging, cherry-picking, urban bias and catastrophic OOPE. The State's answer is strong primary care, mobile units, eSanjeevani, ASHAs, regulated PPPs, Clinical Establishments Act enforcement, standard treatment guidelines and 2.5% of GDP. The missing middle (about 40 crore) needs affordable insurance. Health spending varies sharply across States, and doctor safety and patient rights need balancing.

Marketisation OF Healthcare

Key point

Marketisation refers to the increasing dominance of market-driven private providers in healthcare delivery. While it has expanded access in cities and brought technology, it has also created serious affordability and equity concerns.

Public Versus Private Healthcare Debate PYQ 2024 PYQ 2015

Quote

PYQ 2024 (15 marks): In a crucial domain like the public healthcare system, the Indian State should play a vital role to contain the adverse impact of marketisation of the system. Suggest some measures through which the State can enhance the reach of public healthcare at the grassroots level.

Quote

PYQ 2015 (12.5 marks): Public health system has limitations in providing universal health coverage. Do you think that the private sector could help in bridging the gap. What other viable alternatives would you suggest.

Adverse impacts of marketisation

  • Overcharging and unnecessary procedures to maximise profit.
  • Unnecessary diagnostic tests and surgeries, as documented by multiple CAG reports.
  • Cherry-picking of profitable patients and procedures.
  • Urban bias: private hospitals concentrate where paying capacity is high.
  • Catastrophic OOPE pushing nearly 39 million Indians into poverty annually.
  • Brain drain of public sector doctors to private hospitals.
  • Lack of accountability: private clinics operate with weak regulation.

Strengths of private sector

  • High capacity and technology.
  • Specialty and super-specialty services.
  • Faster service delivery.
  • Innovation in delivery models.

Way forward (PYQ 2024)

  • Strengthen PHCs under NHM targeting 80% rural coverage by 2025.
  • Mobile health units for tribal and remote areas, as in Kerala's mobile vans.
  • Telemedicine expansion through eSanjeevani, which has facilitated over 22 crore consultations.
  • Train and empower ASHAs, who reach 10 lakh villages.
  • Public Private Partnerships with regulation and accountability.
  • Clinical Establishments Act enforcement and price regulation.
  • Standard Treatment Guidelines to prevent unnecessary procedures.
  • Increase public spending to 2.5% of GDP as mandated by NHP 2017.
Way forward / key point

Models like Kerala's robust health network, Tamil Nadu's Amma Clinics and Delhi's Mohalla Clinics show that strong public primary care is the most effective tool against marketisation.

Health Insurance FOR THE Missing Middle

NITI Aayog Report 2021 identified the missing middle problem

  • Nearly 40 crore Indians, mostly informal and self-employed workers, lack any health insurance.
  • They are above the PM-JAY poverty line but cannot afford private insurance.
  • High OOPE risk pushes many into debt and poverty.
  • Concentrated in the unorganised sector, agriculture and gig economy.

Suggestions

  • Affordable standardised insurance like Aarogya Sanjeevani.
  • Voluntary contribution with subsidised premiums for the poor.
  • Use of PM-JAY infrastructure for expanding coverage.
  • Risk pooling through SHGs, cooperatives and trade unions.
  • Tax incentives under Section 80D for self-employed.
Way forward / key point

The missing middle is the next frontier for India's universal health coverage agenda. The NFHS 6 figure of 60.2% household insurance coverage shows progress, but a third of households remain uncovered.

Public Health Investment AND Governance Challenges

Inter-State variations in health spending

  • Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Delhi spend significantly more on health and show better outcomes.
  • Bihar, UP, Jharkhand and MP have lowest per capita spending and worst indicators.
  • 15th Finance Commission recommended Rs 70051 crore of health grants to States.
  • State health spending varies from 4% to 9% of State budget.
  • The federal nature of health under the Seventh Schedule (State List) creates these variations. NHM as a Centrally Sponsored Scheme tries to bridge the gap but fund utilisation by poor States remains low.

Safety of healthcare professionals

  • The murder of a postgraduate doctor at RG Kar Medical College, Kolkata, in 2024 brought healthcare worker safety into national focus.
  • Scale: 75% of doctors in India face some form of violence in their career (IMA Survey).
  • Causes include patient deaths, poor doctor-patient communication, alcohol-related disputes and political pressure.
  • Most attacks happen in emergency wards and casualty departments.
  • Women healthcare workers face additional sexual harassment risk.

Existing legal framework

  • Epidemic Diseases (Amendment) Act, 2020 provides protection during pandemics with imprisonment up to 7 years.
  • State Acts in 20 plus States criminalise violence against doctors.
  • NMC Code of Ethics for hospital security.

Demands and reforms needed

  • A Central Healthcare Workers Protection Act beyond epidemics.
  • Mandatory security audits in all hospitals.
  • CCTV coverage, panic buttons and trained security.
  • Doctor-patient communication training.
  • Faster grievance redressal mechanisms.
  • Workplace harassment cells under the POSH Act.
  • The Supreme Court formed a National Task Force in 2024 to recommend reforms.

Patient Safety Rights Charter

  • The NHRC released a Charter of Patient Rights in 2018 listing 17 patient rights including right to information, right to second opinion, right to confidentiality and right to non-discrimination.
  • States are required to adopt and implement the charter
  • Clinical Establishments Act, 2010 enforces basic standards
  • Many States have not yet implemented the charter fully.
  • Need for patient grievance officers in every hospital.
  • Linkage with eHospital and ABDM for transparent records.
Way forward / key point

Patient rights need to be balanced with healthcare worker safety to create a culture of mutual respect and accountability.

3 · Primary Healthcare · ASHA · Ayushman Arogya Mandirs · SDG 3 · National Health Policy 2017#

In simple words
  • Primary care is the first, cheapest and most equitable level, backed by the Alma Ata (1978) and Astana (2018) declarations. ASHAs (10.4 lakh, launched 2005) and Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (12 services, CHOs) are the community level. SDG 3 targets MMR below 70 and U5MR below 25 by 2030; NHP 2017 set 2.5% of GDP for public health, still unmet at 1.8%.

Primary Healthcare

Key point

Primary healthcare is the first contact point and the most cost-effective level of care. The Alma Ata Declaration 1978 and the Astana Declaration 2018 affirm primary healthcare as the foundation of universal health coverage.

WHY Primary Healthcare IS Critical FOR Sustainable Development PYQ 2021

Quote

PYQ 2021 (10 marks): Besides being a moral imperative of a Welfare State, primary health structure is a necessary precondition for sustainable development. Analyse.

Six reasons

  • Cost-effective: primary care prevents costly tertiary interventions.
  • Equity: reaches the rural and urban poor.
  • Demographic dividend: a healthy workforce drives growth.
  • Economic productivity: healthy citizens are more productive.
  • Resilience to shocks: as seen during COVID-19.
  • Reduces OOPE by catching disease early.
Memory aid · CEDERR

Six reasons: Cost-effective, Equity, Demographic dividend, Economic productivity, Resilience, Reduces OOPE. Sentence (order is free): Care Ensures Development, Equity, Resilience, Recovery. (own mnemonic)

Key point

Srinath Reddy Committee (2011) recommended at least 70% of healthcare budget for primary care, aligning with WHO's 50% recommendation. The NFHS 6 jump in antenatal care visits and immunisation coverage shows the primary care expansion is working at the ground level.

ASHA Workers AND Frontline Workforce PYQ 2018

Quote

PYQ 2018 (10 marks): Appropriate local community level healthcare intervention is a prerequisite to achieve Health for All in India. Explain.

ASHA (Accredited Social Health Activist) workers are the backbone of community health

  • Launched in 2005 under NRHM.
  • 10.4 lakh ASHAs across rural India.
  • One ASHA per 1000 population.
  • Selected from the community and trained on basic health.
  • Receive performance-based incentives rather than a fixed salary.
  • Not salaried employees, a long-standing demand. Their work spans maternal health, immunisation, family planning, TB, malaria and now NCDs.

Recognition

  • WHO Global Health Leaders Award 2022 given to ASHA workers.
  • Critical role during COVID-19 vaccination and surveillance.

Challenges

  • Low and irregular incentives.
  • Lack of formal employee status.
  • Heavy workload without basic equipment.
  • Limited career progression.
Way forward / key point

Anganwadi Workers and ANMs complete the frontline health workforce. Strengthening them is the most cost-effective health intervention India can make.

Ayushman Arogya Mandirs AND HWCs PYQ 2021 PYQ 2018

Key point

Health and Wellness Centres (HWCs) were launched in 2018 as the first pillar of Ayushman Bharat. Renamed Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (AAMs) in 2023.

Key features

  • Target of 1.5 lakh AAMs by 2025 with around 1.7 lakh operational by 2025.
  • Provide 12 comprehensive primary healthcare services including maternal and child health, NCDs, mental health, eye care and elderly care.
  • Staffed by Community Health Officers (CHOs), who are mid-level providers.
  • 1.25 lakh CHOs deployed since 2018.
  • Free essential drugs and diagnostics.
  • Telemedicine linkage through eSanjeevani.

Health AND Sustainable Development Goals: SDG 3 AND Progress

Key point

The Sustainable Development Goals (2015 to 2030) include SDG 3 to ensure healthy lives and promote wellbeing for all at all ages. India is a signatory and the goals are integrated into the Indian planning process. (The 2013 PYQ asked about the earlier Millennium Development Goals that relate to health; SDG 3 is the successor framework.)

Key SDG 3 targets

  • MMR below 70 per 1 lakh by 2030.
  • U5MR below 25 per 1000 by 2030.
  • End AIDS, TB, malaria and neglected tropical diseases.
  • Universal Health Coverage by 2030.
  • Reduce NCD premature mortality by one third.

India's progress as per Economic Survey 2025-26

  • MMR reduced by 86% since 1990, exceeding the global average of 48%.
  • U5MR declined by 78% during 1990 to 2023.
  • NMR reduced by 70% during 1990 to 2023.
  • IMR dropped 37% from 40 (2013) to 25 (2023).
  • TB incidence declined by 21% from 237 (2015) to 187 (2024) per lakh, double the global decline of 12%.
Way forward / key point

India is on track for several SDG 3 targets but needs to accelerate on nutrition, mental health and NCDs.

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Health chapter pp. 39-41. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Ayushman Bharat (2018) has two pillars: AAMs and PM-JAY (Rs 5 lakh per family, 55 crore beneficiaries, expanded to all 70 plus). The ABDM (2021) builds a digital health backbone (ABHA IDs, HPR, HFR, UHI, HCX). Health equity means a fair chance at the highest level of health; inequity shows geographically, socially, economically, by gender and technologically.

Ayushman Bharat Pm-jay

Key point

Ayushman Bharat, launched in 2018, is one of the world's largest publicly funded health insurance programmes. Pillar 1: Health and Wellness Centres (now AAMs) for primary care. Pillar 2: Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY).

PM-JAY features

  • Health cover of Rs 5 lakh per family per year for secondary and tertiary care.
  • Covers 55 crore beneficiaries from the poorest 40% families.
  • No restriction on family size, age or gender.
  • All pre-existing conditions covered from day one.
  • Cashless treatment at empanelled hospitals.
  • 27000 plus empanelled hospitals across public and private sector.

Recent expansion

  • PM-JAY expanded to all citizens aged 70 plus with Rs 5 lakh top-up cover.
  • Will benefit 6 crore senior citizens.
  • This is the biggest expansion since launch.

Key achievements

  • 35.4 crore Ayushman cards issued.
  • 7.79 crore hospital admissions authorised.
  • 21% reduction in OOPE.
  • 49% of cards issued to women.
  • Annual net benefit of USD 26.1 million to district hospitals.
Way forward / key point

The NFHS 6 sharp rise in household health insurance coverage from 41% to 60.2% directly reflects the PM-JAY footprint expansion.

Challenges as per CAG report

  • Database errors with invalid beneficiary data.
  • Infrastructure shortcomings in empanelled hospitals.
  • Financial irregularities including fake claims.
  • Implementation delays.
  • Geographical disparity in utilisation.
Key point

The Expert Committee under V. K. Paul of NITI Aayog was constituted in 2024 to review PM-JAY and recommend reforms.

Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM)

Key point

ABDM, launched in 2021, aims to create a digital health infrastructure to integrate fragmented data and enable seamless service delivery.

Key components

  • Ayushman Bharat Health Account (ABHA): unique 14-digit health ID with around 79 crore accounts created.
  • Healthcare Professionals Registry (HPR) with around 6.57 lakh professionals registered.
  • Healthcare Facility Registry (HFR) with over 4 lakh facilities.
  • Unified Health Interface (UHI) for application interoperability.
  • Health Claim Exchange (HCX) for cashless claims.
  • Digital Health Incentive Scheme to encourage adoption.

Principles

  • Voluntary participation with opt-out option.
  • Free registration.
  • Privacy protection through consent-based architecture.
  • Federated architecture respecting State autonomy.

Challenges

  • Digital divide as rural and elderly cannot access (NFHS 6 shows female internet use jumped to 64.3%, which is encouraging).
  • Privacy concerns without a comprehensive data protection law.
  • Demand and skill mismatch in the digital health workforce.
  • Interoperability issues across State systems.
  • Inadequate primary care data to populate the system.
Way forward / key point

ABDM has the potential to transform Indian healthcare into a data-driven system but requires addressing these challenges with sensitivity.

Concept OF Health Equity IN India

Key point

Health equity, as defined by WHO, is achieved when every individual has a fair opportunity to attain their highest level of health regardless of social, economic or political circumstances.

Dimension of health inequityManifestation
GeographicalUrban India has 75% of healthcare professionals against 34% urban population
SocialSCs and STs face higher child mortality and lower immunisation
EconomicNearly 39 million pushed into poverty annually due to OOPE
GenderWomen face restricted access, especially in marginalised groups
TechnologicalDigital health divide favouring urban populations
Key point

NFHS 6 shows narrowing gender gap in some indicators but widening urban-rural gap in NCDs. Urban women obesity at 42.8% is sharply higher than rural at 25.5%.

Tribal health inequity

  • Tribal infant mortality higher than the national average.
  • Sickle cell disease disproportionately affects tribes.
  • Tribal Health Action Plan under MoHFW addresses tribal-specific issues.
  • Lack of trained personnel in tribal areas.

Strategies to achieve health equity

  • Strengthen primary healthcare with 70% health budget for primary care.
  • Inclusive insurance models covering the missing middle.
  • Tribal and rural-specific interventions like NHM differential financing.
  • Gender-sensitive health planning.
  • Bridge the digital health divide through assisted access points.
  • Address social determinants of health like water, sanitation, nutrition.

Successful models

  • Basthi Dawakhanas in Telangana for the urban poor.
  • Family Health Centres in Kerala.
  • Mohalla Clinics in Delhi.
  • Tamil Nadu Medical Services Corporation for procurement efficiency.
Way forward / key point

Health equity is not a slogan but the test of whether universal health coverage truly reaches everyone.

  1. Right to health under which Article and case? Article 21; Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal
  2. DPSPs on health? 39(e), 41, 42, 47, 48A
  3. Three-tier model based on? Bhore Committee 1946: primary (SC, PHC, AAM), secondary (CHC, SDH, DH), tertiary (medical colleges, AIIMS)
  4. Doctor distribution gap? 67% doctors serve 34% urban; 66% rural have 33% doctors
  5. Public vs private usage? 70% rural, 80% urban use private; private gives ~70% outpatient, 60% inpatient
  6. NFHS 6 TFR? 2.0 (urban 1.6, rural 2.1)
  7. NFHS 6 stunting? 29.3% (from 35.5%)
  8. NFHS 6 insurance coverage? 60.2% of households (from 41%)
  9. NFHS 6 dual transition? Undernutrition and infections falling; obesity, diabetes, hypertension rising
  10. Exclusive breastfeeding trend? Fell to 55.8% from 63.7%
  11. OOPE share now vs 2014-15? 39.4% vs 62.6% (global average 18%)
  12. How many pushed into poverty by OOPE? Nearly 4 crore annually
  13. Missing middle size? About 40 crore without financial protection (NITI Aayog)
  14. Define marketisation. Increasing dominance of market-driven private providers in healthcare delivery
  15. Seven adverse impacts of marketisation (name four). Overcharging; unnecessary tests; cherry-picking; urban bias; catastrophic OOPE; brain drain; weak accountability
  16. State measures (name five). PHCs under NHM; mobile units; eSanjeevani; ASHAs; regulated PPPs; Clinical Establishments Act; standard treatment guidelines; 2.5% GDP
  17. Models against marketisation? Kerala network, Tamil Nadu Amma Clinics, Delhi Mohalla Clinics
  18. Who identified the missing middle? NITI Aayog Report 2021: about 40 crore
  19. Insurance fixes for the missing middle? Aarogya Sanjeevani; subsidised voluntary premiums; PM-JAY infrastructure; SHGs/cooperatives/unions; Section 80D
  20. 15th FC health grants to States? Rs 70051 crore
  21. Why does health spending vary across States? Health is on the State List (Seventh Schedule)
  22. Doctors facing violence? 75% (IMA survey); RG Kar murder 2024
  23. Epidemic Diseases (Amendment) Act 2020? Protection during pandemics; up to 7 years imprisonment
  24. NHRC Charter of Patient Rights? 2018; 17 rights including information, second opinion, confidentiality, non-discrimination
  25. Declarations on primary health? Alma Ata 1978; Astana 2018
  26. Six reasons primary care matters? Cost-effective; equity; demographic dividend; productivity; resilience; reduces OOPE
  27. Srinath Reddy Committee 2011? At least 70% of health budget to primary care (WHO: 50%)
  28. ASHA facts? 2005, NRHM; 10.4 lakh; one per 1000; performance-based incentives, not salaried
  29. ASHA recognition? WHO Global Health Leaders Award 2022
  30. HWC to AAM? HWCs 2018 (first pillar of Ayushman Bharat); renamed Ayushman Arogya Mandirs 2023
  31. AAM features? 12 services; staffed by CHOs (1.25 lakh deployed); free drugs and diagnostics; eSanjeevani link
  32. SDG 3 targets? MMR below 70; U5MR below 25; end AIDS/TB/malaria; UHC; cut NCD premature mortality by one third
  33. India progress? MMR down 86% since 1990; U5MR down 78%; NMR down 70%; IMR 40 to 25; TB incidence down 21%
  34. NHP 2017 replaced? The 2002 policy
  35. NHP 2017 unmet target? 2.5% of GDP public health spending (actual 1.8%)
  36. NHP 2017 shifts? Selective to comprehensive; sectoral to integrated; input-based to outcome-based financing
  37. Ayushman Bharat: launch and two pillars? 2018; AAMs (primary care) and PM-JAY
  38. PM-JAY cover and reach? Rs 5 lakh per family per year; 55 crore beneficiaries (poorest 40%); 27000+ hospitals; cashless; pre-existing conditions from day one
  39. 70 plus expansion? All aged 70 plus with Rs 5 lakh top-up; about 6 crore seniors
  40. Key achievements? 35.4 crore cards; 7.79 crore admissions; 21% OOPE reduction; 49% cards to women
  41. CAG challenges for PM-JAY? Database errors; infrastructure gaps; fake claims; delays; geographical disparity
  42. Who reviews PM-JAY (2024)? Expert Committee under V. K. Paul (NITI Aayog)
  43. ABDM launched and components? 2021; ABHA (14-digit, ~79 crore), HPR, HFR, UHI, HCX, Digital Health Incentive Scheme
  44. ABDM principles? Voluntary, free, consent-based privacy, federated architecture
  45. WHO definition of health equity? Fair opportunity to attain highest level of health regardless of circumstances
  46. Five dimensions of health inequity? Geographical; social; economic; gender; technological
  47. Urban vs rural women obesity? 42.8% vs 25.5% (NFHS 6)
  48. Models of equity? Basthi Dawakhanas (Telangana); Family Health Centres (Kerala); Mohalla Clinics (Delhi); TN Medical Services Corporation
