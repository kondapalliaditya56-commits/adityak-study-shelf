1 · National Commission FOR Women (NCW) · Composition · Powers · Recent WORK · Constitutional Status · PMLA#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 19-23. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The NCW is a statutory body (1992, under the NCW Act 1990) that watches over women’s rights, inquires into violations and advises the government. It has civil-court powers during inquiries but its recommendations are only advisory. There is a long-running demand to give it constitutional status like the NCSC and NCST, for autonomy, binding force and parity. The Parivarik Mahila Lok Adalat gives women a fast, informal way to settle family disputes.

WHAT THE NCW IS

Introduction

  • Set up in 1992 under the National Commission for Women Act, 1990. It is the apex statutory body that monitors constitutional safeguards for women, investigates violations and advises the government on legal and policy reform.
  • It was set up to promote gender justice in line with India’s commitments under CEDAW (1979).
  • It acts as a watchdog and catalyst for women’s rights, empowerment and participation in governance.

Functions AND Powers

Functions (legal review, policy coordination, awareness, grievance redress)

  • 1. Review of constitutional and legal safeguards: checks whether constitutional provisions and laws protect women enough and recommends amendments.
  • 2. Investigation and inquiry: looks into specific complaints and suo motu cases of deprivation of women’s rights, non-implementation of protective laws, or failures of the administration.
  • 3. Advice to government: advises the Centre and States on policy, legal reform and effective implementation of women’s welfare programmes.
  • 4. Monitoring custodial and institutional facilities: inspects jails, remand homes and women’s shelters to ensure humane, lawful treatment.
  • 5. Research and documentation: studies on trafficking, workplace harassment, domestic violence and the declining sex ratio to support evidence-based policy.
  • 6. Public awareness and legal literacy: campaigns and workshops on women’s rights and remedies.
  • 7. Collaboration with NGOs and civil society.
  • 8. Intervention in court cases involving serious violations of women’s rights, especially where public interest is involved or legal aid is needed.

Powers (quasi-judicial: investigative, recommendatory, advisory)

  • Civil-court powers under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, while investigating: summon witnesses, examine them on oath, demand documents and receive affidavits.
  • Power to inquire suo motu or on petition, including non-enforcement of protective laws and maladministration.
  • Power to call for reports from any central or State department or agency on women’s rights and welfare.
  • Power to recommend prosecution, compensation or disciplinary action after an inquiry.
  • Power to visit custodial institutions such as jails, remand homes and shelters.
  • Power to hold public hearings and consultations on issues like sexual violence, trafficking and workplace discrimination.
  • Advisory and recommendatory power: recommendations are not legally binding, but its reports often guide reform, for example to the Dowry Prohibition Act and the Sexual Harassment at Workplace Act.

Recent Interventions (2020-2025)

The NCW has gone beyond its statutory functions to deal with cybercrime, workplace safety, digital awareness and gender-sensitive governance, a shift from redressal to empowerment.

  • Online harassment (2020-24): launched the Digital Shakti Campaign (Phase IV, 2022) with Meta and Cyber Peace Foundation to train women in online safety and digital literacy. Over 3 lakh women sensitised on cyber hygiene and legal recourse.
  • Economic empowerment: the She Is a Changemaker programme (2021) to build leadership among women in politics, PRIs and social sectors. Skill and entrepreneurship workshops for over 12,000 grassroots women leaders.
  • Legal awareness and counselling: expanded the One Stop Centres network and strengthened the 24x7 Women Helpline (181) for counselling, legal aid and rehabilitation. Launched the “We Think Digital” campaign for cyber-legal literacy among college students.
  • High-profile cases: suo motu cognisance of the Hathras gang-rape (2020), Manipur ethnic violence (2023) and the Kallakurichi school suicide case (2022), issuing notices and ensuring time-bound reporting and victim assistance.
  • Law reform: recommended amendments to the Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, pushed gender-budgeting audits and monitored implementation of POSH guidelines in workplaces.
  • Gender sensitisation: with the Bureau of Police Research and Development (BPR&D) trained over 30,000 police personnel on gender sensitivity and victim handling.
  • Marginalised women: initiatives for trafficking survivors, migrant women and transgender women, with health outreach and livelihood schemes (2023).

Demand FOR Constitutional Status FOR THE NCW

The idea: since 1992 the NCW has been statutory. There is a consistent demand to give it constitutional status like the NCSC (Art 338) and NCST (Art 338A), to strengthen its authority and independence.

  • Why it is demanded
  • Limited enforcement: its recommendations are advisory, so governments and police often do not comply.
  • Executive dependence: appointment and funding are controlled by the Ministry of Women and Child Development, hurting autonomy and credibility.
  • Rising gender-based violence: growing crimes against women, online abuse and systemic discrimination need a stronger body with direct accountability.
  • International commitments: constitutional status would match global standards such as the UN Paris Principles (1993) on independent national human rights institutions.
  • Parity:
    • SCs and STs already have constitutional commissions
    • Similar status for women would improve institutional equality

Expected benefits

  • More autonomy: independent appointments, secure tenure and money charged to the Consolidated Fund of India.
  • Binding recommendations on women’s rights, workplace safety and law enforcement.
  • Continuity: protection from political or administrative interference across governments.
  • Stronger policy impact on domestic violence, cyber safety and reproductive rights.
  • Better global standing in compliance with CEDAW.

Challenges the NCW faces today

  • Limited autonomy and perceived political bias. As a statutory body it is less independent than constitutional bodies like the EC or CAG. Allegations of bias came up during the Manipur crisis in 2023.
  • Inadequate resources. A Parliamentary Committee on Empowerment of Women (2022) said staff were too few for all-India coverage.
  • High crime despite oversight. NCRB 2022 recorded over 4.45 lakh cases of crimes against women
  • Conviction rates remain low, so deterrence is weak
  • Marginalised women neglected. Concerns of tribal, Dalit and migrant women seldom reach the NCW
  • Women sugarcane cutters in Maharashtra stayed under-reported despite petitions
  • Advisory nature. Governments can ignore or delay action
  • Many reports (for example after the Kathua rape case, 2018) saw slow State responses
  • Overlapping jurisdictions with State Commissions, the NHRC and Lokpal
  • The Committee on Empowerment of Women has repeatedly asked for a clearer delineation of powers
  • Slow grievance redress. It gets 20,000+ complaints a year (2022 data), many resolved late. There is no real-time, technology-based monitoring.

Way forward

  • Constitutional status for independence, as suggested by the Justice Verma Committee (2013) after the Delhi gang-rape.
  • Transparent, inclusive appointments including women activists and academics, not only government nominees. The Supreme Court in the Anjali Bhardwaj case (2019) stressed transparency in appointments.
  • Reach marginalised women: Dalit, tribal, migrant and rural.
  • More staff, funds and digital infrastructure including tech-based grievance redress.
  • Binding power to enforce key recommendations.
  • New challenges: gig-economy work, cyber abuse and workplace harassment (NCW surveys in 2023 highlight gig women’s vulnerabilities).
  • Stronger collaboration with civil society and State commissions.

Parivarik Mahila LOK Adalat (PMLA)

Launched by the NCW with State Legal Services Authorities as an informal, conciliatory forum for women-related disputes, mainly family and matrimonial ones. It works under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, aiming at speedy, friendly and low-cost justice.

  • It handles domestic violence, maintenance, divorce, dowry, property disputes and custody. Proceedings are women-friendly and non-adversarial.
  • The stress is on mediation, empathy and rehabilitation rather than punishment, so women can seek justice without fear or delay.
  • It works with NGO support in resolving family disputes.

2 · Ncpcr · State Commissions FOR Child Rights (scpcr) · Children’s Courts#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 23-30. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The NCPCR (2007, under the CPCR Act 2005) is the national watchdog for children’s rights; each State may set up a similar SCPCR, and the Act also provides Children’s Courts for speedy, child-friendly trials. It has civil-court powers and monitors the RTE, POCSO and JJ laws. Its advice is not binding, POCSO pendency is high and digital threats are growing, so reform aims at binding powers, fast-track courts and better tech.

National Commission FOR Protection OF Child Rights (ncpcr)

Introduction

  • A statutory body set up in 2007 under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights (CPCR) Act, 2005. It functions under the Ministry of Women and Child Development (MWCD) and is the apex national institution to protect, promote and monitor children’s rights.
  • Its moral and legal base is the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC), 1989, which India ratified in 1992. The UNCRC defines a child as any human being below 18.
  • Its mandate covers review of child-related laws, investigation of violations, monitoring of laws like the RTE Act (2009), POCSO Act (2012) and JJ Act (2015), and promoting awareness and accountability.

Objectives

  • Protection and redress: take notice of cases where child rights are denied or violated and ensure redress by inquiry or legal action.
  • Policy and law review: examine laws, policies and programmes concerning children for alignment with the Constitution and international obligations.
  • Child-centric governance: integrate child-rights concerns in all government decisions and planning.
  • Monitor implementation of the Right to Education, Juvenile Justice and POCSO laws.
  • Research and awareness: studies, reports and outreach to make the public sensitive to child protection.
  • In essence it is a watchdog that keeps the State accountable to every child’s rights to survival, development, protection and participation.

Initiatives to meet its mandate

  • POCSO e-Box: an online complaint box where children can directly report sexual abuse, confidentially, with quick redress and mandatory action under the POCSO Act. Used widely since 2016 so school children can complain without fear.
  • Samvardhan programme: vulnerability mapping to combat child trafficking, tied to district mechanisms and anti-trafficking units. Piloted in high-trafficking districts in Bihar and West Bengal.
  • MASI App (Monitoring App for Seamless Inspection): a digital tool for real-time inspection of Child Care Institutions (CCIs) to ensure compliance with JJ Act norms. It flagged serious gaps in shelter homes after the Muzaffarpur case.
  • GHAR-GO Portal (Go Home and Re-Unite): a digital platform to track and help restore or repatriate children in institutional care to their families or guardians. Used during COVID-19 to restore stranded migrant children.

Functions OF THE Ncpcr

Core functions under the CPCR Act, 2005

  • 1. Legal and institutional review of safeguards for child rights and recommending effective implementation (example: reviewing JJ Act implementation against UNCRC).
  • 2. Monitoring and reporting: annual and special reports to the Central Government, tabled in Parliament with an Action Taken Memorandum.
  • 3. Inquiry and investigation into violations suo motu, on complaint or on court direction. It may summon witnesses, gather evidence and recommend prosecution or remedial action.
  • 4. Addressing vulnerabilities: displacement, trafficking, child labour, conflict, HIV/AIDS, disasters and domestic violence.
  • 5. Children in special circumstances:
    • Those in conflict with law, orphans, children of prisoners and children in institutions
    • Recommending rehabilitation
  • 6. Inspection and oversight: visiting juvenile homes, observation homes and child-care institutions and directing remedial action.
  • 7. Research and policy advocacy.
  • 8. Awareness and education of the public, parents, teachers and institutions (example: Child Rights Week initiatives).
  • 9. Engagement with civil society: NGOs, child welfare organisations and community groups.
  • 10. A limit: it cannot inquire into any matter already pending before a State Commission (SCPCR) or another statutory body, to avoid overlap.

Extra functions under other child laws

  • RTE Act, 2009:
    • Review safeguards for the right to education
    • Inquire into complaints of denial of free and compulsory education
    • Use civil-court powers in such inquiries
  • POCSO Act, 2012:
    • Monitor implementation nationwide
    • Run awareness drives and training for law enforcement and the judiciary
    • Include POCSO work in its annual report to Parliament
  • JJ Act, 2015:
    • Oversee implementation including Child Welfare Committees (CWCs) and Juvenile Justice Boards (JJBs)
    • Recommend improvements in rehabilitation, adoption and foster care
    • Report compliance in its annual submission

Powers AND Working Procedure

Inquiry process (Sections 14-17)

  • 1. How it takes notice: suo motu, on a written complaint from an individual, NGO or child-rights organisation, or on court direction.
  • 2. Calling for reports:
    • It can seek information or a report from the Central or State Government or a subordinate authority within a fixed time. If reports are delayed it can go ahead on its own
    • If satisfied that adequate action has been taken it may close the case and inform the complainant
  • 3. On-spot inquiry: field visits, recording statements and inspecting institutions such as juvenile homes, schools or shelters.
  • 4. Full investigation: if a preliminary review shows a prima facie violation, it starts a full inquiry, directly or through an authorised team.

Civil-court powers (Section 14)

  • Summon and examine witnesses on oath
  • Demand discovery and production of documents
  • Receive evidence on affidavits
  • Requisition public records from any court or office
  • Issue summons for examination of persons or papers
  • Authorise search and inspection of any premises or institution housing children
  • These give it procedural authority and independence from executive interference.

Actions after an inquiry (Sections 15-17)

  • Recommend that the government begin prosecution or disciplinary action, and recommend compensation, rehabilitation or damages for the victim.
  • Approach higher courts: move the Supreme Court or High Court for writs, orders or directions when legal enforcement is needed.
  • Interim relief: suggest rescue, shelter or medical care at once.
  • Follow-up:
    • The government must submit an Action Taken Report within a set period
    • The NCPCR can publish findings and monitor compliance
  • Reports: annual and special reports go to the Central and State Governments and are laid before Parliament and State Legislatures with memoranda on actions taken and reasons for non-implementation.

Working procedure

  • Sittings are held regularly with a minimum quorum
  • Decisions are by majority
  • A Secretariat and Investigation Division with legal, research and field officers (on deputation or direct recruitment).
  • Coordination with SCPCRs for jurisdictional clarity and information exchange.
  • Child-friendly procedure: confidential hearings, anonymity of child victims and non-adversarial questioning, as per POCSO guidelines (2012).
  • Technology: since 2020 online complaint portals and digital tracking through the Baal Swaraj e-monitoring system.

Challenges faced by the NCPCR

  • Non-binding recommendations: limits enforceability. The 2021 child-labour advisories were ignored by several States.
  • Judicial pendency: over 1.6 lakh POCSO cases pending, which weakens deterrence and faith in child protection.
  • Resource and staff deficit: too few people to inspect child care institutions. MASI found gaps in CCIs, but follow-up is poor.
  • Perceived political influence:
    • Perceived bias weakens trust in its independence
    • Its response to the Manipur violence drew criticism for delay
  • Online child sexual exploitation:
    • NCRB 2022 reports rising cybercrimes against children including grooming
    • The COVID-19 lockdown worsened online abuse and child pornography
  • Digital trafficking networks: traffickers use gaming apps and the dark web to recruit children (UNODC 2021).
  • Rural awareness gap: NFHS-5 shows only 36% of rural households know about child rights laws, so reporting is slow.

Way forward

  • Binding powers:
    • Amend the CPCR Act so that recommendations bind
    • The NHRC’s stronger powers could be a model
  • Fast-track mechanisms: special benches for POCSO and JJ Act cases (the Delhi High Court’s 2023 directive shows the need for speedier hearings).
  • AI and digital tools to detect child sexual abuse material online (the EU’s INHOPE network is an example of global cooperation).
  • Cyber-safety education in the NCERT curriculum (MeitY’s 2022 child-safety toolkit is a baseline).
  • More money and staff, including psychologists for child care institutions.
  • Independent appointments: add child-rights activists and legal experts to the selection panel (the Supreme Court’s 2019 CIC judgment stressed transparent appointments).
  • Partner with tech firms: mandatory child-safety audits for gaming and social media companies (the UK Online Safety Bill 2023 offers lessons).

State Commission FOR Protection OF Child Rights (scpcr)

Each State Government can set up an SCPCR by notification in the Official Gazette under the CPCR Act, 2005 (Section 17). It is the State-level counterpart of the NCPCR, giving decentralised monitoring, investigation and promotion of child rights in line with national laws and UN conventions. It ensures State laws, policies and administration match child welfare principles in the Constitution, the UNCRC and national child-protection laws.

Functions of the SCPCR (like the NCPCR, but limited to the State)

  • Examine State laws, welfare schemes and administrative mechanisms and recommend reform to the State Government.
  • Inquire into violations (suo motu or on complaint) and recommend prosecution or departmental action.
  • Inspect juvenile homes, observation homes and shelter homes run by the State or NGOs, ensuring JJ Act standards.
  • Examine problems of children affected by communal violence, trafficking, disasters, HIV/AIDS or domestic abuse and recommend protection.
  • Promote awareness and research on State-specific issues like dropout rates, trafficking or malnutrition.
  • Coordinate with the NCPCR and submit reports for nationwide consistency.
  • Advise the State on rules, policies or schemes under the RTE, JJ and POCSO Acts.

Powers and working procedure

  • Civil-court powers under the CPC, 1908: summon and examine witnesses on oath, require production of documents, receive affidavits, requisition public records, issue summons for witnesses and documents.
  • Same inquiry mechanism as the NCPCR: receive complaints, call for government reports, do field inspections.
  • After the inquiry it may recommend prosecution, relief to victims, or file writ petitions before the State High Court or the Supreme Court.
  • It reports annually and specially to the State Government, which lays the report before the State Legislature with an action-taken memorandum
  • The State informs the Commission of action taken within a set time

Children’s Courts

Why: to ensure speedy trials and child-friendly justice, the CPCR Act, 2005 requires Children’s Courts at State or district level. They deal with offences against children or violations of child rights in a way that respects the child’s age, psychology and dignity. They complement the NCPCR and SCPCRs.

Functions and role

  • Speedy trial of child abuse, trafficking, exploitation or denial of statutory rights, in time-bound proceedings.
  • Child-friendly procedure:
    • In-camera hearings to protect privacy
    • No direct cross-examination of child victims
    • Psychological support or a child counsellor
    • Minimal exposure to the accused during testimony
  • Enforcement: offences under the POCSO Act 2012, the Juvenile Justice Act 2015 and any other law on child welfare or exploitation.
  • Coordination with CWCs and JJBs for holistic rehabilitation and social reintegration after the trial.
  • Restorative orientation: correction, rehabilitation and victim-centred outcomes over punishment, reflecting the best interest of the child principle from the UNCRC (1989).

3 · Minority Rights · Common AND Separate Domain · National Commission FOR Minorities (NCM)#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 30-34. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The Constitution never defines “minority”, but protects them in two ways: rights that every citizen has (the common domain) and rights meant only for minorities (the separate domain, Articles 29, 30, 347, 350A and 350B). The Minorities Commission began in 1978 and became the statutory NCM in 1992. It evaluates progress, monitors safeguards, looks into complaints and recommends to governments, but only for the six notified religious communities.

Constitutional Rights AND Safeguards FOR Minorities

Starting point: the Constitution does not define “minority”, but it recognises communities based on religion or language who may need special protection to keep their identity and have equality. It gives a detailed framework of rights, which shows India’s commitment to pluralism and inclusion.

  • These provisions divide into two domains: the Common Domain (rights open to all citizens, ensuring equality, liberty and justice) and the Separate Domain (protections meant exclusively for minorities to guard their identity, language, culture and educational institutions).
  • This dual framework keeps equality before law universal while protecting minority identities within a secular democracy. The Preamble, which declares India a secular State and guarantees liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship, reflects this spirit.

I. Common Domain (rights OF ALL Citizens)

(A) Directive Principles (Part IV) — not justiciable, but fundamental to governance under Article 37

  • Article 38(2): the State must try to eliminate inequalities in status, facilities and opportunities among individuals and groups living in different areas or in different occupations.
  • Article 46: the State must promote with special care the educational and economic interests of weaker sections, in addition to SCs and STs.
  • Minority groups often fall within weaker sections, so these Articles give them targeted State attention.

(B) Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A)

  • The duty to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood among all Indians, rising above religious, linguistic, regional or sectional diversities.
  • The duty to value and preserve the rich heritage of India’s composite culture.
  • These strengthen social cohesion and mutual respect among the groups that make up the nation.

Way forward / key point

India’s pluralistic vision: the Constitution treats India as multi-religious, multi-lingual and multi-cultural. The framers saw the Indian nation not as a mere collection of individuals but as a two-tier commonwealth: of citizens, and of the cultural, religious and linguistic groups they belong to. Each group, like each citizen, has equal Fundamental Rights and equal Fundamental Duties, so diversity enriches the national identity instead of dividing it.

Protecting weaker sections in a plural society: weakness can come from small numbers or from social, economic or educational disadvantage. So there are special provisions for religious and linguistic minorities, and also for SCs, STs and backward classes. This reaffirms India’s model of secularism and inclusive development, where equality goes hand in hand with diversity and every group, majority or minority, gets both protection and participation.

National Commission FOR Minorities (NCM): History

  1. 1978
    The Ministry of Home Affairs Resolution proposed a permanent institution. Despite many safeguards, minorities felt unequal and discriminated against; the Government said the enforcement of safeguards deserved “the highest importance”. The Minorities Commission was set up in 1978 under the Home Ministry.
  2. 1984
    Transferred to the newly created Ministry of Welfare, a shift from law and order to welfare and development.
  3. 1988
    By a Welfare Ministry Resolution, linguistic minorities were excluded from its jurisdiction, so the Commission looked only at religious minorities.
  4. 1992
    The National Commission for Minorities Act, 1992 gave statutory status. The first statutory Commission was constituted on 17 May 1993.
  5. 1993 notification
    Five religious communities notified as minorities under Section 2(c): Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists and Zoroastrians (Parsis).
  6. 2014
    Jains added by another notification.

Functions (Section 9(1) of the NCM Act, 1992)

  • Evaluating development progress: assessing how minorities fare under Union and State programmes.
  • Monitoring safeguards: overseeing implementation of constitutional and legal safeguards, including Articles 29 and 30 on cultural and educational rights.
  • Advice and recommendations to the Centre and States on better implementation and improving minorities’ condition.
  • Addressing complaints of deprivation of rights and safeguards and taking them up with the relevant authorities.
  • Research and studies on the causes and impact of discrimination against minorities and ways to remove it.
  • Studying socio-economic and educational conditions of minority communities.
  • Policy suggestions to the Union and States for minority welfare.
  • Reports: periodic and special reports to the Central Government on problems minorities face.
  • Other matters referred by the Central Government.

Complaints and redress

  • Common complaints: police excesses or atrocities, service matters in public employment, issues of minority educational institutions, and encroachment or disputes over religious properties.
  • On a complaint, the NCM asks for detailed reports from the Union or State authorities, reviews the findings and recommends redress. So it acts as a statutory watchdog.

4 · National Investigation Agency (NIA) · Functions · Process · Special Courts · Criticism · Ankush Vipan Kapoor (2024)#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 34-38. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The NIA was created by the NIA Act, 2008 after the 26/11 attacks as a central agency to investigate terrorism and other scheduled offences across India without needing State consent. Cases go to special courts and appeals lie to the High Court. Critics point to federalism worries, UAPA detentions, thin staff and low conviction in some cases. In Ankush Vipan Kapoor (2024) the Supreme Court said the NIA may also probe linked non-scheduled offences, after informing the Centre.

WHAT THE NIA IS

Introduction

  • Established through the NIA Act, 2008, after the 26/11 Mumbai terror attacks, to create a specialised federal body for counter-terrorism.
  • Headquartered in New Delhi, it can investigate scheduled offences without State consent, giving a swift and centralised response to terrorism, cross-border crime and offences that affect national security.
  • It works under the Ministry of Home Affairs and is headed by a Director General (IPS rank).

Functions AND Powers

Eight functions, with examples

  • 1. Counter-terror investigation across States without State permission, so the anti-terror response is uniform. Example: the Pulwama attack (2019) probe led to a high conviction rate.
  • 2. Jurisdiction outside India: widened in 2019 to probe offences against Indians abroad. Example: Indian assets targeted in Kabul (2021) were monitored under the wider powers.
  • 3. Exclusive authority on scheduled offences under UAPA, the WMD Act, the Explosives Act and IPC Chapter VI. Example: a fake currency racket under IPC Sections 489A to 489E.
  • 4. Concurrent jurisdiction with State police: it can take over cases from State police within 15 days of referral. Example: the Kerala gold smuggling case moved from State police in 2020.
  • 5. Civil-court-like powers during inquiry: summon witnesses, demand records, attach property. Example: asset attachment in ISIS recruitment cases under UAPA.
  • 6. Specialised cells for terror financing: the TFFC Cell monitors terror financing and counterfeit circulation. Example: Rs 2000 counterfeit notes traced to Pakistan-based networks.
  • 7. International collaboration with Interpol, FBI and Europol on global terror cases. Example: Dawood Ibrahim’s networks flagged through FATF information sharing.
  • 8. Supervision through Special Courts for speedy trial. Example: the Ajmer Dargah blast trial concluded in an NIA Special Court.

HOW THE NIA Works (process)

Eight steps

  • 1. Initial reporting: local police send the FIR to the State and then to the Centre within 24 hours. Example: inputs registered by the NCRB on arms smuggling are shared with the NIA.
  • 2. Central screening within 15 days: the Centre decides whether it is a scheduled offence fit for the NIA. Example: the 2022 Ludhiana blast was quickly handed over.
  • 3. Transfer from State: once the NIA takes over, State police stop all investigation. Example: the West Bengal coal scam probe moved from CID to NIA.
  • 4. Preliminary inquiry and registration: the DG approves the FIR and an inquiry wing begins fact-finding.
  • 5. Investigation with nationwide powers: NIA officers act as station house officers across India. Example: nationwide raids against the PFI in September 2022.
  • 6. Trial in Special Courts: before Sessions Judges appointed on the High Court Chief Justice’s recommendation. Example: the Samjhauta Express blast case in Panchkula.
  • 7. Appeals to the High Court within 30 days: a two-judge bench is required to dispose of them within three months.
  • 8. State coordination during trial: States must give cooperation, logistics and witnesses.

Special Courts under the NIA Act

  • Notified by the Central Government to try only scheduled offences investigated by the agency.
  • Presided over by a Sessions Judge or Additional Sessions Judge nominated by the Chief Justice of the High Court.
  • They can sit outside their normal seat if needed, which gives flexibility for sensitive trials.
  • Appeals go directly to the High Court
  • Timelines are stressed to avoid delays in terror trials

Challenges AND Criticisms

Eight criticisms

  • Federalism: States say the Centre overrides their policing powers. The Kerala CM criticised the NIA takeover of the gold smuggling case.
  • Human rights: allegations of coercive interrogation and prolonged detention under UAPA. Amnesty International flagged arrests after the Bhima Koregaon case.
  • Manpower: only about 1,200 personnel against a rising caseload of 300+ cases a year. A CAG report (2021) flagged staff shortage in regional offices.
  • Overreach and misuse:
    • Perceived selective targeting of NGOs and minority-linked groups
    • Greenpeace India flagged misuse of foreign-funding scrutiny
  • Low convictions in some high-profile cases: the Samjhauta Express blast verdict acquitted all accused (2019), which weakens deterrence and credibility.
  • Technology lag:
    • No AI-driven monitoring of the dark net and encrypted networks
    • Europol reports highlight India’s vulnerability to cyber-radicalisation
  • Coordination gaps with State agencies: delayed intelligence sharing reduces efficiency (the 2016 Pathankot attack probe saw jurisdictional disputes).
  • Perceived political bias:
    • Criticism that NIA cases often match the ruling party’s narrative
    • Opposition flagged selective raids against NGOs in 2021

Way forward

  • Federal cooperation: a Centre-State coordination council for security issues, on the Inter-State Council model.
  • More staff and regional presence: recruitment and new offices in the North-East and border regions (a 2022 parliamentary panel recommended more branch offices).
  • Digital and cyber capability: AI, blockchain and forensic tools for dark-net monitoring (lessons from the US FBI’s Joint Terrorism Task Force).
  • Human rights compliance:
    • NHRC oversight in custodial interrogation and trials
    • UN guidelines on counter-terror measures
  • Efficient special courts: digital courts for faster terror trials (e-Courts Phase III, 2023).
  • Accountability and audits: annual CAG audit of the NIA for budget and impartiality.
  • Global cooperation: stronger MLATs and Interpol red notices for fugitives abroad (cooperation with the UAE sped up extradition of gangsters).
  • Awareness and citizen participation: reporting channels, helplines and civic awareness.

Supreme Court: Ankush Vipan Kapoor v. NIA (2024)

The Supreme Court widened the scope of NIA investigation by reading Section 8 of the NIA Act holistically.

  • 1. Linked offences: the NIA may investigate non-scheduled offences connected with a scheduled offence. Example: if terror financing involves IPC fraud charges, the NIA can probe both.
  • 2. Central approval: the agency must inform the Central Government before widening the investigation. This gives executive oversight and prevents arbitrary extension of powers.
  • 3. Joint investigation: linked offences must be investigated together with the scheduled offence, to avoid duplication, keep consistency and strengthen prosecution.
  • 4. Significance: the ruling widens the NIA’s jurisdiction and reinforces India’s anti-terror architecture. It helps tackle hybrid crimes where terror links overlap with economic and cyber offences.
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: the NIA is India’s federal answer to terrorism and national security threats. Its wide jurisdiction and international reach make it indispensable, but federal friction, resource gaps and digital challenges need fixing. With transparency, modern technology and Centre-State synergy it can become a global-standard counter-terror agency.
