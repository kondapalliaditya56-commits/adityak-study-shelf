1 · National Commission FOR Women · Powers · Constitutional Status Debate#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 19-23. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • NCW (1992, NCW Act 1990) is a statutory, advisory body with civil-court powers. There is a demand for constitutional status (like Arts 338, 338A) for autonomy and binding force, supported by the Verma Committee (2013).

Structure AND Functions

Basics

  • Set up in 1992 under the NCW Act, 1990
  • Apex statutory body for gender justice, in line with CEDAW (1979)
AspectProvision
CompositionChairperson (eminent woman) + 5 Members (one from SC/ST) + Member-Secretary
Appointed byCentral Government
Tenure3 years; reappointable
Removalinsolvency, conviction, incapacity; may resign to the Centre
HQNew Delhi; regional offices as needed

Functions

  • review constitutional and legal safeguards
  • investigate complaints and suo motu cases
  • advise governments
  • inspect jails, remand homes, shelters
  • research (trafficking, workplace harassment, domestic violence, sex ratio)
  • legal literacy and awareness
  • support NGOs
  • intervene in court cases

Powers

  • civil court powers (CPC 1908)
  • inquire suo motu or on petition
  • call for reports from departments
  • recommend prosecution, compensation, discipline
  • visit custodial places
  • hold public hearings
  • advisory only, yet shaped reforms to the Dowry Prohibition Act and POSH law

Recent Initiatives (2020-2025)

Initiatives

  • Digital Shakti (Phase IV, 2022; with Meta, CyberPeace): over 3 lakh women sensitised
  • She Is a Changemaker (2021): over 12,000 grassroots leaders
  • One Stop Centres, 181 helpline
  • "We Think Digital" campaign
  • suo motu: Hathras (2020), Manipur (2023), Kallakurichi school suicide (2022)
  • Indecent Representation of Women Act amendments
  • Gender budgeting audits
  • POSH monitoring
  • trained over 30,000 police with BPR&D
  • trafficking survivors, migrant and transgender women (2023)

Demand FOR Constitutional Status

Reasons

  • limited enforcement
  • dependence on Ministry of WCD
  • rising gender violence
  • Paris Principles
  • parity with Arts 338, 338A bodies

Expected benefits

  • Autonomy
  • Funds from Consolidated Fund
  • binding directives
  • continuity
  • stronger policy impact
  • global standing (CEDAW)

Challenges

  • Statutory status limits independence
  • Political bias allegations in Manipur 2023
  • thin staff (2022 Parliamentary Committee)
  • NCRB 2022:
    • Over 4.45 lakh crimes against women
    • Low conviction
  • Dalit, tribal, migrant women neglected (e.g. Maharashtra sugarcane cutters)
  • advisory (post-Kathua 2018 responses slow)
  • overlap with State Commissions, NHRC
  • 20,000+ complaints a year (2022), slow redress

Way forward

  • constitutional status (suggested by the Justice Verma Committee, 2013)
  • transparent, inclusive appointments (Anjali Bhardwaj, 2019)
  • reach marginalised women
  • staff, funds, digital grievance systems
  • binding recommendations
  • gig economy, cyber abuse, harassment (2023 surveys)
  • partner NGOs and SHRCs
Way forward / key point

Parivarik Mahila Lok Adalat: NCW with State Legal Services Authorities; informal, conciliatory forum under the Legal Services Authorities Act 1987 for family disputes (domestic violence, maintenance, divorce, dowry, property, custody); mediation and rehabilitation.

2 · Ncpcr · Scpcr · Children's Courts#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 23-30. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • NCPCR (2007, CPCR Act 2005) is the apex child-rights watchdog, with civil-court powers and a say in RTE, POCSO and JJ Acts. States have SCPCRs; Children's Courts (Sec 25) give child-friendly trials. Advice is not binding.

Ncpcr

Basics

  • statutory, set up 2007 under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005, under the Ministry of WCD
  • UNCRC 1989, ratified by India 1992
  • Child = below 18
  • monitors RTE 2009, POCSO 2012, JJ Act 2015

Objectives

  • protection and redressal
  • review laws and policies
  • child-centric governance
  • monitor key laws
  • research and awareness

Initiatives

  • POCSO e-Box for reporting abuse
  • Samvardhan: vulnerability mapping against trafficking (Bihar, West Bengal pilots)
  • MASI app: inspect Child Care Institutions (post-Muzaffarpur)
  • GHAR-GO portal: restoration of children to families (Covid)
  • Baal Swaraj e-monitoring (since 2020)
AspectProvision
CompositionChairperson + 6 Members (at least two women)
Member fieldseducation; child health/welfare; juvenile justice; ending child labour; child psychology/sociology; child laws
Appointed byCentral Government; 3-member selection committee chaired by the Union WCD Minister
Tenure3 years; renewable once (max two terms)
AgeChairperson up to 65; Members up to 60
Removalinsolvency, incapacity, moral turpitude, misuse, absence from 3 consecutive meetings

Functions

  • review safeguards
  • annual and special reports (tabled with action-taken memorandum)
  • inquire suo motu, on complaint or court direction
  • study hindering factors (trafficking, labour, conflict, HIV/AIDS)
  • children in special circumstances
  • inspect juvenile homes
  • research, awareness, NGOs
  • limit: cannot inquire into matters before an SCPCR or another statutory body

Powers (Sections 14-17)

  • civil court powers: summon, documents, affidavits, public records, search/inspect premises
  • Recommend prosecution, compensation
  • Approach HC/SC
  • Interim relief
  • ATR
  • child-friendly procedures per POCSO (confidential, anonymous)

Challenges

  • non-binding (2021 child labour advisories ignored)
  • 1.6 lakh+ POCSO cases pending
  • staff deficit
  • perceived political influence (Manipur)
  • online exploitation (NCRB 2022)
  • digital trafficking (gaming apps, dark web; UNODC 2021)
  • NFHS-5: only 36% rural households know child rights laws

Way forward

  • amend the Act for binding powers
  • special POCSO/JJ benches
  • AI tools for CSAM
  • cyber safety in NCERT curricula
  • more staff and psychologists
  • diverse selection panel
  • child-safety audits by tech firms

Scpcr AND Children's Courts

AspectSCPCR
BasisSection 17 of the 2005 Act; State notification
CompositionChairperson + 6 Members (2 women)
Appointed byState Government; selection committee chaired by the Minister in charge of children
Tenure3 years, max two terms; ages 65 and 60
Functionsreview state laws, inquire, inspect child institutions, special circumstances, research, coordinate with NCPCR, advise on rules
Powerscivil court powers; may recommend prosecution or file writs; annual report to State Legislature

Children's Courts (Section 25)

  • State Government with HC Chief Justice concurrence notifies a Sessions Court as Children's Court or sets up a separate court per district
  • try offences against children (JJ Act, POCSO)
  • Public Prosecutor/Special PP with 7 years
  • child-friendly: in-camera, no direct cross-examination, counsellor, little contact with accused
  • Work with CWCs and JJBs
  • Restorative, "best interest of the child"

3 · Minority Rights · Common AND Separate Domain · National Commission FOR Minorities#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 30-33. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The Constitution never defines "minority". It gives minorities rights in a common domain (shared by all citizens) and a separate domain (Arts 29, 30, 347, 350A, 350B). NCM (statutory, 1992) covers six notified religious communities.

TWO Domains OF Minority Rights

Common domain

  • DPSP:
    • 38(2) reduce inequalities
    • 46 weaker sections
  • Duties:
    • Harmony
    • Composite culture
  • FR:
    • 14
    • 15(1),(2)
    • 15(4)
    • 16(1),(2)
    • 16(4)
    • 25(1)
    • 26
    • 27
    • 28

Separate domain

  • 29(1) conserve language, script, culture
  • 29(2) no denial of admission to State-aided institutions
  • 30(1) minorities may establish and administer institutions
  • 30(2) no discrimination in aid
  • 347 recognition of a language
  • 350A mother tongue at primary stage
  • 350B Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities
  • Explanation I to Art 25: Sikhs and kirpan
Value addition

Vision: India as a two-tier commonwealth of citizens and cultural, religious, linguistic groups; special provisions also for SCs, STs and Backward Classes; equality harmonised with diversity.

National Commission FOR Minorities

  1. 1978
    Ministry of Home Affairs Resolution; Minorities Commission set up under MHA
  2. 1984
    moved to the new Ministry of Welfare
  3. 1988
    Welfare Ministry Resolution: linguistic minorities excluded
  4. 1992
    NCM Act gave statutory status
  5. 17 May 1993
    first statutory Commission constituted
  6. 2014
    Jains added to the notified list

The first notification (1993) named five: Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Zoroastrians (Parsis). Section 2(c): a minority is a community notified by the Central Government. Census 2011 basis. The handout's table omits the Parsi row but the total is shown as 19.3%.

Memory aid · MCSBZJ

Six notified: Muslims · Christians · Sikhs · Buddhists · Zoroastrians · Jains. (own mnemonic)

AspectNCM
CompositionChairperson, Vice-Chairperson and 5 Members
Representationat least 5 incl. Chairperson from notified minorities
Appointed byCentral Government (Ministry of Minority Affairs)
Tenure3 years; removal by Centre for insolvency, moral turpitude, unsound mind, misuse, absence from 3 meetings

Functions (Section 9(1))

  • evaluate development progress
  • monitor safeguards incl. Arts 29-30
  • recommend to governments
  • examine complaints
  • research on discrimination
  • socio-economic and educational studies
  • Policy suggestions
  • Periodic and special reports
  • other matters referred by the Centre

Complaints

  • Police excesses
  • Service matters
  • Minority institutions
  • Religious property encroachments
  • NCM seeks reports, then recommends redress (no binding force)

4 · National Investigation Agency · Scheduled Offences · Challenges · 2024 Ruling#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 34-37. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • NIA (NIA Act 2008, after 26/11) probes scheduled terror-linked offences across India without state consent and, since 2019, abroad. Federalism, UAPA misuse, staff gaps and low conviction are the debates. Ankush Vipan Kapoor (2024) allows linked non-scheduled offences with Central intimation.

Basics AND Powers

AspectDetail
BasisNIA Act, 2008 after the 26/11 Mumbai attacks; 2019 amendment widened jurisdiction
ControlMinistry of Home Affairs; HQ New Delhi; branches Mumbai, Hyderabad, Kolkata, Guwahati, Kochi, Lucknow, Raipur, Jammu
HeadDirector General (IPS)
Jurisdictionconcurrent across India; extended outside India (2019)
Special CourtsSessions Judges nominated with High Court Chief Justice; appeal to High Court, target 3 months

Functions and powers

  • investigate without State permission (e.g. Pulwama 2019)
  • offences against Indians abroad
  • scheduled offences: UAPA, WMD Act, Explosives, IPC Ch. VI
  • take over cases from state police (e.g. Kerala gold smuggling, 2020)
  • Civil-court-like powers
  • Attach property
  • TFFC cell for terror financing and fake currency
  • work with Interpol, FBI, Europol
  • trial in Special Courts (e.g. Ajmer Dargah blast)
Scheduled categoryExamples
Terrorism and securityUAPA 1967; Atomic Energy Act 1962; WMD Act 2005
Explosives and armsExplosive Substances Act 1908; Anti-Hijacking Act 2016; prohibited arms
Counterfeit currencyIPC 489A-489E
Offences against StateIPC Chapter VI, 121-130
2019 additionshuman trafficking, cyber-terrorism, counterfeit currency, prohibited arms
InternationalSAARC Convention Act 1993; offences abroad involving Indians

Working Process

  1. 1
    local police report to State, then Centre within 24 hours
  2. 2
    Centre screens within 15 days whether it is a scheduled offence
  3. 3
    once NIA takes over, state police stop investigating
  4. 4
    DG approves FIR; preliminary inquiry
  5. 5
    officers act as station house officers across India
  6. 6
    trial in Special Court (e.g. Samjhauta Express blast case, Panchkula)
  7. 7
    appeal to High Court within 30 days; two-judge bench to decide within 3 months
  8. 8
    states must cooperate with logistics and witnesses

Criticisms AND WAY Forward

Challenges

  • federalism (Kerala CM on gold case)
  • human rights, long UAPA detention (Bhima Koregaon)
  • staff: about 1,200 personnel vs 300+ cases a year (CAG 2021)
  • overreach, selective targeting
  • low conviction (Samjhauta acquittals 2019)
  • cyber and darknet gaps
  • coordination gaps (Pathankot 2016)
  • perceived political bias

Way forward

  • Centre-State coordination council (like Inter-State Council)
  • more staff, offices in Northeast
  • AI, blockchain, forensic tools
  • NHRC oversight of interrogations
  • digital Special Courts (e-Courts Phase III)
  • annual CAG audit
  • MLATs, Interpol notices
  • awareness and helplines
Note

Ankush Vipan Kapoor v. NIA (2024): Section 8 of the NIA Act read holistically. NIA may investigate non-scheduled offences linked to a scheduled offence (e.g. IPC fraud with terror financing), must inform the Central Government before widening the probe, and must investigate linked offences jointly. This widens jurisdiction for hybrid terror-economic-cyber crimes.
