1 · Nature OF Fundamental Rights · Article 12 · BCCI#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights handout pp. 4-7. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Fundamental Rights sit in Part III. Most work against the State, so Article 12 defines who the State is. Courts use tests (statute, control, agency, public function) to decide if a body is State. BCCI is the standing example: not State, but still reachable by writ for public duty.

WHAT Fundamental Rights ARE

Meaning

  • rights found in Part III of the Constitution
  • rights outside Part III are not Fundamental Rights:
    • The Right to Vote
    • The statutory Right to Information (RTI Act, 2005)
  • but the right to know and receive information is recognised as part of Article 19(1)(a)
CategoryArticles
Right to EqualityArts 14-18
Right to FreedomArts 19-22
Right against ExploitationArts 23-24
Right to Freedom of ReligionArts 25-28
Cultural and Educational RightsArts 29-30
Constitutional RemediesArt 32 (Supreme Court); Art 226 gives remedies through High Courts
Memory aid · EFERCC

Categories in order: Equality (14-18) · Freedom (19-22) · Exploitation (23-24) · Religion (25-28) · Cultural-educational (29-30) · Constitutional remedies (32). (own mnemonic)

Nature OF Fundamental Rights

Negative rights

  • most rights stop the State from acting, e.g. denying equality

Vertical application

  • most rights protect individuals against the State

Positive rights

  • some require the State to act, e.g. education under Art 21A

Horizontal application

  • Some also bind private persons, e.g. Art 17 (untouchability)
  • Other violations by private persons are met through civil and criminal law

Other features

  • Justiciable: enforced by courts through Art 32 (SC) and Art 226 (HCs)
  • Citizens vs all persons:
    • Some only for citizens (Art 19)
    • Others for all persons including foreigners (Arts 14 and 21)
  • Not absolute: subject to reasonable restrictions on constitutional grounds

Emergency and special cases

  • Art 358: Art 19 protections are affected during a National Emergency on grounds of war or external aggression
  • Art 359:
    • President may suspend the right to move courts for specified rights
    • Arts 20 and 21 enforcement cannot be suspended
  • Art 33: Parliament may restrict or remove some rights of Armed Forces, public-order forces, intelligence and specified telecom personnel for discipline. Example: Police Forces (Restriction of Rights) Act, 1966

Self-executing vs needing a law

  • most rights are directly enforceable without a law
  • some need a law:
    • Art 17 → Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955
    • Art 21A → RTE Act, 2009
    • Art 23 → laws against trafficking and forced labour
  • Art 35 gives Parliament law-making power to implement specified rights

Article 12: Definition OF State

Value addition

Art 12 defines State for Part III. Since most rights run vertically against the State, we must know who the State is.

State includes

  • Government and Parliament of India
  • Government and Legislature of each State
  • Local authorities (rural and urban)
  • Other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India

Why "other authorities" is the debate

  • Wording is vague
  • State functions are dynamic and are increasingly outsourced to bodies acting on its behalf
  • question: can citizens claim Fundamental Rights against these bodies too? The SC has given tests

Judicial tests

  • set up by a statute or executive order (e.g. RBI)
  • substantial financial and administrative control by government
  • works as an agency or instrumentality of the State
  • performs sovereign or public functions (defence, taxation, law and order)
Memory aid · SFAP

Four tests: Statute-created · Financial and administrative control · Agency of State · Public or sovereign function. (own mnemonic)

Note

Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. IICB (2002): the tests are not rigid or isolated. Overall question: is the body financially, functionally and administratively dominated or controlled by government? Control must be deep and pervasive; mere regulatory control is not enough.

Is the judiciary "State"?

  • Art 12 does not mention the judiciary
  • judicial functions:
    • Courts protect rights
    • Decisions are challenged by appeal or review, not under Art 12
  • administrative functions (staff recruitment and management):
    • Judiciary can be treated as State
    • Such acts can be challenged for violating rights

CASE Study: BCCI

Facts and Zee Telefilms (2005)

  • BCCI is a private society registered under the Tamil Nadu Societies Registration Act, yet has a near monopoly over Indian cricket and selects the national team
  • Zee Telefilms v. Union of India (2005):
    • Constitution Bench held BCCI is not State: not created by statute
    • No deep, pervasive government control
    • No government financial support
    • Functions important but not sovereign
  • yet the Court separated being State (Art 12) from performing a public function: a public duty can attract writ jurisdiction of the High Court under Art 226 even if the body is not State
BCCI is State (argument)BCCI is not State (argument)
monopoly control over a national sportnot created by statute
public functions affecting careersno pervasive government control
represents India in international bodiesfinancially independent, mostly private funds
decisions affect Fundamental RightsAjay Hasia and Pradeep Kumar Biswas tests not fully met
Way forward / key point

Law Commission 275th Report (2018) recommended bringing the BCCI under the RTI Act because of its public functions. The debate is really about fitting the modern administrative State into Art 12.

National Sports Governance Act, 2025 (handout; verify details)

  • Presidential assent 18 August 2025
  • A statutory framework replacing the non-statutory National Sports Development Code, 2011
  • National Sports Board (NSB) recognises and regulates National Sports Federations, with power to suspend or cancel recognition for governance failures
  • National Sports Tribunal for sports disputes
  • recognised bodies must set up Ethics, Dispute Resolution and Athletes Committees and codes protecting athletes, especially women and minors
  • aims at Olympic and Paralympic Charter alignment: India's 2036 Summer Olympics aspiration

RTI link and concerns

  • RTI applies if a body is substantially financed by government, so financially independent BCCI is not automatically a public authority
  • performing public functions does not automatically make a body State (Art 12) or a public authority (RTI)
  • concerns:
    • Over-centralisation in the NSB (government role in appointments)
    • Conflict of a domestic regulator with the autonomy of FIFA and ICC

2 · Affirmative Action · THE CASE FOR AND Against Reservation#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights handout pp. 7-10. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Affirmative action opens elite positions to groups that were kept out. Ambedkar wanted objective constitutional measures, not waiting for a change of heart. Know the eight arguments for, and the objections with their replies (injustice, merit, caste not class, creamy layer, mismatch).

Meaning AND TWO Approaches

Affirmative Action

  • Broader and more refined term than reservation
  • Became prominent when US President John F. Kennedy used it in an executive order in 1961 for equal opportunity
  • set of anti-discriminatory measures giving access to preferred positions (IITs, IIMs, IAS, IPS) to groups who would otherwise be excluded or unrepresented
  • These positions were historically monopolised by upper sections (gender, caste, class)
  • The State tries to alter the social composition of elites

Gandhian (spiritual) approach

  • Ashwini Deshpande calls it the Evangelical Approach
  • moral regeneration of upper castes through philanthropic upliftment

Ambedkar's objective approach

  • secular, not spiritual
  • Rejected waiting for a change of heart
  • We cannot wait years for moral regeneration
  • needs objective measures, e.g. constitutional provisions for SCs and STs

Arguments IN Favour

Eight arguments for

  • Historical injustice:
    • Compensation for centuries of untouchability and deprivation
    • USA (racial injustice), Australia (Aboriginal communities)
  • Division of labourers, not labour:
    • Work assigned by accident of birth
    • Link between caste and job weaker among upper castes, but lower castes (Dalits, manual scavenging) still face it
  • Class and caste coincide:
    • Lower castes face poverty plus social isolation
    • Dalits also face landlessness
  • Untouchability persists in new forms: separate utensils for domestic help, shoes outside the kitchen, trouble renting a home, honour killings over inter-caste marriage, caste preference in matrimonial ads
  • Labour-market dualism: Ashwini Deshpande on unequal outcomes. Thorat and Attewell (2007): applications with Hindu non-Dalit, Hindu Dalit and Muslim identities, same qualifications, different outcomes
  • Bridging inequality:
    • Development alone does not close the gap
    • Affirmative action bridges equality in law and equality in fact
  • Benefits society:
    • Not only the individual
    • Promotes the weakest and allows full use of the talent pool
Memory aid · HDC-UL-BB

For: Historical injustice · Division of labourers · Caste-class overlap · Untouchability persists · Labour-market dualism · Bridging law-fact gap · Benefits society. (own mnemonic)

Way forward / key point

Answer frame for Mains: state the objection, concede what is valid, reply with evidence, then give the reform (targeting, support), not abolition.

3 · Beyond Quota · Better Targeting · Creamy Layer · Sub-classification#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights handout pp. 10-14. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Quota alone cannot close gaps, so add support (land, jobs, handholding, diversity index). Then fix targeting: revise lists, apply the creamy layer, sub-classify OBCs (Rohini) and SCs (Davinder Singh 2024). Rohit Nathan (2026) says parental salary alone cannot decide creamy layer.

Quota PLUS Policy

Idea (Satish Deshpande)

  • Quota Plus: reservation alone cannot solve all socio-economic disadvantage

Limits of quota

  • urban vs rural gap:
    • Most backward classes, including Dalits, live in rural India with poor schooling
    • Quotas in engineering and medical colleges help those already able to qualify
  • 50% ceiling set by the SC (subject to later developments)
  • Tamil Nadu has historically gone up to 69%
  • Raising percentages alone does not remove structural disadvantage

Additional measures

  • land reforms (many Dalits are landless labourers)
  • non-farm employment generation
  • setting up MSMEs
  • handholding of beneficiaries: academic and institutional support in professional colleges
  • private sector Diversity Index on gender, community, caste
Memory aid · HELM-D

Quota Plus: Handholding · Employment off-farm · Land reform · MSMEs · Diversity index. (own mnemonic)

Diversity Index (illustrative)Class
0 - 0.33Low diversity
0.33 - 0.66Medium diversity
0.66 - 1.00High diversity

Use of the index

  • Centre and States could weigh diversity in public procurement and outsourcing
  • Discussed in the UPA period
  • Idea is private-sector diversity without relying only on compulsory reservation

Long-term objective

  • Art 334:
    • Original reservation of seats for SCs and STs in Lok Sabha and State Assemblies for ten years
    • This is political, not jobs or education
  • Extended by amendments
  • 104th Amendment Act, 2019 extended it to 25 January 2030
  • handout proposal:
    • Phased reduction of dependence on quotas once equal opportunity is reached
    • Review at three levels: promotion quotas, job quotas, education quotas. This is a policy proposal, not a constitutional requirement

Better Targeting

Three tools

  • revise backward-class lists through the constitutional process: add new groups, remove those that no longer qualify
  • Creamy layer to exclude advanced members
  • Criteria to be reviewed regularly
  • sub-classification within OBCs: a few communities take a disproportionate share

Justice G. Rohini Commission (2017)

  • appointed under Art 340 to study unequal distribution of benefits among OBCs in the Central List and suggest sub-categorisation within the 27% quota
  • reported illustrative split into four groups of 2%, 6%, 9%, 10%: reported proposals, not implemented
  • Report submitted to the President on 31 July 2023
  • Submission does not mean implementation

Creamy Layer

Meaning and scope

  • stops relatively advanced members of backward classes from cornering benefits
  • Central framework applies to OBC reservation in jobs and admissions
  • Income threshold ₹8 lakh per annum, with detailed Income/Wealth Test conditions
  • Salary and agricultural income treated separately
  • SC/ST:
    • Jarnail Singh (2018) discussed the principle for promotions
    • But the Centre and most States have no general OBC-style exclusion for SC/ST, and it is not generally applied to initial appointments
Note

Union of India v. Rohit Nathan (2026) (handout spells it Rohith; verify): Bench of Justices P. S. Narasimha and R. Mahadevan on UPSC candidates whose parents worked in PSUs, banks, private sector.

What the SC held

  • Parental salary alone cannot decide creamy layer
  • The status and category of the parent's post matter
  • 1993 Office Memorandum (based on Indra Sawhney 1992) is the foundation
  • The 2004 clarificatory letter is invalid to the extent it changed the 1993 criteria (a clarification cannot add substantive conditions)
  • Under the 1993 OM, salary and agricultural income are excluded from the Income/Wealth Test
  • Parental service status still counts
  • Art 14: treating PSU and private-sector employees' children differently from similarly placed government employees without valid basis violates equality
  • creamy layer is rooted in substantive equality (Arts 14, 15, 16), not mere administrative policy
  • Art 142 used for relief: supernumerary posts for eligible candidates wrongly denied non-creamy-layer benefit, with a time-bound process

Critique

  • test may become too income-dependent though affirmative action targets social discrimination
  • Separate treatment of agricultural income lets prosperous farming families pass the test
  • Benefits may pass across generations to the better-off
  • but the framework also uses parental status and other criteria, not only income

Sub-classification OF SCs: Davinder Singh (2024)

State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024)

  • seven-judge Constitution Bench, 6-1: States may sub-classify within Scheduled Castes for fairer distribution of benefits
  • recognises that inequality exists among SC communities themselves

Conditions

  • must rest on empirical data
  • cannot be used to include or exclude communities from the Presidential SC List (Art 341)
  • subject to judicial review
  • cannot give the entire benefit to one sub-group arbitrarily
Memory aid · JEMS

Davinder Singh limits: Judicial review · Empirical data · Must not alter Art 341 list · Sub-group cannot take everything. (own mnemonic)

Value addition

Opinions of individual judges on a creamy layer for SCs are not a universal rule; keep them distinct from an administrative or statutory exclusion.

4 · Dominant Caste Demands · Maratha · EWS · Effectiveness#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights handout pp. 14-17. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Backwardness must be shown by current objective data, not past victimhood or votes. Jats were rejected in Ram Singh (2015); the Maratha quota went 16% to 13/12% to struck down (2021), then a 2024 Act is pending. EWS 10% was upheld 3-2 in 2022.

Dominant-caste Demands: JAT

Background

  • Centre and States have discretion in listing communities as OBC, within constitutional safeguards and judicial review
  • the Jati-Varna link is clear at the top and bottom of the hierarchy but weaker among middle castes, so backwardness is contentious
  • 2014:
    • On the eve of Lok Sabha elections the UPA decided to include Jats in the Central OBC list in certain States
    • Critics saw electoral motive
    • Rejected by the SC in Ram Singh (2015)

Ram Singh v. Union of India (2015): principles

  • Historical injustice alone cannot identify OBCs
  • Present social and educational conditions must count
  • continuous evolution of backward classes: e.g. recognition of transgender persons in NALSA (2014)
  • Self-perception cannot be the sole basis
  • Need objective, contemporary data (no single time period fixed)
  • Questioned the adequacy of material behind the Jat decision
  • Need for reliable caste-based data

Vertical discrimination

  • against groups lower in the traditional hierarchy
  • answered by Arts 15(4) and 16(4) (affirmative action in education and jobs)

Horizontal discrimination

  • cuts across castes: women, transgender persons
  • Answered by Arts 15(1) and 16(2)
  • Horizontal reservation cuts across vertical categories where provided
  • also needs attitude and infrastructure change, not quotas alone

Maratha Reservation: Timeline

  1. 2014
    Maharashtra announces 16% for Marathas and a proposed 5% for Muslims in education and jobs; legal challenges over backwardness, religion and the ceiling
  2. 2018
    SEBC Act, 2018: 16% for Marathas in education and jobs; challenged in the Bombay High Court
  3. 2019
    Bombay HC broadly upheld but cut it to 13% in jobs and 12% in education
  4. 2021
    Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil: SC struck down the 2018 Act; no extraordinary circumstances to cross the 50% ceiling; reaffirmed Indra Sawhney (1992)
  5. 2024
    new SEBC Act: 10% for Marathas, additional to existing quotas; challenged on the ceiling. Handout: as of October 2026 still before the Bombay HC (verify)
Memory aid · SEBC-16-13-10

Maratha numbers: 16 (2018 Act) → 13/12 (HC 2019) → struck (SC 2021) → 10 (2024 Act). (own mnemonic)

EWS Reservation

Constitutional basis

  • 103rd Amendment Act, 2019 added Arts 15(6) and 16(6): 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections, subject to conditions

Criticisms

  • breaks the 50% ceiling of Indra Sawhney
  • Art 16(4) tests are backwardness and inadequate representation
  • EWS may not meet them
  • purely economic criterion: earlier cases said social backwardness cannot rest on economics alone
  • A separate vertical category though poverty cuts across communities
  • Excludes poor persons in SC, ST, OBC groups
Note

Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022): five-judge bench upheld the amendment 3-2; economic criteria valid; exclusion of SC/ST/OBC upheld. The ceiling and economics points are criticisms debated, not the legal position that invalidates EWS.

Effectiveness OF Reservation

Gains (supporters)

  • education gaps between SC/ST and general population narrowed
  • more representation in services and institutions
  • rise of a Dalit middle class

Continuing challenges

  • unequal access to quality education
  • concentration of benefits
  • inadequate representation at higher levels
  • judge by representation and broader socio-economic progress
