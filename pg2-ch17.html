1 · Federalism IN India · Features · Debate · Evolution · Forms OF Federalism#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Centre State Relations handout pp. 3-7. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Federalism means powers are divided by the Constitution between the Centre and the States, and neither can take the other's share away at will. India is a "holding together" federation: a large country that gave power to regions to keep unity. It has real federal features and strong unitary features, so scholars call it quasi-federal, cooperative or sui generis. In practice it has moved through centralised, regional, judicially corrected, coalition and cooperative-competitive phases.

WHAT IS Federalism?

Meaning

  • Federalism is a political system in which powers are constitutionally divided between a Central Government and regional governments. Both levels get their authority from the Constitution and use power within their own spheres.
  • Merely having regional governments does not make a system federal. A system is truly federal when regional governments have constitutionally guaranteed powers that the Centre cannot take away at will.
BasisComing Together FederationHolding Together Federation
OriginIndependent political units unite to form a federationA larger political entity distributes power among regional units
ObjectiveMutual security and common interestsAccommodate regional diversity while keeping national unity
Distribution of powersConstituent units generally enjoy stronger constitutional autonomyThe Centre generally enjoys greater powers
ExamplesUSA, Switzerland, AustraliaIndia, Spain, Belgium
Value addition

India's position: India follows the holding together model, combining regional autonomy with a strong constitutional Union.

Federal Features OF THE Indian Constitution

Despite the strong Centre, several provisions make India a federation:

  • 1. Dual polity: the Constitution sets up Union and State governments, each with constitutionally assigned powers. States are not merely administrative agents of the Union.
  • 2. Written Constitution: clearly defines the powers, functions and limits of both levels.
  • 3. Division of powers: the Seventh Schedule distributes subjects among the Union List, State List and Concurrent List.
  • 4. Supremacy of the Constitution:
    • Both Union and State governments must act within it
    • Neither has unlimited legislative power
  • 5. Rigid amendment procedure: under Article 368, amendments touching specified federal provisions need ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.
  • 6. Independent judiciary: the Supreme Court settles constitutional disputes between Union and States under Article 131, subject to constitutional conditions.
  • 7. Bicameralism: the Rajya Sabha represents States and Union Territories in Parliament and has special powers under Articles 249 and 312.

Unitary Features OF THE Indian Constitution

The Constitution also gives the Centre a dominant position:

  • 1. Strong Centre: the Union List holds subjects of national importance, and Parliament has residuary powers under Article 248.
  • 2. Single Constitution and single citizenship (no separate State constitutions or State citizenship, unlike some federations).
  • 3. Parliament's power over States (Article 3): Parliament can alter State boundaries, names and areas without the concerned State's consent.
  • 4. Union supremacy over the Concurrent List (Article 254): a Union law ordinarily prevails over a conflicting State law, subject to exceptions.
  • 5. Emergency provisions: in a National Emergency (Article 352) the Union gets extra legislative and executive powers over States.
  • 6. President's Rule (Article 356): the Union can take over State administration when constitutional machinery fails, subject to parliamentary and judicial safeguards.
  • 7. Governor's office:
    • Appointed by the President (Article 155)
    • May reserve State Bills for the President under Article 200
  • 8. Integrated institutions: a single judiciary, All-India Services, Election Commission and CAG give uniformity across the country.
  • 9. Fiscal centralisation: the Union controls important revenue sources while States depend on tax devolution and Central grants.
Note

Why these centralising features? They were designed to protect national unity, administrative coordination and constitutional stability, especially during political or security crises (the Constituent Assembly was working in the shadow of Partition).

Nature OF Indian Federalism: THE Debate

Views of scholars

  • K. C. Wheare: "quasi-federal". He described India's Constitution as federal in structure but with a strong unitary bias.
  • Granville Austin: "cooperative federalism". He stressed that the Centre and States must work together to reach national development and constitutional goals.
  • Sui generis means "of its own kind". India's federal system does not fit fully into the traditional federal or unitary models. It combines constitutional autonomy for States with a Centre strong enough to keep national unity.

"India has a federal Constitution but a strong-Centre government" — arguments for the statement:

  • Constitutional centralisation: residuary powers, emergency provisions and Union supremacy on the Concurrent List favour the Centre.
  • Political centralisation: national parties and centralised party leadership can reduce State governments' autonomy.
  • Financial dependence: States rely heavily on Central transfers and Centrally Sponsored Schemes.
  • Administrative control: Governors, All-India Services and Union directions can strengthen Central influence over State administration.

Counterarguments

  • Constitutional autonomy: States have independent legislative and executive powers in their own sphere.
  • Judicial protection: in S. R. Bommai (1994) the Supreme Court recognised federalism as part of the Basic Structure and restricted arbitrary use of Article 356.
  • Regional political assertion: regional parties and coalition politics have raised the bargaining power of States.
  • Shared institutions: the GST Council, Finance Commission and Inter-State Council provide ways to cooperate and negotiate.
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: India is neither purely federal nor purely unitary. It is a federal system with a strong Centre, designed to combine national integration with regional autonomy.

Evolution OF Indian Federalism

Indian federalism has never been static. Its working has changed with political developments, economic reforms, regional movements and court decisions.

  1. 1950-1967: Centralised federalism
    One-party dominance: Congress governed the Centre and most States, so there was little political confrontation. Planning Commission (1950): centralised planning and resource allocation strengthened Union influence. Article 356 was used against State governments, including the Communist government in Kerala in 1959. Nature: the Centre dominated; States had limited bargaining power.
  2. 1967-1980: Regionalism and friction
    1967 elections: non-Congress governments came to power in several States. Regional parties such as the DMK grew and demanded autonomy. The Rajamannar Committee (1969) was set up by the Tamil Nadu government to study Centre-State relations. The Emergency (1975-77) showed how far the Constitution can centralise power. Nature: the old consensus weakened and State autonomy became a major demand.
  3. 1980s-1990s: Assertive States, judicial correction
    Regional movements in Punjab, Assam, Andhra Pradesh and other States. The Sarkaria Commission (1983) recommended more consultation, restraint in using Article 356 and stronger cooperative institutions. In S. R. Bommai (1994) the Supreme Court made President's Rule subject to judicial review and affirmed federalism as basic structure. Nature: States became more assertive and the judiciary built safeguards against arbitrary central intervention.
  4. 1990s-2014: Coalitions and economic federalism
    Coalition governments: regional parties became partners at the Union level and gained bargaining power. 1991 liberalisation: States began competing for investment and shaping their own economic development. 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) strengthened Panchayats and Municipalities. The Inter-State Council (1990) was set up on the Sarkaria Commission's advice. Nature: political bargaining and economic competition increased the role of States.
  5. 2014 onwards: Cooperative and competitive federalism
    NITI Aayog (2015) replaced the Planning Commission. GST Council (2017) enabled shared decisions on indirect taxes under Article 279A. States compete for investment and governance rankings. Fiscal tensions continue over GST compensation, cesses, surcharges and Centrally Sponsored Schemes. After 2024, greater dependence on coalition partners at the Union level has renewed the relevance of regional bargaining. Nature: cooperation, competition and bargaining together, while the Centre's constitutional position stays strong.

Different Forms OF Indian Federalism

India's federalism does not follow a single model. Several overlapping forms describe how it works.

1. Asymmetric federalism

  • Meaning: different States or regions have different constitutional arrangements to suit their history, geography and culture.
  • Special provisions: Articles 371 to 371J give special arrangements for certain States. The Sixth Schedule gives autonomous arrangements for specified tribal areas in the North-East.
  • Significance: it accommodates regional diversity without weakening national unity (details in the Inter-State chapter).

2. Bargaining federalism (associated with W. H. Morris-Jones)

  • Meaning: continuous negotiation between the Centre and States over resources, powers and policies.
  • Political bargaining: regional parties negotiate with the Union on policy priorities and State-specific demands.
  • Fiscal bargaining: States seek greater tax devolution, grants and financial flexibility.
  • Institutional bargaining:
    • Earlier through the Planning Commission and National Development Council
    • Today through the GST Council and other forums
  • Significance: it lets States influence national decisions, but too much political bargaining can weaken consistent policymaking.

3. Cooperative federalism

  • Meaning: collaboration and partnership between the Centre and States to reach common national goals.
  • Mechanisms:
    • GST Council (shared decisions on GST)
    • NITI Aayog Governing Council (PM and Chief Ministers consult)
    • Finance Commission (recommends financial transfers)
    • Inter-State Council (coordination)
    • Joint implementation of programmes such as MGNREGA and the Right to Education Act
  • It works best when institutions create space for dialogue, fiscal transfers reduce dependence, and legislation shares responsibility.
  • Challenges:
    • Strong Union powers limit State autonomy
    • Different parties ruling at the Centre and States can sharpen confrontation
    • Coordination bodies meet rarely and have little authority
    • Conditional grants reduce States' freedom to set priorities
    • Centralised party leadership can influence State decisions

4. Competitive federalism

  • Meaning: States compete to attract investment, resources and economic opportunity by improving governance and infrastructure.
  • Instruments:
    • Ease of Doing Business reforms
    • NITI Aayog indices (State Health Index, School Education Quality Index, SDG India Index, India Innovation Index)
    • Investment summits such as Vibrant Gujarat and Magnetic Maharashtra
  • Significance: encourages policy innovation and administrative efficiency, better infrastructure and service delivery, and helps States respond to globalisation.
  • Challenges:
    • Poorer States lack resources to compete with developed States
    • Investment concentrates in already developed regions
    • Excess rivalry can weaken cooperation and create regional tensions
  • Way forward: competitive federalism should work within a framework of cooperative federalism, with adequate support for economically weaker States.

5. Conflictual federalism

  • Meaning: prolonged disputes between the Centre and States over constitutional powers, financial resources or administrative authority.
  • Areas of conflict:
    • Legislative (Union intervention in State subjects, Concurrent List laws)
    • Administrative (Governor's office, CBI jurisdiction, Central forces, All-India Services)
    • Financial (tax devolution, GST compensation, borrowing limits, conditional grants)
    • Inter-State (river waters, boundaries, resources)
  • Significance: some disagreement is normal in a working federation, but prolonged confrontation weakens institutional trust and governance.
Way forward / key point

Overall understanding: cooperation, competition, bargaining and conflict are not mutually exclusive. They exist together in Indian federalism. The challenge is to make sure political disagreements do not undermine constitutional autonomy and cooperative governance.

2 · Centre-state Legislative Relations · Lists · Residuary · 42nd Amendment · Doctrines#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Centre State Relations handout pp. 7-9. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Legislative power is divided by territory (Article 245) and by subject (Article 246, Seventh Schedule). The Union List beats the State List; on the Concurrent List the Union law prevails in a clash. Residuary powers go to Parliament. The 42nd Amendment moved five subjects to the Concurrent List. When Union and State laws overlap, courts use doctrines such as pith and substance, colourable legislation, harmonious construction, federal supremacy and ancillary powers.

Distribution OF Legislative Powers

The Constitution distributes legislative power on two bases.

  • 1. Territorial jurisdiction (Article 245). Parliament can make laws for the whole or any part of India. A State Legislature can make laws for the whole or any part of the State. A law made by Parliament is not invalid merely because it operates outside India (extra-territorial operation).
  • Doctrine of Territorial Nexus: a State law may affect persons, property or activities outside the State if there is a real and sufficient connection between the State and the subject of the law. Case: State of Bihar v. Charusila Dasi (1959) upheld the application of State legislation where a sufficient territorial connection existed.
  • Key distinction:
    • Parliament has wider territorial authority
    • State legislation ordinarily operates within the State, subject to territorial nexus

2. Distribution of subjects (Article 246). The Seventh Schedule divides subjects into three lists.

Union List (List I)State List (List II)Concurrent List (List III)
Parliament has exclusive legislative authorityState Legislatures ordinarily have exclusive authorityBoth Parliament and State Legislatures can make laws
Subjects of national importanceSubjects of regional and local importanceSubjects needing national uniformity and regional flexibility
Defence, foreign affairs, banking, currency, atomic energyPolice, public order, public health, agriculture, prisonsEducation, forests, criminal law, marriage, electricity

Constitutional position

  • The Union List prevails where legislative fields overlap and cannot be reconciled.
  • Parliament may legislate on State List subjects for Union Territories (Article 246(4)).
  • On the Concurrent List, under Article 254 a Union law ordinarily prevails over a conflicting State law, subject to constitutional exceptions.

Residuary Powers (article 248)

Residuary subjects are matters not listed in the Union, State or Concurrent Lists.

  • Parliament has exclusive authority to legislate on them, including residuary taxation powers.
  • This is an important centralising feature of Indian federalism.
  • Contrast with the USA: there, undelegated powers generally remain with the States or the people.

Transfer OF Subjects TO THE Concurrent LIST

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 moved five subjects from the State List to the Concurrent List:

  • 1. Education
  • 2. Forests
  • 3. Weights and Measures
  • 4. Protection of Wild Animals and Birds
  • 5. Administration of Justice, including the constitution and organisation of courts other than the Supreme Court and High Courts

Principles OF Interpretation OF Legislative Powers

Disputes arise when a Union law and a State law seem to cover the same subject. Courts use several doctrines to decide validity.

1. Doctrine of Pith and Substance

  • Meaning: courts look at the true nature and main purpose of a law, not its incidental effects.
  • A law is not automatically invalid just because it incidentally enters another legislature's field. If its pith and substance is within the legislature's competence, incidental encroachment can be allowed.
  • Case: State of Bombay v. F. N. Balsara (1951) upheld major provisions of the Bombay Prohibition Act despite incidental overlap with Union subjects.

2. Doctrine of Colourable Legislation

  • Meaning: what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly.
  • It stops a legislature from indirectly using a power the Constitution does not give it. Courts look at the substance of the law, not just its title or form.
  • Case: K. C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953) explained the doctrine in relation to legislative competence.

3. Doctrine of Harmonious Construction

  • Meaning: entries in different lists should be read together so that each has meaning.
  • Courts try to reconcile apparently conflicting entries
  • One entry should not needlessly make another redundant
  • Case: Calcutta Gas Company v. State of West Bengal (1962) applied harmonious interpretation to overlapping entries.

4. Principle of Federal Supremacy

  • Meaning: where entries genuinely conflict and cannot be reconciled, the Union List prevails over the State List. This follows the scheme of Article 246.
  • On Concurrent List subjects, conflicts between valid Union and State laws are governed by Article 254.
  • Significance: protects national legislative priorities, but limits State autonomy.

5. Doctrine of Ancillary Powers

  • Meaning: the power to legislate on a subject includes powers reasonably necessary to make the law effective.
  • Example: the power to impose a tax ordinarily includes the power to lay down its assessment, collection and prevention of evasion.

6. Limits on delegated legislation

  • Parliament and State Legislatures may delegate detailed rule-making to the executive.
  • But essential legislative functions, especially deciding legislative policy, cannot be delegated completely.
  • Significance: stops legislatures from surrendering their core lawmaking job to the executive.
Note

How to remember the order: Pith and substance (what is it really about?) → Colourable legislation (is it a disguise?) → Harmonious construction (can the entries live together?) → Federal supremacy (if not, Union List wins) → Ancillary powers (what comes with the power?).

3 · Parliament ON State LIST · Repugnancy (Art 254) · Model LAWS · FARM LAWS · Assent TO Bills#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Centre State Relations handout pp. 9-13. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Parliament can legislate on State List subjects in specific cases: Rajya Sabha resolution (249), National Emergency (250), request by States (252), treaties (253), Union Territories and President's Rule. Article 254 settles repugnancy on the Concurrent List. Model laws, the Farm Laws 2020 and the Governor's delay in giving assent (Tamil Nadu 2025 and the November 2025 Presidential Reference) show where federal friction lies.

Parliament's Power TO Legislate ON State LIST Subjects

Although the State List is normally for State Legislatures, the Constitution lets Parliament legislate on State subjects in specified circumstances.

Article 249: Legislation in the national interest

  • The Rajya Sabha may authorise Parliament to legislate on a State List subject in the national interest.
  • It needs a resolution backed by not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.
  • The resolution stays valid for a maximum of one year and can be renewed for one year at a time.
  • A law made this way ceases to operate six months after the resolution expires, except for things already done.
  • Federal significance: the Rajya Sabha, which represents States, enables temporary Union legislation on matters normally reserved to States.
  • GST: Article 249 also allows such a resolution on Goods and Services Tax under Article 246A.

Article 250: During a National Emergency

  • Parliament can legislate on State List subjects while a National Emergency (Article 352) is in force.
  • Laws made this way cease to operate six months after the Emergency ends, subject to protection of past actions.
  • Rationale: coordinated national action in extraordinary circumstances.

Article 251: Inconsistency between Union and State laws under Articles 249 and 250

  • State Legislatures do not lose their legislative powers when Parliament legislates under Article 249 or 250.
  • But if the State law and the Union law conflict, the Union law prevails.
  • The conflicting State law is inoperative only while the parliamentary law continues to have effect.

Article 252: At the request of States

  • Two or more States may request Parliament to legislate on a State List subject.
  • The State Legislatures must pass resolutions (in a bicameral State, both Houses must approve).
  • Parliament may then make a law applying to those States. Other States can adopt it by passing similar resolutions.
  • Once made, the law can be amended or repealed only by Parliament, not by the State Legislature.
  • Example: the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 was made using Article 252.
  • Federal significance: States can seek common legislation without permanently moving the whole subject to the Union List.

Article 253: Implementing international agreements

  • Parliament can make laws to implement international treaties and agreements, conventions with foreign countries, and decisions of international conferences and organisations.
  • This power extends even to matters in the State List.
  • Federal concern: international agreements increasingly affect agriculture, environment, health, education and development, which are State responsibilities.
  • Punchhi Commission: recommended more State involvement in treaty negotiations where agreements directly affect State List subjects.

Other circumstances

  • Union Territories (Article 246(4)): Parliament can legislate on State List subjects for territories not part of a State.
  • President's Rule (Article 356): Parliament may exercise the State Legislature's legislative powers when authorised during President's Rule.

Repugnancy Between Union AND State LAWS (article 254)

Repugnancy arises when a valid State law conflicts with a valid parliamentary law on a Concurrent List subject.

  • Article 254(1): general rule. If a State law conflicts with a Central law, the Central law prevails and the State law is void to the extent of the inconsistency. The rule applies whether the parliamentary law was made before or after the State law.
  • Article 254(2): exception through Presidential assent. A State law on a Concurrent List subject can prevail within that State, even if it conflicts with an earlier parliamentary law, provided the State Bill was reserved for the President's consideration and got Presidential assent. But Parliament can later amend, vary or repeal the State law or make a conflicting law on the same matter.
  • Federal significance: Article 254(2) gives States limited flexibility to adopt laws suited to local needs.

How courts decide repugnancy

  • Mere differences in wording or approach do not automatically show repugnancy.
  • Courts ask: do the two laws contain directly conflicting provisions? Would obeying one mean violating the other? Did Parliament intend to occupy the entire field?
  • Significance: avoids needless invalidation of State laws while respecting constitutional supremacy.

Other Instruments OF Legislative Relations

Model laws on State subjects

  • What: draft laws prepared by the Union Government for States to adopt, modify or reject voluntarily. They promote common standards without Parliament legislating directly on State List subjects.
  • Examples:
    • Model Agricultural Land Leasing Act, 2016 (leasing reforms)
    • Model Agricultural Produce and Livestock Marketing Act, 2017 (market reforms)
    • Model Tenancy Act, 2021 (tenancy regulation and disputes)
    • Model Prisons and Correctional Services Act, 2023 (prison reforms)
  • Advantages:
    • National uniformity while preserving State autonomy
    • States can adapt reforms to local conditions
    • Promotes cooperative federalism and policy innovation
  • Concerns:
    • Uneven adoption across States
    • Central funding incentives may indirectly steer State choices
    • Model laws can become a tool of "soft centralisation" if States are not truly consulted

Case study: the Farm Laws, 2020

  • The three Farm Laws became a major controversy over how legislative powers are divided.
  • Constitutional position:
    • State List Entry 14 (agriculture)
    • State List Entry 28 (markets and fairs)
    • Concurrent List Entry 33 (trade and commerce in specified goods, including foodstuffs)
  • Union's argument: Parliament could legislate under Entry 33 of the Concurrent List on trade and commerce in foodstuffs.
  • States' objections:
    • (1) legislative encroachment, as agricultural markets ordinarily fall in State jurisdiction
    • (2) loss of APMC revenue, as trade outside regulated mandis could cut State market fees
    • (3) fear for MSP, as farmers feared marketing changes could gradually weaken procurement (the laws did not abolish MSP)
    • (4) inadequate consultation
  • State response: Punjab and some other States passed legislation to modify the operation of the Central laws in their territory, raising questions under Article 254.
  • Outcome: the three laws were repealed in 2021 after sustained protests. The Supreme Court did not finally decide their legislative constitutionality.
  • Federal lesson: reforms touching State responsibilities need consultation, consensus and constitutional clarity, even if Parliament has a plausible source of competence.

Reservation OF State Bills AND Assent (articles 200 AND 201)

Article 200: when a Bill passed by the State Legislature is presented to the Governor, he may

  • 1. Grant assent.
  • 2. Withhold assent, with the constitutional procedure of returning a non-Money Bill for reconsideration.
  • 3. Reserve the Bill for the President's consideration.
  • The Governor must reserve a Bill if it would so reduce the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position the Constitution gives it.
  • Article 201: the President may grant or withhold assent to a reserved Bill and may direct reconsideration of a non-Money Bill.
  • Federal concern: delay in giving assent or reserving Bills can block State legislation and create uncertainty over the powers of elected State governments.

Supreme Court developments on assent

  • 1. Tamil Nadu Governor case (April 2025). A two-judge Bench examined the Governor's long delay in acting on Bills passed by the Tamil Nadu Legislature. It held that the Governor cannot indefinitely withhold assent or use inaction to obstruct legislation (no pocket veto or absolute veto)
  • It set specific time limits for the Governor and President to act under Articles 200 and 201
  • And using Article 142 it declared 10 Bills deemed assented, finding the Governor's actions unconstitutional
  • 2. 16th Presidential Reference (November 2025). A five-judge Constitution Bench, in advisory jurisdiction under Article 143, clarified the position: (a) three options under Article 200 (assent; withhold and return a non-Money Bill with comments; reserve for the President) and the Governor has discretion to choose among them
  • (b) no judicial timelines: courts cannot fix deadlines, as the Constitution gives none
  • (c) no deemed assent: courts cannot use Article 142 for it, as that would replace a constitutional function and breach separation of powers
  • (d) limited judicial intervention: in prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction, courts may issue a limited mandamus to act within a reasonable time, without dictating the decision
  • (e) limited justiciability: decisions under Articles 200 and 201 cannot ordinarily be challenged on merits before the Bill becomes law

Effect on the Tamil Nadu judgment

  • The November 2025 opinion did not formally set aside the specific relief given to Tamil Nadu in April 2025: the 10 Bills kept their legal validity.
  • But the larger Bench's reading superseded the earlier reasoning on judicially imposed timelines and deemed assent, which can no longer be treated as binding precedent.
  • Constitutional significance: the Court tried to balance State legislative autonomy, gubernatorial discretion, separation of powers and judicial oversight, while making clear that indefinite inaction is not constitutionally acceptable. (current affairs; verify)

Commission Recommendations ON Legislative Relations

Sarkaria Commission (1983-88)

  • Strong Centre without over-centralisation: it accepted the need for a strong Union but warned against excessive interference in State autonomy.
  • Concurrent List: the Centre should legislate only where national uniformity is necessary, after consulting States.
  • Residuary powers:
    • Keep residuary taxation powers with the Union
    • Place other residuary matters in the Concurrent List
  • Article 249:
    • It opposed deleting or amending Article 249, as existing safeguards were enough
    • Suggested procedural safeguards in the Rajya Sabha to build consensus
  • Reservation of Bills: the Governor should behave like constitutional machinery, and the Centre should decide on a Bill reserved for the President within four months.
  • Other measures:
    • Liberal use of Article 254(2) for State-specific laws
    • Framework legislation by the Union on Concurrent List subjects, leaving details to States
    • Pre-legislative consultation through the Inter-State Council and sectoral forums
    • Stronger parliamentary committee scrutiny of laws raising federal questions

Way forward

  • 1. Respect legislative autonomy: Parliament should avoid needless intervention in State List subjects.
  • 2. Strengthen consultation: consult States before laws on Concurrent List subjects and international agreements.
  • 3. Reform assent procedures: more transparency and accountability in handling State Bills.
  • 4. Strengthen the Rajya Sabha: give more attention to State interests.
  • 5. Encourage model laws: voluntary, adaptable model legislation where coordination is needed without binding uniformity.
  • 6. Promote cooperative federalism: use the Inter-State Council, GST Council and other forums to settle legislative disagreements through dialogue.
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: Centre-State legislative relations combine State autonomy with a strong Union framework. This keeps national unity, but excessive centralisation weakens federal trust. The way forward is constitutional restraint, meaningful consultation and respect for State legislative powers.

4 · Centre-state Administrative Relations · Directions · AIS · Councils · BSF · Commissions#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Centre State Relations handout pp. 13-18. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Executive power follows legislative power. The Union can give binding directions to States (Articles 256, 257, 339(2), 347, 350A, and in emergencies), and States and the Union can delegate functions to each other (258, 258A). All-India Services bind the administration together. Coordination bodies (Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils, NEC, NITI Governing Council, GST Council) exist but are weak. Flashpoints are broad directions, BSF jurisdiction and Centrally Sponsored Schemes.

Distribution OF Executive Powers

The general principle is that executive power follows legislative power. The government that can make laws on a subject ordinarily also has the job of implementing them.

  • The Constitution also lets the Union issue binding administrative directions to States on certain matters, in normal times and in emergencies.

Directions in normal times

  • Article 256: every State must use its executive power to ensure compliance with laws made by Parliament. The Union may give directions to States for implementing such laws.
  • Article 257: States must use their executive power without impeding or prejudicing the Union's executive power. The Union may give directions about (a) building and maintaining communications declared important for national or military purposes and (b) protection of railways within a State. The Union must compensate States for specified extra costs of following such directions.
  • Article 339(2): the Union may direct States on preparing and carrying out schemes essential for the welfare of Scheduled Tribes.
  • Article 347: the President may direct a State to officially recognise a language spoken by a substantial section of its population, for specified purposes.
  • Article 350A:
    • States and local authorities should provide mother-tongue instruction at the primary stage for linguistic minority children
    • The President may give directions to ensure this

Directions during emergencies

  • National Emergency (Article 352): the Union may direct States on how they use their executive power, even on matters ordinarily in the State sphere.
  • President's Rule (Article 356): the President may take over specified executive functions of the State when its constitutional machinery fails.
  • Financial Emergency (Article 360): the Union may give directions on financial propriety, salary reductions and reservation of State financial Bills for the President.
  • Consequence of non-compliance (Article 365): if a State fails to comply with constitutionally valid Union directions, the President may hold that the State government cannot be carried on according to the Constitution. But non-compliance does not automatically justify President's Rule: action under Article 356 stays subject to constitutional safeguards and judicial review.

Mutual Delegation OF Executive Functions

The Constitution lets the Union and States hand administrative functions to each other for efficient governance.

  • Article 258 (Union to States): the President may, with the State Government's consent, entrust Union executive functions to State authorities. Parliament may also confer specified powers and duties on State authorities by law. The Union must compensate for specified extra administrative costs.
  • Article 258A (States to Union): the Governor may, with the Union Government's consent, entrust State executive functions to Union authorities.
  • Significance: gives administrative flexibility and coordination, supports cooperative federalism, and does not permanently alter legislative powers.

All-india Services AND Administrative Federalism

All-India Services (AIS) are common to the Union and States and promote administrative integration and uniform standards of governance.

  • Constitutional basis: Article 312 lets Parliament create new All-India Services after a Rajya Sabha resolution passed by two-thirds of members present and voting.
  • Existing services: Indian Administrative Service (IAS), Indian Police Service (IPS), Indian Forest Service (IFoS).
  • Significance:
    • Administrative unity (common standards across Union and States)
    • National integration (officers serve in different States and with the Union)
    • Professional expertise (continuity and specialised experience)
    • Policy coordination (helps carry out Union and State programmes)

Issues in managing All-India Services

  • 1. Dual control: officers work under State governments but are governed by Central service rules, creating tension over accountability and control.
  • 2. Central deputation: disagreements over releasing officers for Union assignments.
  • 3. Political interference: frequent transfers and postings hurt independence and continuity.
  • 4. Cadre shortages: shortages weaken State administrative capacity and discourage deputation.
  • 5. Federal autonomy: States argue that too much Central control over officers reduces their ability to run their own administration.
  • Reform: better Union-State consultation, cadre planning, professional independence and transparent transfer policies.

Institutions FOR Centre-state Coordination

Effective federalism needs institutions that encourage consultation, cooperation and settlement of differences.

Inter-State Council (Article 263)

  • Basis: Article 263 lets the President set up an Inter-State Council if it serves the public interest. It was set up in 1990 on the Sarkaria Commission's recommendation.
  • Composition:
    • The Prime Minister is Chairperson
    • Chief Ministers of all States
    • Chief Ministers of Union Territories with Legislative Assemblies
    • Administrators of other UTs
    • Specified Union Ministers and other participants under applicable provisions
  • Functions:
    • (1) inquire into and advise on disputes between States
    • (2) investigate and discuss matters of common interest to Union and States
    • (3) make recommendations for better coordination of policy and administrative action
  • Nature:
    • Advisory and recommendatory
    • It cannot enforce its decisions
  • Issues:
    • (1) meets infrequently and is not a regular forum
    • (2) recommendations are not binding
    • (3) weak follow-up and monitoring
    • (4) States need not be consulted through the Council before every important Union decision that affects them
    • (5) the GST Council and NITI Aayog Governing Council have taken over some consultation
  • Sarkaria Commission: recommended a permanent Inter-State Council. Punchhi Commission: favoured strengthening its authority, consultation and dispute-resolution role.

Zonal Councils

  • Five Zonal Councils were set up under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956.
  • Composition:
    • The Union Home Minister is Chairperson
    • Chief Ministers of member States are members and rotating Vice-Chairpersons
    • Two other Ministers from each member State
    • Representatives of the UTs concerned
  • Objectives:
    • Prevent acute State consciousness and regionalism
    • Develop the habit of cooperative working
    • Resolve inter-State and Centre-State problems
    • Promote regional economic and social development
    • Handle issues of linguistic minorities, infrastructure and administrative coordination
  • Significance: a regional platform to settle common problems by dialogue.

North Eastern Council (NEC)

  • Set up under the North Eastern Council Act, 1971. It covers the eight North-Eastern States and gives a platform for regional planning, infrastructure development and inter-State coordination, with representation of the member States and the Union Government.
  • Significance: addresses the North-East's distinctive geographic, developmental and security challenges.

Other coordination mechanisms

  • NITI Aayog Governing Council: consultation on national development policy and State priorities.
  • GST Council (Article 279A): joint decisions on GST.
  • Empowered Committee of State Finance Ministers: helped the move to VAT and GST.
  • National Integration Council: forum for national integration and communal harmony.
  • Chief Secretaries' and DGP conferences: administrative coordination, information-sharing and policy implementation.

Issues IN Centre-state Administrative Relations

1. Broad powers under Articles 256 and 257

  • Concern: these Articles give the Centre wide authority to give binding directions to States.
  • Issues:
    • The vague wording gives the Union much discretion
    • Frequent directions can interfere with States' own administrative duties
    • They can become a source of confrontation when different parties rule the Centre and States
    • And Article 365 non-compliance can be used to justify action over breakdown of constitutional machinery
  • Punchhi Commission:
    • Articles 256 and 257 should be safeguards of last resort
    • Formal directions should usually follow consultation and warnings

2. BSF jurisdiction

  • The Border Security Force works under the BSF Act, 1968. Section 139 lets the Union Government confer specified powers on BSF personnel by notification.
  • Extension (2021): the Union changed the area within which the BSF may use specified powers (arrest, search and seizure under certain laws).
StateEarlier limitRevised limit
Punjab15 km50 km
West Bengal15 km50 km
Assam15 km50 km
Gujarat80 km50 km
Rajasthan50 km50 km

The notification also covered the entire territory of specified North-Eastern States and the Union Territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh.

  • Important distinction: the wider area is about specified statutory powers. It does not transfer general responsibility for police and public order from States to the BSF.
  • Union's justification:
    • Better response to cross-border infiltration and organised crime
    • Better action against narcotics, weapons and other smuggling
    • Uniform operational area across border States
    • Quicker coordination with State Police against trans-border offences
  • States' concerns:
    • (1) federal encroachment, as police and public order are State subjects
    • (2) inadequate consultation (Punjab and West Bengal objected)
    • (3) overlapping jurisdiction may cause BSF-State Police conflict
    • (4) civil liberties, since wider search and arrest powers need safeguards
    • (5) a larger area does not improve security unless capacity and cooperation improve too
  • Way forward: stronger BSF-State Police coordination, intelligence-sharing, operating protocols and human-rights safeguards.

3. Union ministries on State List subjects

  • Subjects like agriculture, public health, police and local government mostly fall in the State List, yet the Union runs ministries and programmes in several of these areas. This creates a debate between national coordination and State autonomy.
  • In favour:
    • (1) national standards keep minimum service levels across States
    • (2) inter-State problems such as pandemics, pollution and disease outbreaks need coordinated response
    • (3) Centrally Sponsored Schemes give resources to fiscally weaker States
    • (4) technical expertise of Union institutions. Example: during COVID-19 national coordination was needed for vaccine procurement, distribution and public health measures
  • Against:
    • (1) encroachment blurs the constitutional division of duties
    • (2) one-size-fits-all schemes ignore regional needs
    • (3) fiscal dependence: conditional funding pushes States to adopt Union priorities
    • (4) political centralisation: control over grants and programmes raises the Centre's influence. Example: the creation of the Ministry of Cooperation raised concern about Union involvement in a subject mainly assigned to States
  • Way forward: the NITI Aayog Sub-Group of Chief Ministers on Centrally Sponsored Schemes favoured rationalising schemes and more State flexibility: focus Union support on core national priorities, give States flexibility in implementation, consult before launching programmes touching State subjects, and balance national goals with State needs.

Commission Recommendations ON Administrative Relations

Sarkaria Commission (1983-88)

  • 1. Restrained use of Union directions: use Articles 256 and 257 carefully, respecting State autonomy.
  • 2. Inter-State Council: set up a permanent council under Article 263.
  • 3. Prior consultation: consult States widely before Union executive action or legislation on Concurrent List subjects.
  • 4. President's Rule: Article 356 only as a last resort, with safeguards.

Punchhi Commission (2007-10)

  • 1. Articles 256 and 257: Union directions should be safety valves, not tools of routine administrative control.
  • 2. Inter-State Council: strengthen its authority, dispute-resolution role and capacity.
  • 3. Zonal Councils: meet regularly and follow up on recommendations.
  • 4. Sectoral coordination: a Forum of Chief Ministers, chaired by a Chief Minister on rotation, to coordinate policy in sectors such as energy, food, education, environment and health.
  • 5. Central forces: consult States better when deploying them.
  • 6. Administrative federalism: balance national security, effective governance and State autonomy.

Way forward

  • 1. Strengthen intergovernmental institutions: revive the Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils with regular meetings and follow-up.
  • 2. Respect State autonomy: use binding Union directions only where constitutionally necessary, preferably after consultation.
  • 3. Investigative coordination: clear procedures for CBI consent, inter-State investigations and information-sharing.
  • 4. Reform All-India Service management: better cadre management, deputation procedures and professional independence.
  • 5. Security cooperation: BSF, Central forces and State Police should coordinate without needless jurisdictional conflict.
  • 6. Rationalise Centrally Sponsored Schemes: more State flexibility in planning, implementing and using funds.
  • 7. Promote cooperative federalism: replace needless confrontation with dialogue, trust and shared administrative responsibility.
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: administrative relations aim at effective implementation of laws and national coordination while preserving State autonomy. Success depends on constitutional restraint, administrative cooperation and mutual trust; stronger intergovernmental institutions can balance a strong Union with the rights of States.
