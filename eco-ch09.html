1 · Capital markets: the four ways to classify contracts#

In capital markets the demanders and suppliers themselves decide, so they bear the risks and enjoy most of the reward. Intermediaries give information, build trust and do back-end work. (In banking, by contrast, the bank decides: see Chapter 7.) Contracts are classified by:

  1. Rights and obligations: debt versus equity.
  2. Maturity: less than 1 year is short term; more than 1 year is long term.
  3. Terms and conditions: standardised versus customised.
  4. Settlement: spot versus futures (derivatives).
Money market versus capital market
Money marketCapital market
ContractsShort-term (below 1 year) debt contracts (equity gives rights in perpetuity, so it is long term)Equity, and debt of more than 1 year (loans, bonds)
PurposeManage working capital and short-term liquidity mismatchesFixed capital formation (factories, expansion)
FeaturesLess risky, usually unsecured, low interest, extremely liquid: “near money”Money turns into an asset for long: money loses liquidity (the asset itself may be liquid)

T-bills, CMBs, CP and CoD
InstrumentIssuerKey facts (from the handout)

3 · Customised versus standardised, spot versus futures#

Two more splits
Customised (OTC)Standardised
LiquidityMarket not well developed, not easily tradable except via specific counters: Over The Counter (OTC)Many buyers and sellers, easily tradable on markets
ExamplesLoans, debentures, preference sharesBonds, common equity
  • Spot contracts: settled on the spot (buyer pays, seller delivers); also called CNC (Cash and Carry).
  • Futures (standardised) / Forwards (OTC): signed today, exchange at a future date at a predetermined price. The underlying is the commodity or contract; the agreed price is the strike price (the biggest factor is the current market price, CMP); the time limit is maturity.
  • Derivatives: contracts with no value of their own; they derive value from the underlying (futures, options, swaps, securitised loans). The handout likens one to a betting slip on rain.

4 · From a business idea to a public company#

  • A company exists independently of the entrepreneur, so it can enter enforceable contracts, sue and be sued. Authorised capital is the total shares of some face value it may issue; the part issued is paid-up capital. Promoters bring the initial capital. Shares can be given without money, e.g. sweat equity for employees.
  • Growth stages: angel investors (money plus experience, network, mentoring; a valuation is negotiated and equity given); then venture capital funds, private equity funds, investment banks (who raise from firms and HNIs); finally the public.
  • Primary market: the company issues new shares from its authorised capital. Secondary market: an existing investor sells to a new one; the company is nowhere involved.
  • Preference shares: customised shares with priority rights (fixed dividend first and capital back first on bankruptcy). Only beyond a point are standardised shares issued for liquidity.
Public issue
TermMeaning
IPO / FPOInitial / Follow-on Public Offer: the company issues shares and raises capital
OFS (Offer for Sale)Existing owners sell to the public: a secondary market sale. The company receives ZERO. Example in the handout: a ₹10,000 Cr IPO with ₹9,000 Cr as OFS means the company raises only ₹1,000 Cr

The IPO process (SEBI-prescribed)

  1. The company decides the amount (e.g. ₹10,000 Cr) and manner (fresh issue or OFS, capital structure, valuation) with advisors: investment banks (lead manager, underwriter). An underwriter promises to buy all unsold shares.
  2. Application to SEBI: documents (balance sheet, promoter records, risks). The DRHP (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) has no valuation; after approval and once the price band is final it becomes the RHP. (“Red herring”: intentionally lacks final price and quantity.)
  3. Revealing to the public by advertisement.
  4. Application and allotment through ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount): the money is blocked, not debited, so you keep earning savings interest and refunds are not delayed.
  5. Cut-off price. Over-subscription: demand exceeds supply (fair allocation); under-subscription: demand below supply (the underwriter steps in). The green shoe option is an over-allotment option for price stabilisation: the underwriter borrows extra shares from promoters and sells them; if the price crashes on listing, the cash is used to buy back shares from the market.
  6. Successful applicants get shares in their demat accounts; others get funds unblocked.

Public issues and trading both take place on NSE and BSE; exchanges serve the primary as well as the secondary market (commonly forgotten).

5 · Stock market intermediaries#

Five main intermediaries
IntermediaryRole (from the handout)
Stock exchanges (NSE, BSE)Background tasks, trust between strangers, information on demand, supply and price, for rational price discovery. Brokers first met under a Banyan tree in Mumbai in 1875 (today’s BSE). Three functions: trust (the market is the guarantor; the “Amazon” function), convenience (transfer of title via the Registrar), rationality / price discovery (participation gives depth, so no one can manipulate the price)
Clearing house (CH)The trust architect. Pre-funding: buyer’s money is blocked in the trading account and the seller’s shares are blocked in the demat account before the order. Novation: the CH becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, so counterparty risk is annihilated
Depositories and DPsSafe storage of digital assets (shares, bonds, MF units, T-bills). The Depositories Act, 1996 created NSDL and CDSL and mandated dematerialisation. Before, shares were paper, settlement took 14 days, and brokers misused client assets, culminating in the 1992 stock market scam
BrokersThe gateway: citizens cannot trade directly. A SEBI-registered broker opens a trading account (holds funds); a demat account is opened with NSDL or CDSL
BanksProvide money to investors, own or borrowed

The flow: open trading and demat accounts, place a buy order via the broker, the CH makes sure obligations are met first, orders form bids and asks, a matched order is executed. The time from execution to credit of shares and money is the settlement cycle. Settlement is now T+1 (T = transaction day) with a migration to same-day (T+0) started.

Handout alert: demat date and settlement

The handout says the Depositories Act was passed in 1996 and, later, that SEBI required dematerialisation in 1995. The 1996 Act is the statutory date; 1995 is given for the SEBI requirement. Also, settlement was 14 days earlier and is T+1 now. Exam-hall rule: quote Depositories Act 1996 (NSDL, CDSL); mention 1995 only if a question uses it.

6 · Returns: absolute, IRR, alpha and beta#

Ways to look at returns
KindMeaning
Absolute returnsPoint to point over a period
Average returnsPer year, as a compounded average
IRR / XIRRInternal rate of return on cash flows (regular or irregular investments, dividends, rent, maintenance). The handout’s house example: bought at ₹2 Cr, worth ₹5 Cr in 5 years, but rent and maintenance cash flows change the real return
Comparative returnsAgainst a benchmark (Nifty 50, Sensex or a relevant index)
AlphaThe additional return a portfolio earns over the benchmark
BetaThe portfolio’s volatility relative to the benchmark. Beta 1 moves exactly with the Nifty; beta 1.5 is 50% more volatile (riskier). High beta with no alpha means more risk without better returns

7 · Benchmarks and indices: Nifty, Sensex and market versus economy#

  • A benchmark is a basket of listed companies with weights by relative importance (assessed mainly by free-float market capitalisation). Its weighted average price (WAP) at the base date is indexed to 100 or 1000. If WAP goes from 10,000 (index 100) to 100,000 the index is 1000: the sector gave 10 times over 10 years. A relevant benchmark for an IT-heavy portfolio is an IT index.
  • Indices are rebalanced about every 6 months (firms exit and enter, weights change).
Sensex versus Nifty
BSE SensexNifty 50
Companies3050
Base value1001000

Market versus economy

  • Broad indices are used as a proxy for economic health, which can mislead. They reflect only the WAP of 50 largest, most formal, cash-rich corporations, not unorganised MSMEs, agricultural distress, poverty, unemployment, inflation or trade.
  • In systemic shocks (COVID-19) the market can hit record highs while GDP falls and unemployment rises, because large firms survive and capture the share of dying small businesses.
  • Forward-discounting mechanism: the economy is a mirror of the past and present, but the stock market discounts the future (about 18 months ahead in the handout’s words). A stock price is the aggregate of what millions expect a company to earn.

8 · Derivatives: the highway-and-land example#

The handout uses land worth ₹10 L that could be worth ₹50 L if a highway is approved or ₹5 L if cancelled, with the decision in one year.

Spot versus futures versus option
ContractWhat you doIf approved (₹50 L)If cancelled (₹5 L)Risk
Spot (cash and carry)Pay ₹10 L now, take the papersYou own itYou own it100% risk, all capital tied up
Futures / forwardAgree to buy for ₹11 L in one year; no money nowProfit ₹39 LLoss ₹6 L (bound to buy a worthless plot)Symmetrical, an obligation for both
OptionPay a non-refundable ₹1 L premium for the right, not the obligation, to buy at ₹11 LNet profit ₹38 L (cost ₹12 L)Loss only ₹1 L: tear up the contractAsymmetrical: upside large, downside capped at the premium
Mapping to market terms
In the exampleTerm
The barren landUnderlying asset (shares, gold, wheat)
The 1-year deadlineExpiry date
The agreed ₹11 LStrike price
The ₹1 L tokenOption premium
  • The option itself becomes tradable and valuable (others may offer ₹15 L for it): that is why derivatives are traded.
  • Philosophy: derivatives are risk-management (hedging) vehicles; institutions buy options as insurance. Retail traders use the leverage (₹1 L premium controls ₹50 L of exposure) for gambling; the handout says 99% of retail derivative traders lose money.
  • The ticking clock: in spot markets you need to be right on direction; in derivatives you must be right on direction AND time. A call option expiring March 31 is worthless if the highway is announced April 1.

9 · Expert handling of money: mutual funds and ETFs#

  • Retail trap: slogans like “Yeh market sabka hai” and FOMO; by the time news reaches retail traders, institutions have known for months. The handout says 9 out of 10 retail traders lose money and advises handing capital to experts, i.e. mutual funds.
  • Every “fund” has: an owner, a designated objective, a fund manager, voluntary contributors and a corpus.
Mutual fund versus ETF
Mutual fundETF (Exchange Traded Fund)
StructureA Trust under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882; investors are beneficiaries; a board of Trustees oversees. An AMC (Asset Management Company) launches schemes and hires fund managersPassively managed: e.g. a Nifty 50 ETF holds the exact 50 stocks in index weights
Unit valueInvestors get Units; value of one unit is NAV = (assets − liabilities and expenses) ÷ units. TER (Total Expense Ratio) is capped by SEBIValue computable every second
TradingNAV computed once a day (EOD); buy and sell with the AMC, not on the exchangeUnits listed on the stock exchange and traded live like shares

10 · Crossing borders: depository receipts and P-Notes#

Depository receipts
InstrumentMeaning
MechanismAn Indian company (the handout uses Infosys) deposits shares with a local custodian bank in Mumbai; an overseas depository bank issues receipts against them, traded in dollars abroad. Avoids conforming the whole accounting system to US standards
ADRAmerican Depository Receipt: traded in the US
GDRGlobal Depository Receipt: Europe or other global markets
IDRIndian Depository Receipt: the reverse; a foreign company raises rupees from Indian investors by issuing IDRs on BSE/NSE (the handout’s example: Standard Chartered)

Participatory Notes (P-Notes) are officially Offshore Derivative Instruments (ODIs). They were used as a “washing mechanism” (round-tripping) for black money: illegal wealth goes to a tax haven (Mauritius), is handed to a SEBI-registered FII which invests in its own name; the FII issues a P-Note to the owner, passing on the profits. SEBI could not see the real owner. In 2016-2017 SEBI required FIIs to disclose the Ultimate Beneficial Owner (UBO) of every P-Note on request; issuance then fell sharply.

Mains practice: answer skeletons

Try the answer on a blank page first, then open the skeleton.

Distinguish the money market from the capital market. Explain the instruments of the money market.

Intro: The money market is short-term debt for working capital; the capital market is long-term for fixed capital.

  • Under 1 year, debt, near money, usually unsecured.
  • T-bills (91/182/364 days), CMBs (7-15 days): only the Centre; WMA for States.
  • CP: investment-grade, 5 AIFIs. CoD: tradable bank deposit.
  • Capital market: equity and debt over 1 year.

Closing line: The money market keeps the economy’s plumbing liquid.

Explain how a company raises capital and the process of an IPO. Why should investors read an OFS differently?

Intro: Companies raise capital through equity and debt at successive stages of growth.

  • Angel, VC/PE, investment banks, public issue.
  • IPO process: lead manager and underwriter, DRHP to RHP, ASBA, cut-off, green shoe, demat allotment.
  • Primary versus secondary market.
  • In an OFS the company receives zero: it is early investors cashing out.

Closing line: Read the size of the fresh issue, not the headline.

Discuss the role of the stock exchange, clearing house and depositories in building trust in the market.

Intro: A stock market is an institutional solution to anonymous strangers trusting each other with money.

  • Exchange: trust, convenience and price discovery.
  • Clearing house: pre-funding and novation remove counterparty risk.
  • Depositories Act 1996, NSDL and CDSL ended physical fraud after the 1992 scam.
  • Settlement shortened to T+1 and moving to T+0.

Closing line: Trust is the product that markets sell.

Why is the stock market not a reliable indicator of the health of the economy?

Intro: Indices reflect a weighted average of a few large listed firms.

  • Nifty 50 excludes MSMEs, agriculture, unemployment, poverty.
  • COVID: record highs while GDP fell, as large firms captured share.
  • Forward-discounting: prices reflect expectations about the future.
  • Constituents tend to do well when the economy does, but there is no guarantee.

Closing line: The economy mirrors the past; the market bets on the future.

Differentiate futures and options. Why were derivatives designed, and why do retail traders lose?

Intro: Both derive their value from an underlying asset.

  • Futures: symmetrical obligation; options: right with a capped loss (premium).
  • Underlying, strike, expiry, premium.
  • Designed for hedging by institutions.
  • Retail: leverage, need to be right on direction and time; 99% lose per the handout.

Closing line: Derivatives are insurance in the hands of institutions and lottery tickets for retail.
