Answer: (A)

Per 'Why the British Pulled Back from Higher Education After 1870', the British view was that mass literacy would create politically aware peasants who would read nationalist material and demand rights — so they limited primary education funding. 2018PYQ 19(Prelims 2018)

Which of the following led to the introduction of English Education in India? 1. Charter Act of 1813 2. General Committee of Public Instruction, 1823 3. Orientalist and Anglicist Controversy Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

Answer: (D)

(D) ✓ — The first humble beginning with respect to education in India was made by the Charter Act of 1813, which directed the East India Company to sanction ₹ 1 lakh annually to encourage learned Indians and for promotion of Modern Sciences. A decade before Lord Macaulay arrived in India, the General Committee of Public Instruction was formed in 1823, which was to guide the company on the matter of education. The General Committee of Public Instruction had two groups viz Orientalists and Anglicists on the issue of the Development of Education of India. Orientalists supported the use of vernacular languages and Anglicists supported the use of English. Finally, In 1835, the education policy of Lord Macaulay was published titled "Minute on Indian Education" in which he advocated educating Indians through English and also enrich Indian Languages' so that they become the vehicles of European scientific, historical and literary expression.

2018PYQ 22(Prelims 2018)

Regarding Wood's Despatch, which of the following statements are true? 1. Grants-in-Aid system was introduced. 2. Establishment of universities was recommended. 3. English as a medium of instruction at all levels of education was recommended. Select the correct answer using the codes given below.

Answer: (A)

(A) ✓ — In 1854 'Wood's Despatch' was sent to the Governor General Lord Dalhousie. Wood's Despatch is called Magnacarta of English Education in India. The main recommendations of Charles Wood's Despatch are as follows

- It declared English as the medium of instruction, for higher education. English language was the most perfect medium of education. It also emphasised the importance of the vernacular languages, for it was through the medium of the vernacular languages, that European knowledge could infilter to the masses.

- It proposed the setting up of vernacular primary schools' in the villages at the lowest stage, followed by Anglo-Vernacular high schools and an affiliated college at the district level.

- It recommended a system of grants-in-aid to encourage and foster private enterprise in the field of education. This grants- in-aid was conditional on the institutions employing qualified teachers and maintaining proper standards of teaching.

- Universities on the model of the London University were proposed for Calcutta, Bombay and Madras.

FYQ 1 Compare the social-reform strategies of Raja Ram Mohan Roy, Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar and Jotirao Phule, with special focus on how each chose his beneficiary group.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Compare the three reformers along beneficiary-group + strategy axes, then weigh effectiveness.

Guiding themes:

1. Roy's reform was upper-caste-Hindu-focused — Brahmo Samaj 1828 + Sati abolition 1829 aimed at rationalising orthodox Hinduism from within using Vedantic monism ('Sati' + 'The Brahmo Samaj (1828)').

2. Vidyasagar worked within Hindu shastric framework but turned it into a weapon — used Parashar Smriti to argue for widow remarriage (Hindu Widow Remarriage Act 1856) + ran 35 girls' schools as practical demonstration ('Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820–1891)'-17).

3. Phule rejected Vedic authority entirely — Satyashodhak Samaj 1873 + Gulamgiri 1873 anchored reform in shudra-atishudra interests, attacking caste at its scriptural root ('Jyotirao Phule (1827–1890)').

4. Beneficiary-group axis: Roy = upper-caste Bengal intelligentsia; Vidyasagar = upper-caste widows + future generations via girls' education; Phule = shudras, women across caste, and untouchables.

5. Strategy axis: Roy = doctrinal reconstruction; Vidyasagar = shastric jurisprudence + lived practice; Phule = mass mobilisation via Marathi pamphlets + alternative schools + non-Brahmin priest-training.

6. Conclusion: each reformer's chosen audience defined his ceiling. Roy + Vidyasagar's upper-caste-anchored reforms could be co-opted by reformist orthodoxy; Phule's anti-caste politics could not — which is why Ambedkar later named Phule (with Buddha + Kabir) as his three teachers ('Character and Failures of Early 19th-Century Reform' close).

FYQ 2 Examine the role of print culture in the making of modern Indian political consciousness, 1780-1947.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Trace the press's evolution + assess its impact on nationalist political consciousness.

Guiding themes:

1. The first newspaper — Hicky's Bengal Gazette 1780 — was already adversarial to the EIC, setting the press's anti-establishment tradition before Indians joined the trade ('How the Press Began in India').

2. Press Regulations cycle (1799 censorship → 1823 licensing → 1835 Metcalfe liberation → 1857 Gagging Act → 1878 Vernacular Press Act → 1881 Ripon repeal) shows the colonial state's contradictory grip — alternating between repression and concession ('Growth of Vernacular Press and Early Regulations (1800–1857)'-27.4).

3. Indian-run vernacular press (Sambad Kaumudi 1821, Rast Goftar, Kesari + Mahratta, The Hindu, Amrita Bazar Patrika) created an inter-regional reading public that thought in pan-Indian terms about colonial policy ('20th Century: Resistance and Regulations (1900–1947)').

4. The 1907 split + Swadeshi years showed the press's capacity to mobilise — Bal Gangadhar Tilak's Kesari articles built mass political consciousness; the resulting trials (1897, 1908, 1916) made Tilak a martyr-figure ('Print Culture' + cross-reference to Ch 29 Extremists).

5. Vernacular press in independence years — Gandhian Young India + Harijan + Navajivan, plus Maulana Azad's Al-Hilal — extended political consciousness to mass-vernacular audiences not reachable via English press ('Print Culture').

6. Conclusion: print culture transformed colonial governance into a contested public domain. Without the press, the moderate-to-extremist transition, the Swadeshi boycott, and Gandhian mass politics would have lacked their connective tissue. The press was the nervous system of the freedom movement ('20th Century: Resistance and Regulations (1900–1947)' close).
