In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • Judicial review lets courts strike down laws and orders that break the Constitution (Art. 13, Arts. 32 and 226). The Ninth Schedule (281 laws) is not a blanket shield: laws added after 24 April 1973 can be tested (I.R. Coelho, 2007). Judicial activism (Art. 142, PILs) must stay balanced by restraint.
  • The High Court (Arts. 214-231) has a Chief Justice and other judges
  • Judges retire at 62, and are removed like Supreme Court judges (motion by 100 LS or 50 RS members, 3-member inquiry, special majority). Its powers are original, writ, appellate, supervisory, control over lower courts, court of record and judicial review
  • Below the High Court sit the District and Sessions Court, civil and magistrate courts, tribunals (Arts. 323-A and 323-B), and people's forums: Lok Adalats (final award, no appeal), Gram Nyayalayas (2008), Family Courts (1984) and Consumer Commissions (2019 Act; District up to Rs 50 lakh, State up to Rs 2 crore, National above Rs 2 crore).
  • Parliament = President + Rajya Sabha + Lok Sabha (Art. 79). RS: max 250 (245 now), permanent, 6-year terms, one-third retire every 2 years, chosen by STV. LS: max 552 (543 elected now), 5-year term, first-past-the-post.
  • Members:
    • Qualify at 30 (RS) and 25 (LS) (Art. 84)
    • Disqualified under Art. 102, the RPA 1951 and the Tenth Schedule
    • Seat falls vacant for double membership (10 / 14 days) or 60 days of absence. The Speaker (Art. 93) and the RS Chairman (the Vice-President) preside
  • Business: sessions at most 6 months apart, motions and closure, four kinds of Bills (ordinary, Money Art. 110, Financial Art. 117, Constitution Amendment), joint sitting (Art. 108), the Budget in six stages, three Funds, privileges. Parliament is not sovereign like Britain's.

Key terms

Judicial review
Courts test laws and executive orders against the Constitution; a law that fails is ultra vires and void; it is part of the Basic Structure
Collegium
Senior Supreme Court judges led by the CJI who recommend judges; evolved in the Second (1993) and Third (1998) Judges Cases
Court of record (Art. 215)
A High Court keeps its judgments as permanent precedents and can punish contempt of itself and of lower courts
Lok Adalat
People's court under Sec 19 of the Legal Services Authorities Act 1987; settles by compromise; award is a civil court decree and cannot be appealed
Money Bill (Art. 110)
Deals only with tax, borrowing and the Consolidated Fund; only Lok Sabha introduces it; Rajya Sabha must return it in 14 days; the Speaker's certificate is final
Quorum
Minimum members for business: one-tenth of the House, so 55 in Lok Sabha and 25 in Rajya Sabha
Charged expenditure (Art. 112(3))
Spending charged on the Consolidated Fund (President, judges, CAG, UPSC, debt); Parliament can discuss it but not vote on it
Prorogation
The President ends a session (Art. 85(2)); adjournment only ends a sitting; dissolution ends the Lok Sabha itself

1 · Judicial review and the Ninth Schedule#

  • Judicial review is the process by which courts examine whether legislative and executive actions conform to the Constitution. It is a check on arbitrary power and upholds the supremacy of the Constitution and citizens' rights.
  • First established in Marbury v. Madison (1803) by Chief Justice John Marshall of the US Supreme Court.
  • In India the power flows directly from the Constitution and is vested in both the Supreme Court and the High Courts.
  • The Supreme Court has held judicial review is part of the basic structure: it cannot be curtailed even by a constitutional amendment.
  • It tests the validity of laws and executive orders. If they violate the Constitution they are declared ultra vires (beyond authority), null and void. It is also a legitimiser of government action that stays within limits.
  • It does not mean every law is automatically examined
  • It works only when a law's validity is challenged in a case or a citizen claims violation of rights. The courts are "sentinels on the qui vive"

Due process versus procedure established by law

ConceptMeaningApplication in India
Procedure established by lawA law is valid if passed by a competent authority following the proper legislative procedure, regardless of fairnessOriginally adopted in Art. 21; emphasised in A.K. Gopalan (1950)

  • Two functions:
    • Legitimising conforming government action
    • Protecting the Constitution from encroachment by any organ. Seen as the "interpretational and observer" role
  • Suo motu action and PILs expand access by relaxing locus standi (only the aggrieved person may sue)
  • The court can act even without a direct complaint when public welfare is at stake
  • Memory aid (Six uses of judicial review): Supremacy of the Constitution, Misuse of power prevented, Protecting rights, Federal balance, Independence of judiciary, Tyranny of executive prevented: "Strong Men Protect Fair Independent Trials."

How the Supreme Court has widened rights through review

  • Freedom of the press under Art. 19(1)(a) though not named
  • protective discrimination (reservations) held compatible with equality
  • Right to Education and Right to Clean Environment read into Art. 21
  • Delay in trials as denial of justice
  • Free legal aid and speedy trials as part of fundamental rights
  • PILs and activism for bonded labourers, women, children and marginalised groups
  • CNG for public transport in Delhi and compulsory helmets show review as a "living instrument"

The Ninth Schedule and judicial review

  • Introduced by the First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951 with Art. 31B, under PM Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • Purpose: protect socially progressive laws (land reforms, agrarian equality, abolition of the zamindari system) from being invalidated for violating Fundamental Rights.
  • Originally 13 Acts
  • Today 281 laws (the handout adds "of which about 66 were added before 24 April 1973")

Landmark cases on the Ninth Schedule

CaseYearPrinciple
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala1973Basic Structure Doctrine; laws violating basic structure cannot be protected by the Ninth Schedule
Waman Rao v. Union of India1981Only laws added before 24 April 1973 (the Kesavananda date) are fully protected; later additions can be judicially reviewed
I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu2007No blanket immunity; laws inserted after 1973 can be struck down for violating Arts. 14, 19, 21 or the Basic Structure

Five conclusions of I.R. Coelho (2007)

  • 1: No law can destroy the Basic Structure, whether by amendment or inclusion in the Ninth Schedule
  • 2: The impact and effect test: if the consequence of a law violates basic features, it is invalid
  • 3: All Ninth Schedule additions after 24 April 1973 can be challenged under Arts. 14, 19, 21
  • 4:
    • Protection is conditional, not absolute
    • Each case on its merits
  • 5:
    • A law already upheld cannot be re-challenged
    • A struck-down law cannot revive by mere inclusion in the Schedule
  • NCRWC recommendations:
    • Use the Ninth Schedule only as a last resort, for agrarian reform or Directive Principles in Arts. 39(b) and 39(c)
    • Make inclusion transparent with justification for each
    • Set an institutional review mechanism to check periodically whether laws in the Schedule remain relevant and valid

2 · Judicial activism and restraint#

  • Judicial activism is an assertive judiciary reading the law as a living instrument of social transformation, a shift from a reactive judiciary to a creative, proactive one.

Origin and constitutional basis

  • Term coined by Arthur Schlesinger Jr. in the USA in 1947.
  • In India it grew from the mid-1970s under Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer, Justice P.N. Bhagwati and Justice O. Chinnappa Reddy, especially after the Emergency (1975-77) exposed executive overreach.
  • Legitimacy from Art. 32 (SC writs for Fundamental Rights), Art. 226 (HC writs "for any other purpose"), Art. 142 (SC may pass any decree for complete justice).
  • Memory aid (Roots of activism): Krishna Iyer, Bhagwati, Chinnappa Reddy: "Keen Bold Courts."

3 · High Courts: organisation, appointment, tenure#

  • The High Courts are the highest judicial authorities at State level, below the Supreme Court, with superintendence and control over all subordinate courts in their jurisdiction.

Establishment and composition

  • Art. 214: a High Court for each State. Art. 231: Parliament may set up a common High Court for two or more States, or States and a Union Territory.
  • Examples:
    • Punjab and Haryana HC serves both States and Chandigarh
    • Gauhati HC serves several north-eastern States
  • 25 High Courts (2025)
  • Delhi, Bombay, Madras, Calcutta and Allahabad are the oldest, from British rule
  • Art. 216:
    • A Chief Justice and such other judges as the President deems necessary
    • Strength varies with population, pendency and workload and can be altered
  • Seat:
    • Usually the State capital, with permanent or circuit benches elsewhere. Examples: Bombay HC benches at Nagpur, Aurangabad, Goa
    • Madhya Pradesh HC at Jabalpur with benches at Indore and Gwalior

Appointment (Art. 217) and qualifications

  • Judges are appointed by the President. For the Chief Justice: consult the CJI and the Governor. For other judges: consult the CJI, the Governor and the Chief Justice of that High Court.
  • In practice the Collegium System (Second Judges Case 1993, reaffirmed by the Third Judges Case 1998): senior Supreme Court judges headed by the CJI recommend names
  • The President normally follows

4 · High Courts: powers and independence#

  • Every High Court has original, appellate, writ, supervisory and administrative jurisdiction plus judicial review.

1. Original jurisdiction

  • Election disputes (MPs and State legislators) under the RPA, 1951.
  • Revenue matters.
  • Enforcement of Fundamental Rights by writs (Art. 226).
  • Transfer of constitutional cases from subordinate courts with a substantial question of constitutional interpretation.
  • Original civil jurisdiction in higher-value cases: Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Delhi. Before 1973 the first three also had original criminal jurisdiction, abolished by the Criminal Procedure Code, 1973.

2. Writ jurisdiction (Art. 226)

  • Writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto.
  • Purposes:
    • (1) enforce Fundamental Rights
    • (2) any other purpose (ordinary legal rights). So the High Court's jurisdiction is wider than the Supreme Court's Art. 32 and concurrent with it for Fundamental Rights
  • It can reach persons or authorities outside its territory if the cause of action arises within it.

4. Supervisory jurisdiction (Art. 227)

  • Superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its territory, except military courts. Powers: call for returns, frame rules and forms, regulate records and accounts, fix fees of clerks, sheriffs, officers and legal practitioners.
  • Covers judicial and administrative supervision
  • Works as revisional jurisdiction
  • May be used suo motu
  • An extraordinary power, to be used sparingly, in cases of:
    • Excess of jurisdiction
    • Gross violation of natural justice
    • Error of law
    • Disregard of binding precedent
    • Perverse findings
    • Manifest injustice

5. Control over subordinate courts (Arts. 233-235)

  • The Governor consults the High Court in appointment, posting and promotion of District Judges and in recruitment to the judicial service.
  • Manages posting, promotion, leave, transfer and discipline of judicial officers below District Judge.
  • May withdraw cases involving a substantial constitutional question, decide them, or return them with its ruling.
  • Its decisions bind all subordinate courts in its jurisdiction.

6. Court of Record (Art. 215)

  • Record of proceedings: permanent record and precedent value, cannot be challenged before subordinate courts.
  • Power to punish for contempt of itself and subordinate courts (civil or criminal), under the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971. It cannot take cognisance where the act is already an IPC offence.
  • Inherent power to review and correct its own judgments though not written in the Constitution.

7. Judicial review

  • Examines laws and executive orders of Centre and State
  • Unconstitutional ones are ultra vires. Flows from Arts. 13 and 226
  • Part of the Basic Structure. Grounds: violates Part III
  • Beyond the maker's competence
  • Repugnant to the Constitution
  • The 42nd Amendment (1976) tried to bar High Courts from questioning Central laws
  • The 43rd Amendment (1977) restored the position

  • A forum where pending cases and pre-litigation disputes are settled amicably. Organised at all levels from the Supreme Court to Taluk courts.

    • Mutual consent
    • Application by one party

  • Powers of a Civil Court under the CPC, 1908:

    • The Lok Adalat is a Civil Court for procedure, contempt and enforcement
  • Award:
    • final and binding
    • Treated as a decree of a civil court
    • Enforceable like one
    • no appeal. No award without consent

8 · Parliament and the composition of the two Houses#

  • Parliament is the chief legislative authority of the Union and represents the will of the people at the national level. It works in the Westminster-pattern parliamentary system, where the executive is accountable to the legislature.
  • Part V (Articles 79-122) lays down structure, composition, tenure, offices, procedures, privileges and legislative powers of Parliament.
  • The aim is both supremacy of the legislature in lawmaking and responsible government in the Union.

Three elements of Parliament

  • Parliament = President + Council of States (Rajya Sabha) + House of the People (Lok Sabha).
  • The Hindi names Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha were formally adopted in the 1950s.
  • Rajya Sabha = upper chamber representing States and Union Territories. Lok Sabha = directly elected lower chamber representing the citizens.
  • The President is not a sitting member of either House, yet is an essential part: a law passed by both Houses needs the President's assent.
  • The President also summons and prorogues sessions, dissolves the Lok Sabha, addresses the Houses and promulgates ordinances when Parliament is not in session.

President-in-Parliament versus the American scheme

  • India (Westminster model):
    • Executive is embedded in the legislature ("President-in-Parliament")
    • President is part of Parliament
    • Accountability of executive to legislature
    • Not strict separation of powers
  • USA:
    • President is a separate, non-legislative head
    • The legislature (Congress) has only two Houses (Senate and House of Representatives)
  • Memory aid (Three limbs): President, Rajya Sabha, Lok Sabha: "Parliament Runs on Limbs: three, not two."

Composition of the two Houses

  • Both Houses are built differently: one indirect and federal, the other direct and popular.

Rajya Sabha (Council of States)

  • Sanctioned maximum 250: up to 238 represent States and UTs, 12 are nominated by the President for eminence in literature, science, art and social service.
  • Currently 245: 229 from States, 4 from UTs, 12 nominated. Seats are allotted by the Fourth Schedule.
  • States: members are indirectly elected by the elected members of the State Legislative Assemblies, by proportional representation through the single transferable vote. Seats are broadly population-based (larger States more, smaller States like Tripura have one member), unlike the US Senate where every State has equal representation.
  • Union Territories: also indirect, by a specially constituted electoral college for each UT, same STV method. Only UTs with sufficient population or legislative arrangements are represented.
  • Nominated:
    • 12 persons with distinguished contribution in arts, science, literature and social service
    • They enrich debate without contesting elections
  • Result: a federal chamber with indirect, population-weighted representation plus a small expert component.

Lok Sabha (House of the People)

  • Constitution fixes the maximum at 552: 530 from States, 20 from UTs, 2 Anglo-Indians nominated by the President.
  • 104th Amendment Act, 2020 abolished Anglo-Indian nomination and extended SC/ST seat reservation for another decade.
  • Currently 543 elected: 530 from States, 13 from UTs.
  • States:
    • Directly elected from territorial constituencies by universal adult franchise. Voting age 18 years, reduced from 21 by the 61st Amendment Act, 1988. Seats are broadly population-based
    • Boundaries are revised by Delimitation Commissions
  • Union Territories:
    • Parliament decides how UT members are chosen
    • The Union Territories (Direct Election to the House of the People) Act, 1965 provides direct election. Larger UTs like Delhi get more seats
  • Nominated (historical):
    • Until 2020 up to two Anglo-Indian members if the community lacked representation
    • Initially temporary, periodically extended until the 104th Amendment ended it

9 · Electoral system of the Lok Sabha#

  • Framework: Articles 81, 82 and 170, refined by amendments and delimitation. Principles: territorial representation, population-based seat allocation, universal adult franchise.

Territorial constituencies and parity

  • Each State is divided into single-member territorial constituencies.
  • Parity between States:
    • Ratio of population to seats broadly uniform (Art. 81(1))
    • States with population below six million are exempt from strict proportionality
  • Parity within a State: constituencies carry roughly equal population.
  • "Population" = figures of the latest decennial census.

Delimitation and the freeze

  • Art. 82 lets Parliament readjust constituencies after each census, through a Delimitation Commission. Commissions: 1952, 1962, 1972, 2002.
  • 42nd Amendment (1976) froze inter-State seat allocation and boundaries at 1971 levels until 2000, to encourage population control.
  • 84th Amendment (2001) extended the freeze till 2026
  • It also allowed rationalising boundaries on population changes (initially 1991 census, updated to 2001 by the 87th Amendment) without altering the total seats per State
  • Purpose: protect States that have controlled population while keeping proportional representation.

Freeze of seats over time

  • 1976: 42nd Amendment: seats and boundaries frozen at 1971 levels until 2000
  • 2001:
    • 84th Amendment: freeze extended till 2026
    • Boundaries may be rationalised (1991 census) with no change in seats per State
  • 87th Amendment: Rationalisation basis updated to the 2001 census

SC and ST reservation (Article 330)

  • Reserved constituencies in the Lok Sabha in proportion to SC/ST population in each State.
  • Reserved seats are delimited after each census, by the same method as general seats.
  • Common electorate: all voters vote, but candidates must belong to the reserved category. SC/ST members may also contest general seats.
  • Reservation was meant for ten years from the Constitution
  • Extended repeatedly, most recently by the 95th Amendment Act (2009) and continued under the 104th Amendment Act (2020), which also ended Anglo-Indian nomination

Voting method: First-Past-the-Post (FPTP)

  • Single-member constituency
  • highest votes wins
  • Merits:
    • Direct accountability of MP to constituents
    • Simple
    • Helps stable government formation
  • Rajya Sabha uses PR with STV (Articles 80 and 66)
  • Lok Sabha prefers simplicity, local representation and clear outcomes

Why the Constituent Assembly rejected PR for the Lok Sabha

  • Problems of PR:
    • Complexity for a largely illiterate electorate
    • Party fragmentation and unstable coalitions
    • Costly and administratively complex
    • Voters choose parties not candidates
    • No by-elections (vacancies cannot be filled by individual contests)
  • What FPTP gives:
    • Direct accountability
    • Constituency-based representation
    • Stability
    • Reservation and delimitation correct the representation of marginalised communities
  • Memory aid (Five faults of PR): Complexity, Instability, Cost, Weak link, No by-elections: "Careful Indians Chose Winner-takes-all Normally."

10 · Duration and continuity of the two Houses#

  • Provisions mainly in Articles 80, 83, 85 and 352, with the Representation of the People Act, 1951.

Rajya Sabha: the permanent chamber

  • First constituted in 1952
  • not subject to dissolution
  • One-third retire every two years
  • Vacancies are filled by State Assembly election or by Presidential nomination (Art. 80(3))
  • Six-year term under the RPA, 1951
  • The Constitution itself prescribes no fixed term
  • Retiring members can be re-elected or re-nominated indefinitely.
  • In the first Rajya Sabha the members to retire at the end of the initial term were chosen by lottery to start the rotation.
  • Role: revising chamber, check on hasty or populist measures.

Lok Sabha: the temporary house

  • Normal term five years from its first sitting (Art. 83(2))
  • It then dissolves automatically
  • The President on the advice of the Council of Ministers can dissolve it earlier (loss of majority or instability). Such dissolution is not subject to judicial review (handout's statement).
  • Emergency extension: during a national emergency (Art. 352), Parliament may extend its term by law, one year at a time, but not beyond six months after the emergency has ceased.

Rajya Sabha versus Lok Sabha: continuity

  • Rajya Sabha:
    • Permanent
    • 1/3 retire every 2 years
    • 6-year term
    • No dissolution
    • No special emergency provision needed
  • Lok Sabha:
    • Temporary
    • 5-year term from first sitting
    • Dissolves
    • Term extendable 1 year at a time in emergency
    • Can be dissolved early by President

Why the terms differ (rationale)

  • 1. Stability versus accountability:
    • Permanence of the Rajya Sabha shields legislation from short-term swings
    • The five-year Lok Sabha term lets voters judge the government at regular intervals, with early dissolution if the government loses majority. 2. Federal versus popular representation: Rajya Sabha gives continuity to regional voices (smaller States keep a voice)
    • Lok Sabha carries the direct popular mandate. 3. Adaptation to extraordinary circumstances: Lok Sabha's term flexes in an emergency (Arts. 83(2), 352)
    • Rajya Sabha needs no such provision. 4. Synergy: Rajya Sabha anchors stability, expertise and federal representation
    • Lok Sabha ensures responsiveness and adaptability. Even if the Lok Sabha is dissolved or in a crisis, Rajya Sabha keeps legislative functioning
  • Handout's illustration: GST needed long debate aligning federal and State views, aided by the Rajya Sabha's stable composition. Laws with State implications (land acquisition, environmental rules) gain from the Rajya Sabha's federal view.

Statutory qualifications (RPA, 1951)

  • Becoming subject to a disqualification under Art. 102 or any parliamentary law vacates the seat (office of profit, citizenship, conviction under RPA, defection).
  • President decides after consulting the Election Commission (Art. 103). The 1959 Act lists exempted offices.

3. Resignation

  • Written to the Chairman (Rajya Sabha) or Speaker (Lok Sabha)
  • Seat vacant once accepted
  • Presiding officer may check that it is voluntary and genuine
  • Under Rule 240 can refuse if made under duress or coercion
  • The handout mentions 2019 resignations of MLAs and MPs examined for voluntariness.

4. Absence (Article 101(4))

  • A member absent from all sittings for 60 consecutive days without permission may have the seat declared vacant by the House.
  • Days when the House is prorogued or adjourned for more than four consecutive days are excluded.
  • Leave is granted by the House (ill health, official duties abroad).

5. Other situations

  • Election declared void by a High Court under the RPA (appeal to the SC under Section 116A).
  • Expulsion by the House for conduct unbecoming (Arts. 105 and 122)
  • Handout example: the 2005 "cash-for-query" scandal
  • Elected President (Art. 58) or Vice-President (Art. 66): must vacate the seat.
  • Appointed Governor: Art. 158(2): a Governor cannot be a member of either House.

6. Election of a disqualified candidate

  • Constitution lays down no procedure
  • The RPA does. Under Sections 80-84 an election petition goes to the High Court, which can declare the election void. The Supreme Court under Art. 136 is the final appellate authority
  • Memory aid (Ways to lose the seat): Double membership, Disqualification, Resignation, Absence, Other (void election), Expulsion, President or VP election, Governor appointment: "Don't Dally, Report All Office Exits, Please, Gentlemen."

14 · Presiding officers: the Speaker and Deputy Speaker#

  • Each House has its own presiding officers to ensure smooth, impartial and efficient proceedings. Lok Sabha: Speaker, assisted by the Deputy Speaker. Rajya Sabha: Chairman (the Vice President), assisted by the Deputy Chairman. Each House also keeps a panel of chairpersons or vice-chairpersons to preside in their absence.

Speaker of the Lok Sabha: election and tenure

  • Elected by Lok Sabha members from among themselves, as soon as possible after the House's first sitting
  • The President fixes the date of the election
  • Office falls vacant if the Speaker:
    • (1) ceases to be a member
    • (2) resigns in writing to the Deputy Speaker
    • (3) is removed by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members (effective majority), with 14 days' prior notice
  • Normally holds office for the life of the House (five years) but continues after dissolution until a new Speaker is elected by the new House.
  • A removal motion can be admitted only if supported by at least 50 members.

Speaker's office across dissolution

  • New Lok Sabha meets: Outgoing Speaker vacates office immediately before the first meeting (Art. 94(2))
  • Speaker Pro Tem:
    • President appoints a newly elected member (usually the senior-most)
    • President administers his oath
  • Pro Tem's duties:
    • Administer the oath to new members (Art. 99) and preside until the new Speaker is elected
    • Helps form the panel of chairpersons
  • New Speaker elected (Art. 93): Office of Speaker Pro Tem ceases automatically

Role, powers and functions

  • Head of the Lok Sabha, principal spokesperson, guardian of its privileges and procedures. Authority comes from the Constitution, the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business, and conventions.
  • Maintains order and decorum
  • final interpreter of the Constitution's provisions, the Rules and precedents as they apply to the House
  • Can adjourn or suspend a sitting in the absence of quorum (one-tenth of the total membership).
  • Casting vote only in a tie
  • Does not vote in the first instance
  • Presides over joint sittings convened by the President under Art. 108.
  • May permit a secret sitting on the request of the Leader of the House.
  • His certification of a Money Bill under Art. 110 is final and binding
  • It is endorsed before transmission to the Rajya Sabha or President
  • Decides defection disqualification under the Tenth Schedule
  • Kihoto Hollohan (1992) made this subject to judicial review
  • Ex-officio Chairman of the Indian Parliamentary Group and of the Conference of Presiding Officers of Legislative Bodies in India.
  • Appoints chairpersons of all Lok Sabha committees
  • Himself chairs the Business Advisory Committee, Rules Committee and General Purposes Committee

Independence and impartiality

  • Security of tenure: removable only by a resolution of a majority of the total membership, motion admitted with 50 members' support.
  • Salary fixed by Parliament under the 1954 Act, charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (no annual vote)
  • Stated at ₹4,00,000 per month (revised by the 2018 Rules)
  • Conduct and decisions cannot be discussed in the House except by a substantive motion
  • His rulings on procedure and discipline are beyond the courts (privilege, Art. 122)
  • Votes only to break a tie.
  • Order of precedence (Ministry of Home Affairs): rank seven, with the Chief Justice of India, above all Cabinet Ministers except the PM and Deputy PM.
  • UK convention: Speaker resigns from party on election. In India the Speaker retains party membership, yet is expected to rise above party.
  • Why "Speaker"?:
    • He hardly speaks in a session or gives views on a Bill
    • He speaks only to tell members to sit down. The name comes from Britain, where the Speaker summed up the views of members and communicated them to the King

"Once a Speaker, Always a Speaker"

The proposal

  • Why India should consider it:
    • Speaker is from the ruling party and depends on it for re-election
    • Protects all MPs' rights (Nehru, Mavalankar)
    • Fair anti-defection adjudication (SC ordered Telangana Speaker to decide on 10 defecting MLAs within three months)
    • UK precedent (Sir Lindsay Hoyle re-elected unopposed in 2024)
  • Challenges:
    • Career disincentive and shrinking talent pool
    • Weak neutrality conventions in India
    • Judicial overhang
    • Risk of executive influence over an isolated Speaker
    • Reduced accountability
  • Way forward (handout):
    • Codify neutrality by convention or law (resign from party, no re-entry into active politics, supermajority removal)
    • Transfer anti-defection adjudication to an independent tribunal
    • Build UK-like conventions (the opposition does not contest the Speaker's seat)
    • Training in constitutional law and procedure
  • Global practice:
    • UK Speaker resigns party and is re-elected across governments
    • Canada Speaker chosen by secret ballot
    • Australia Speaker party-affiliated but neutral, votes only to break ties
  • Mavalankar (first Lok Sabha Speaker): the Speaker "belongs to all the members or belongs to none". Nehru: the Speaker is "the symbol of the nation's freedom and liberty".

Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha

  • Elected by the House itself under Art. 93, after the Speaker's election
  • The Speaker fixes the date
  • Holds office for the life of the House
  • Vacates if he ceases to be a member, resigns in writing to the Speaker, or is removed by a majority of all the then members after 14 days' notice
  • Acts as Speaker when the Speaker is absent or the office vacant, with all powers, including presiding over a joint sitting. He is not subordinate to the Speaker
  • He is directly responsible to the House
  • Unique feature: whenever appointed a member of a parliamentary committee he automatically becomes its chairman.
  • Does not vote first, casting vote in a tie. During his own removal proceedings he does not preside but may speak and take part in debate.
  • While the Speaker presides he is an ordinary member (debates and votes).
  • Pay: fixed by Parliament, charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
  • Convention:
    • Up to the 10th Lok Sabha both Speaker and Deputy Speaker were usually from the ruling party
    • since the 11th Lok Sabha the Speaker is from the ruling party and the Deputy Speaker is typically from the main opposition

15 · Chairman, Deputy Chairman and leaders in Parliament#

Chairman of the Rajya Sabha

  • Art. 64: the Vice-President is ex-officio Chairman. This links the executive and legislative organs.
  • Art. 65(2): when the VP acts as President or discharges the President's functions he does not perform the Chairman's duties.
  • Unlike the Speaker, the Chairman is not a member of the Rajya Sabha
  • So no vote in the first instance, only a casting vote in a tie (Art. 100(1))
  • Similar to the Speaker: presides, decides points of order, interprets provisions. Two powers belong only to the Speaker: deciding a Money Bill (Art. 110(3)) and presiding over a joint sitting (Art. 118).
  • Removal only by removal as Vice-President (Art. 67(b)): resolution of the Rajya Sabha by an effective majority, agreed to by the Lok Sabha.
  • Pay under the 1953 Act, Consolidated Fund
  • ₹4,00,000 per month, with housing, travel and medical facilities equal to the Speaker
  • Art. 65(3): when VP acts as President he gets the President's emoluments, not the Chairman's.
  • Art. 90(b): while a resolution for his removal is under consideration he cannot preside, though he may speak and take part, without voting.

Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha

  • Created by Art. 89(2)
  • The regular day-to-day presiding officer
  • elected from among members by a simple majority of members present and voting
  • Holds office while a member
  • Vacates (Art. 90) if he ceases to be a member, resigns in writing to the Chairman, or is removed by a majority of all the then members after 14 days' notice
  • When presiding has the Chairman's powers
  • No vote first, casting vote in a tie
  • May represent the Rajya Sabha in State functions or delegations
  • Art. 97: pay of Chairman and Deputy Chairman fixed by Parliament, Consolidated Fund.

Panel of Vice-Chairpersons (Rajya Sabha)

  • Chairman nominates it under the Rules
  • Any member presides in the absence of both Chairman and Deputy Chairman. It is a procedural, not constitutional arrangement
  • If both offices are vacant, no panel member can preside
  • Under Art. 91(1) the President appoints a Rajya Sabha member to perform the Chairman's duties until elections

Leaders in Parliament

Leader of the House

    • The Prime Minister if a Lok Sabha member

  • Chief government spokesperson
  • Works with the Speaker or Chairman to keep order and consensus
  • The PM may nominate a Deputy Leader of the House. The US equivalent is the Majority Leader.

Leader of the Opposition (LoP)

  • Leader of the largest opposition party with at least one-tenth of the House's strength. Statutory recognition by the Salary and Allowances of Leaders of Opposition in Parliament Act, 1977 (first time).

    • Alternative policy framework ("government in waiting")

    • Shadow Cabinet

16 · Sessions, quorum, voting and majorities#

  • Under Art. 85(1) the President summons each House from time to time, so that no more than six months elapse between two sessions. Parliament therefore meets at least twice a year
  • Typically three sessions

  • A session begins with the first sitting and runs until prorogation or, for the Lok Sabha, dissolution. The gap between sessions is recess (no business).
  • Each session has many sittings, usually a morning sitting (11 am-1 pm) and an afternoon sitting (2 pm-6 pm). The Speaker or Chairman controls adjournment of sittings.

Four terms that confuse

  • Adjournment: suspends a sitting for a set time (hours, days, weeks) without ending the session.
  • Adjournment sine die:
    • Suspends the sitting without fixing a date
    • The presiding officer can reconvene before or after
  • Prorogation: formal end of a session (ends the sitting and the session). The presiding officer declares adjournment sine die, but the President prorogues under Art. 85(2).
  • Dissolution:
    • Only the Lok Sabha
    • Ends its life irrevocably (Art. 83(2)), automatically after five years (extendable in a national emergency under Art. 352) or prematurely by the President on the Council of Ministers' advice. The Rajya Sabha is permanent

Public Bill versus Private Member's Bill

  • Public (Government) Bill:
    • Introduced by a minister
    • seven days' notice
    • Backed by the Cabinet, higher chance of passage, priority time

    • Raises issues and holds government to account

Stages of an ordinary Bill (Art. 111 on assent)

From introduction to Act

  • 1. First reading:
    • Introduced by a minister or private member after leave of the House
    • Title and objectives read
    • no debate
    • Then published in the Gazette. If already gazetted before introduction, leave is not required
  • 2. Second reading:
    • The critical stage with three sub-stages: general discussion (principles; may take it up at once, send to select/joint committee, or seek public opinion)
    • committee stage (clause by clause, report to the House)
    • consideration stage (vote on each clause; amendments incorporated)
  • 3. Third reading:
    • The Bill as a whole
    • Only accept or reject
    • no amendments
    • Majority of members present and voting passes it
  • 4. Second House:
    • First, second and third readings again
    • four options: pass without amendments
    • Pass with amendments and return
    • Reject
    • Take no action
  • Deadlock:
    • Disagreement, or no action for six months: President may summon a joint sitting (Art. 108)
    • Majority there passes it
  • 5. President's assent:
    • Grant, withhold, or return for reconsideration (not for Money Bills)
    • If passed again by both Houses the President must assent (suspensive veto)

Money Bills and Financial Bills

Money Bill (Article 110)

  • A special class of Bills dealing exclusively with government finance. Art. 110 gives an exhaustive list of matters that make a Bill a Money Bill, singly or together:
  • – imposition, alteration or abolition of taxes;
  • – regulation of borrowing by the Union;
  • – custody, payment into and withdrawal from the Consolidated and Contingency Funds
  • appropriation from the Consolidated Fund
  • – declaring or increasing expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund;
  • – receipt or custody of public money, or audit of Union/State accounts.
  • Provisions only incidentally connected may still make it a Money Bill. Fines, licence or service fees, and local taxes for local purposes are excluded from being Money Bills merely on that account.
  • Speaker's certificate (Art. 110(3)) settles any dispute and is final
  • It is endorsed when the Bill goes to the Rajya Sabha or the President
  • Introduced only in the Lok Sabha, on the President's recommendation (Arts. 110 and 117).
  • Rajya Sabha (Art. 109):
    • Can only recommend
    • Must return the Bill within 14 days. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject any or all recommendations
    • If it rejects them, the Bill is deemed passed in the original form. If the Rajya Sabha does not return it within 14 days, it is deemed passed in the form sent by the Lok Sabha
  • Assent (Art. 111):
    • The President may give or withhold assent
    • Cannot return it
  • Why special:
    • Control of taxation and spending rests with the directly elected house
    • Avoids delay in budgetary work
    • Predictable timetable for fiscal measures. Classification is contentious because it curtails the Rajya Sabha

Joint sitting of the two Houses (Article 108)

  • An extraordinary device to break a deadlock between the Houses
  • Balances bicameral scrutiny with decisive lawmaking

When can a joint sitting be summoned?

  • Deadlock arises:
    • (a) the other House rejects the Bill
    • (b) the Houses finally disagree on amendments
    • (c) the other House takes no action for six months from receipt (time prorogued or adjourned for more than four consecutive days is excluded)
  • President summons: Discretionary but within constitutional limits and conventions
  • Vote:
    • A Bill is deemed passed if a majority of members present and voting in the joint sitting approve
    • In practice favours the larger Lok Sabha

Rules of the joint sitting

  • Presiding:
    • The Speaker
    • If absent, the Deputy Speaker
    • If both unavailable, the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. The Chairman of the Rajya Sabha does not preside (he is not a member of either House)
  • Lok Sabha Rules govern the conduct. Quorum = one-tenth of the total membership of both Houses together.
  • Scope:
    • Only ordinary Bills and financial Bills
    • not Money Bills (Lok Sabha overrides) and not Constitution Amendment Bills (each House must pass separately under Art. 368)
  • Amendments: no genuinely new amendments, except those that were the precise cause of the deadlock or those needed because of delay.
  • Once the President gives notice of summoning, neither House can proceed further with the Bill.
  • If the Lok Sabha is dissolved after the notice but before the sitting, the joint sitting can still be held
  • If dissolved before the notice, the Bill lapses and no sitting can be summoned

Financial authority

23 · Parliamentary privileges#

  • Privileges are special rights, immunities and exemptions of the Houses, their committees and members, protecting independence, dignity and functioning. They extend to persons entitled to take part in proceedings, such as the Attorney General and Union Ministers (Art. 105). They do not extend to the President, though he is part of Parliament.

Collective privileges

  • Publication of proceedings: each House controls publication. The 44th Amendment Act, 1978 restored the press's freedom to report true proceedings without prior permission, except in secret sittings.
  • Exclude strangers and hold secret sessions on sensitive matters of national importance.
  • Regulate internal procedure: frame rules, conduct business, adjudicate procedural disputes.
  • Punish breach of privilege (members and outsiders): reprimand, admonition, imprisonment, suspension, expulsion.
  • Immediate information of arrest, detention or conviction of a member.
  • Summon witnesses and papers
  • Hold inquiries
  • Judicial immunity: courts cannot question proceedings (Art. 122).
  • Legal protection in the House: no one, member or outsider, can be arrested or served legal process within the precincts without the presiding officer's permission.

Individual privileges

  • Freedom from arrest in civil matters:
    • During the session and 40 days before and after
    • only civil cases, not criminal or preventive detention
  • Freedom of speech in Parliament, including votes and speeches in committees (Art. 105)
  • No court proceedings for statements in the House, subject to the Constitution and rules
  • Exemption from jury service and the right to refuse to appear as a witness in court cases during sessions.
  • Memory aid (Individual privileges): Arrest (civil only, 40 days), Speech (Art. 105), Jury exemption: "All Speak Justly."

Breach of privilege and contempt

  • Breach of privilege = violating the rights or immunities of members individually or the House collectively
  • punishable by the House (Art. 105)
  • Contempt of the House = any act or omission that obstructs the House, members or officers or undermines the House's dignity, authority and honour. It is broader: e.g. disobedience of a lawful order of the House is contempt though not a specific breach of privilege.

Breach versus contempt

  • Breach of privilege:
    • Violation of a specific right or immunity
    • May amount to contempt
  • Contempt of the House:
    • Broader
    • Includes breach of privilege and other acts lowering the House's authority (e.g. disobeying its lawful order)

Sources of privileges

Six sources

  • 1. Constitution:
    • Art. 105(1): freedom of speech and right to publish proceedings
    • Other privileges initially like the British House of Commons at 26 January 1950
  • 2. 44th Amendment (1978):
    • Replaced the Commons reference: privileges remain as they existed on 20 June 1979 until Parliament defines them
    • A verbal change with the same practical effect
  • 3. Statutes:
    • Parliament may define, modify or expand them
    • no exhaustive law codifies privileges yet
  • 4. Rules of Procedure: Each House's rules and standing orders, with mechanisms to punish breach and contempt
  • 5. Conventions: Practices that develop over time
  • 6. Judicial interpretations: Courts balance parliamentary autonomy with fundamental rights
  • Memory aid (Six sources): Constitution, 44th Amendment, Statutes, Rules, Conventions, Judgments: "Courts Allow Some Rights, Carefully Judged."
  • Shortcut:
    • Privileges numbers. 40 days before and after the session (civil arrest only). 44th Amendment 1978: press right
    • Reference date 20 June 1979.

24 · Limits on Parliament: sovereignty and weak oversight#

Parliamentary sovereignty: Britain

  • In Britain Parliament is the supreme legal authority: it can enact, amend or repeal any law without legal limit.
  • A.V. Dicey:
    • Three implications: (1) unlimited legislative power (De Lolme: Parliament "can do everything except make a woman a man and a man a woman")
    • (2) no legal distinction between ordinary and constitutional law, so no separate constituent authority
    • (3) no judicial review: courts cannot invalidate Acts

Why India's Parliament is not sovereign

Four limits

LimitIndiaBritain
1. Written ConstitutionCodified; delineates powers of the three organs; legislative and constituent powers are legally distinct; some amendments need ratification by at least half of State legislatures (Art. 368)Unwritten
2. Federal structureDivision of powers (Arts. 245-255); Parliament mainly on Union and Concurrent Lists (Arts. 246-254); on State List only in exceptions (Arts. 249, 250, 252, 253, 356)Unitary; all legislative power at the centre
3. Judicial oversightJudicial review (Art. 13) strikes down laws inconsistent with Part III; Supreme Court and High Courts keep the rule of lawCourts cannot question Acts
4. Fundamental rightsJusticiable rights, Part III (Arts. 12-35); Art. 13(2): any inconsistent law is voidNo codified charter; liberties by common law and conventions
  • Comparison: the Indian Parliament is more like the U.S. Congress: limited by a written Constitution, federal division, judicial review and a Bill of Rights. India adopted the parliamentary model, but its sovereignty is restricted to keep the balance of powers, rights and federal harmony.
  • Memory aid (Four limits): Written Constitution, Federalism, Judicial review, Fundamental rights: "We Face Judges' Firmness."

Sovereign Britain versus bound India

  • Britain:
    • Unlimited legislation
    • No separate constituent power
    • No judicial review
    • Unitary
    • No written charter of rights
  • India:
    • Written Constitution
    • Federal division
    • Judicial review (Art. 13)
    • Justiciable Part III rights
    • Basic structure limits amendments

Why parliamentary oversight is weak in practice

  • Size and complexity of government machinery:
    • Parliament cannot scrutinise every detail
    • technical budget documents hinder financial oversight
    • Ordinary members lack expertise in public finance (Arts. 112, 266)
  • Executive dominance:
    • The government has the Lok Sabha majority, controls the agenda, so Parliament often ratifies rather than shapes
    • Large Houses make coordination and debate cumbersome
  • Post facto financial control:
    • PAC, Estimates Committee and COPU examine spending after it is incurred
    • The guillotine curtails debate on demands for grants
  • Delegated legislation moves detailed lawmaking to the bureaucracy
  • ordinances (Art. 123) let the President, on the Council of Ministers' advice, bypass Parliament
  • Political factors:
    • Majority support discourages criticism
    • Weak, fragmented opposition
    • Oversight is sporadic, politically motivated or symbolic
    • Decline in decorum and ethics
  • Reforms needed: committee systems, financial scrutiny, legislative processes and opposition participation.
  • Memory aid (Weak oversight): Size, Executive dominance, Post-facto control, Delegated legislation, Guillotine, Opposition weakness: "Silent Executives Pass Dull Guillotined Opposition."

Which case upheld tribunals as effective substitutes for High Courts, and which made High Court review part of the Basic Structure?
Sampath Kumar (1987) and Chandra Kumar (1997).
Who heads NALSA, SLSA, DLSA and the Taluk Committee?
CJI, High Court Chief Justice, District Judge, Senior Civil Judge.
What is the status of a Lok Adalat award?
Final and binding, deemed a civil-court decree, no appeal.
What limit applies to Permanent Lok Adalats?
Pre-litigation public utility disputes up to Rs 1 crore; no non-compoundable offences.
Features of the Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008?
Effective 2 October 2009; Nyayadhikari with JMFC powers; summary trial; offences up to two years; appeals within 30 days.
Where do Family Court appeals go?
Directly to the High Court within 30 days (Section 19); not against a consent order.
Which tier hears consumer cases above Rs 2 crore?
The National Commission (NCDRC); appeal to the Supreme Court within 30 days.
What are the three elements of Parliament?
President, Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha (Art. 79).
Which Part and Articles deal with Parliament?
Part V, Articles 79-122.
Maximum and present strength of the Rajya Sabha?
Maximum 250 (238 + 12 nominated); present 245 (229 States + 4 UTs + 12 nominated).
Maximum and present strength of the Lok Sabha?
Maximum 552 (530 + 20 + 2 Anglo-Indian); present 543 (530 + 13 UTs).
Which Amendment abolished Anglo-Indian nomination?
The 104th Amendment Act, 2020; it also extended SC/ST reservation for a decade.
Who lowered the voting age to 18?
The 61st Amendment Act, 1988 (from 21).
What did the 42nd and 84th Amendments do to seats?
42nd froze seat allocation at 1971 levels until 2000; 84th extended the freeze till 2026.
Delimitation Commissions listed in the handout?
1952, 1962, 1972, 2002.
Why was PR rejected for the Lok Sabha?
Complexity, instability, cost, weak voter-representative link, no by-elections.
How is the Rajya Sabha kept permanent?
One-third retire every two years; six-year term under the RPA, 1951; no dissolution.
Minimum age for Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha?
30 and 25 years.
What does the 106th Amendment insert?
Arts. 330A, 332A and 334A (and amends 239AA): one-third seats for women, effective after census and delimitation; 15-year sunset, rotation of seats.
What did Lily Thomas (2013) do?
Struck down Section 8(4) RPA; disqualification on conviction is immediate.
Five grounds under Art. 102(1)?
Office of profit; unsound mind; undischarged insolvent; loss of citizenship; disqualification by parliamentary law.
Who decides disqualification under Art. 103?
The President, after obtaining and acting on the Election Commission's opinion.
Pradyut Bordoloi (2001) test for office of profit?
Power of appointment and removal; remuneration; executive or administrative powers; influence under government control.
RPA disqualification periods: Sections 8, 8A, 9, 10A?
Conviction 2 years or more: from conviction plus 6 years after release; corrupt practice up to 6 years; dismissal 5 years; failure to lodge expenses 3 years.
Tenth Schedule grounds?
Voluntarily giving up membership; voting or abstaining against the whip; independent joins a party; nominated member joins after six months.
What is Kihoto Hollohan (1992)?
The Speaker's decisions under the Tenth Schedule are subject to judicial review.
Days rules for vacating a seat?
10 days (elected to both Houses), 14 days (Parliament and State Legislature), 60 days absence.
What penalty applies for sitting or voting without taking the oath?
₹500 per day.
Who is the Speaker removed by?
A resolution of a majority of all then members, with 14 days' notice; motion needs 50 members to be admitted.
Speaker's special powers?
Certifies Money Bills (Art. 110(3)), presides over joint sittings, chairs the BAC, Rules and General Purposes Committees, appoints committee chairpersons.
Who is the Rajya Sabha Chairman?
The Vice-President ex-officio (Art. 64); not a member; removed only as VP (Art. 67(b)).
What is a Speaker Pro Tem?
A newly elected member (usually senior-most) appointed by the President to administer oaths and preside until the Speaker is elected.
Source of Leader of Opposition status?
Salary and Allowances of Leaders of Opposition in Parliament Act, 1977; leader of largest opposition party with at least one-tenth strength; Cabinet Minister rank.
What is a whip's legal status?
Not in the Constitution, Rules or any statute; a convention from Britain.
Gap between two sessions?
Not more than six months (Art. 85(1)).
Who prorogues and who adjourns?
President prorogues (Art. 85(2)); the Speaker or Chairman adjourns.
What survives dissolution of the Lok Sabha?
Bills pending in the Rajya Sabha and Bills passed by both Houses awaiting Presidential assent.
Quorum in each House?
One-tenth: 55 in the Lok Sabha and 25 in the Rajya Sabha.
Effective majority is used for?
Removing the Speaker and the Vice-President.
Which motion is a calling attention?
Draws a minister's attention to an urgent matter; an Indian innovation since 1954.
Adjournment motion rules?
50 MPs in Lok Sabha; not allowed in Rajya Sabha; not less than 2 hours 30 minutes.
Four types of closure?
Simple, compartments, kangaroo, guillotine.
Short notice question?
Less than ten days' notice; oral.
Zero Hour rules?
Introduced 1962; Rule 377 (LS) and Rule 180-E (RS); raised without notice.
Ordinary Bill stages?
First reading, second reading (general discussion, committee, consideration), third reading, second House, assent.
How long can the Rajya Sabha hold a Money Bill?
14 days; then deemed passed as passed by the Lok Sabha.
Financial Bill (I) versus (II)?
(I) Art. 117(1): Lok Sabha only, recommendation before introduction. (II) Art. 117(3): either House, recommendation at consideration.
Deadlock triggers for joint sitting?
Rejection; final disagreement on amendments; no action for six months.
Joint sittings so far?
Dowry Prohibition Bill (1960), Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill (1977), POTA (2002).
Six stages of the Budget?
Presentation; general debate; Standing Committee scrutiny; voting on demands; Appropriation Bill; Finance Bill.
Cut motions?
Policy (Re 1), Economy (specific reduction), Token (₹100).
What is a vote on account?
Interim approval, typically for two months, about one-sixth of estimated expenditure.
Special grants?
Supplementary, additional, excess, vote of credit, exceptional, token.
Three government funds?
Consolidated Fund 266(1), Public Account 266(2), Contingency Fund 267.
Ways to express lack of confidence besides no-confidence?
Motion of Thanks defeated, Money Bill rejected, censure, adjournment motion, cut motion.
Modes of amendment by Parliament?
Simple majority; special majority; special majority plus half of State legislatures.
Rajya Sabha exclusive powers?
Art. 249, Art. 312, initiation of VP removal, approval of emergency when LS dissolved.
Individual privileges?
Freedom from civil arrest (40 days before and after session), freedom of speech (Art. 105), jury and witness exemption.
Which Amendment dealt with privileges and the press?
The 44th Amendment (1978): press may report true proceedings; privileges as on 20 June 1979.
Dicey's three implications of sovereignty?
Unlimited legislative power; no distinction between ordinary and constitutional law; no judicial review.
Four limits on the Indian Parliament?
Written Constitution, federal structure, judicial oversight, fundamental rights.

Close: Strengthening committees and debate time can restore the individual MP's role.
Mains 2020: Rajya Sabha has been transformed from a 'useless stepney tyre to supporting organ in past few decades. Highlight the factors as well as the areas which this transformation could be visible.
Intro: Rajya Sabha is a federal, permanent chamber with equal powers in many areas and special powers in some Body: Equal: ordinary laws, Constitution Amendment Bills, election and impeachment of the President, VP removal, judges and CEC and CAG removal recommendations, ordinances, emergency proclamations; inferior: Money Bills (14 days), budget voting, no-confidence; special: Art. 249, Art. 312, initiation of VP removal, emergency approval when the Lok Sabha is dissolved; permanence gives continuity and a revising role (GST example). Close: Rajya Sabha is a revising and federal chamber, not a rival; its special powers give it a distinct place.
Mains 2020: 'Once a Speaker, Always a Speaker'! Do you think this practice should be impart objectivity to the office of Speaker of Lok Sabha? What could be its implications for the robust functioning of parliamentary business in India?
Intro: The Speaker is from the ruling party and depends on it for re-election, so neutrality is questioned Body: For: impartiality; Nehru and Mavalankar on protecting all MPs; fair anti-defection adjudication (Telangana case); UK precedent (Hoyle re-elected unopposed 2024); against: career disincentive; weak Indian conventions; judicial overhang; risk of executive influence; reduced accountability; way forward: codify neutrality, supermajority removal, independent tribunal for defection, training; global: UK resigns party, Canada secret ballot, Australia neutrality. Close: Neutrality needs conventions and structural reform, not only a slogan.
Mains 2021: To what extent, in your view, the Parliament is able to ensure accountability of the executive in India?
Intro: Accountability rests on Arts. 75 and 77, tools of oversight and financial control; practice is weaker than theory Body: Tools: Question Hour, Zero Hour, discussions, calling attention, adjournment, censure and no-confidence motions; collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)) and individual responsibility; other signs: Motion of Thanks, Money Bill, cut motion; financial: PAC, Estimates Committee, COPU, annual grant and rule of lapse; weaknesses: size of government, executive dominance, post facto control, guillotine, delegated legislation, ordinances, weak opposition. Close: Parliament has the tools; majority dominance and procedural limits blunt them.
Mains 2021: Explain the constitutional provisions under which the Legislative Councils are established. Review the working and current status of Legislative Councils with suitable illustrations.
Intro: Art. 169 lets a State Assembly seek creation or abolition of its Council Body: Assembly passes a resolution; Parliament enacts the law to give effect to it; the State legislature cannot itself amend the Constitution (Art. 368) except for this; purpose: bicameral system at State level only where necessary; the handout has no notes on current status or illustrations. Close: Councils exist at the State's request and Parliament's law; the handout stops there.
Mains 2023: Discuss the role of presiding officers in legislatures in maintaining order and impartiality in conducting legislative work and in facilitating best democratic practices.
Intro: Presiding officers: Speaker, Deputy Speaker, Chairman, Deputy Chairman, with panels Body: Maintain order and decorum; final interpreters of Rules and precedents; casting vote only in a tie; Speaker certifies Money Bills and decides Tenth Schedule cases, now subject to judicial review; safeguards: removal by effective majority with 14 days' notice, pay charged on the Consolidated Fund, conduct not discussed except on a substantive motion; impartiality gaps: Speaker keeps party membership; reforms such as Once a Speaker Always a Speaker. Close: Impartiality rests on tenure safeguards and conventions that India is still building.
Mains 2024: 'The growth of cabinet system has practically resulted in the marginalisation of the parliamentary supremacy.' Elucidate.
Intro: Executive dominance of the legislative agenda is the core of the argument Body: Government is formed by the majority in the Lok Sabha and controls the agenda; Parliament often ratifies rather than shapes laws; ordinances (Art. 123) and delegated legislation bypass debate; guillotine and post facto committee control; the Indian Parliament is also bound by the Constitution, federal division, judicial review and rights (not sovereign like Britain); control tools: no-confidence, censure, cut motions. Close: Parliament is limited by its own executive and by the Constitution; oversight reforms can rebalance.

Traps#

Trap 1 · CAT appeals: Supreme Court or High Court? The handout says CAT appeals go directly to the Supreme Court under Section 28 of the 1985 Act, but also says Chandra Kumar (1997) made tribunals subject to High Court writ jurisdiction under Arts. 226 and 227. Rule: after Chandra Kumar, first go to the High Court (Division Bench), then the Supreme Court.
Trap 2 · What Rajya Sabha cannot do Rajya Sabha cannot bring a no-confidence motion, cannot use the adjournment motion, cannot introduce or reject a Money Bill, and cannot vote on demands for grants. Rule: money and ministry control sit with Lok Sabha; Rajya Sabha only discusses and recommends.
Trap 3 · Speaker-only powers Only the Speaker certifies a Money Bill (Art. 110(3)) and presides over a joint sitting (Art. 118). The RS Chairman does neither because he is not a member of either House. Rule: Money Bill certificate + joint sitting chair = Speaker; if absent, Deputy Speaker, then Deputy Chairman of RS.
Trap 4 · Which seat falls vacant Elected to both Houses without choosing in 10 days: the Rajya Sabha seat goes. Elected to Parliament and a State Legislature and no resignation from the State in 14 days: the Parliament seat goes. Two seats in one House with no choice: both go. Rule: 10 days = Houses, 14 days = State Legislature, 60 days = absence (House may declare vacant).
