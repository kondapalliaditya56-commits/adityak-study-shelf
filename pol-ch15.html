1 · Judicial review and the Ninth Schedule#

Judicial review is the process by which courts examine whether legislative and executive actions conform to the Constitution. It is a check on arbitrary power and upholds the supremacy of the Constitution and citizens' rights.

  • First established in Marbury v. Madison (1803) by Chief Justice John Marshall of the US Supreme Court.
  • In India the power flows directly from the Constitution and is vested in both the Supreme Court and the High Courts.
  • The Supreme Court has held judicial review is part of the basic structure: it cannot be curtailed even by a constitutional amendment.
  • It tests the validity of laws and executive orders. If they violate the Constitution they are declared ultra vires (beyond authority), null and void. It is also a legitimiser of government action that stays within limits.
  • It does not mean every law is automatically examined; it works only when a law's validity is challenged in a case or a citizen claims violation of rights. The courts are "sentinels on the qui vive".

Six uses of judicial review
SMPFIT

Supremacy of the Constitution, Misuse of power prevented, Protecting rights, Federal balance, Independence of judiciary, Tyranny of executive prevented: "Strong Men Protect Fair Independent Trials."

How the Supreme Court has widened rights through review

  • Freedom of the press under Art. 19(1)(a) though not named; protective discrimination (reservations) held compatible with equality.
  • Right to Education and Right to Clean Environment read into Art. 21; delay in trials as denial of justice; free legal aid and speedy trials as part of fundamental rights.
  • PILs and activism for bonded labourers, women, children and marginalised groups; CNG for public transport in Delhi and compulsory helmets show review as a "living instrument".

The Ninth Schedule and judicial review

  • Introduced by the First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951 with Art. 31B, under PM Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • Purpose: protect socially progressive laws (land reforms, agrarian equality, abolition of the zamindari system) from being invalidated for violating Fundamental Rights.
  • Originally 13 Acts; today 281 laws (the handout adds "of which about 66 were added before 24 April 1973").
Landmark cases on the Ninth Schedule
CaseYearPrinciple
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala1973Basic Structure Doctrine; laws violating basic structure cannot be protected by the Ninth Schedule
Waman Rao v. Union of India1981Only laws added before 24 April 1973 (the Kesavananda date) are fully protected; later additions can be judicially reviewed
I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu2007No blanket immunity; laws inserted after 1973 can be struck down for violating Arts. 14, 19, 21 or the Basic Structure

Five conclusions of I.R. Coelho (2007)

1
No law can destroy the Basic Structure, whether by amendment or inclusion in the Ninth Schedule
2
The impact and effect test: if the consequence of a law violates basic features, it is invalid
3
All Ninth Schedule additions after 24 April 1973 can be challenged under Arts. 14, 19, 21
4
Protection is conditional, not absolute; each case on its merits
5
A law already upheld cannot be re-challenged; a struck-down law cannot revive by mere inclusion in the Schedule
  • NCRWC recommendations: use the Ninth Schedule only as a last resort, for agrarian reform or Directive Principles in Arts. 39(b) and 39(c); make inclusion transparent with justification for each; set an institutional review mechanism to check periodically whether laws in the Schedule remain relevant and valid.

2 · Judicial activism and restraint#

Judicial activism is an assertive judiciary reading the law as a living instrument of social transformation, a shift from a reactive judiciary to a creative, proactive one.

Origin and constitutional basis

  • Term coined by Arthur Schlesinger Jr. in the USA in 1947.
  • In India it grew from the mid-1970s under Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer, Justice P.N. Bhagwati and Justice O. Chinnappa Reddy, especially after the Emergency (1975-77) exposed executive overreach.
  • Legitimacy from Art. 32 (SC writs for Fundamental Rights), Art. 226 (HC writs "for any other purpose"), Art. 142 (SC may pass any decree for complete justice).
Roots of activism
KBC

Krishna Iyer, Bhagwati, Chinnappa Reddy: "Keen Bold Courts."

  • Relationship: review is the foundation (Arts. 13, 32, 226); activism is its progressive form; "review ensures rule of law, activism ensures rule of justice": the first prevents misuse of power, the second fills the void when power is not used. Review is the tool; activism is the spirit.

Why activism arose

Constitutional and practical

  • Constitutional supremacy (Arts. 32, 226, 142 make the judiciary a "sentinel on the qui vive")
  • Rule of Law (Art. 14) and rebalanced separation of powers
  • transformative constitutionalism (Preamble and Part IV turned into enforceable rights such as Education and Clean Environment). Wadehra's causes: collapse of responsible governance
  • citizens' expectations
  • judicial enthusiasm and reformist zeal
  • legislative vacuum
  • constitutional scope for innovation (Art. 21, Art. 142)

Situational and philosophical

  • Kashyap's grounds: legislative paralysis
  • weak or fragmented governments
  • political expediency
  • failure to protect basic rights (Subhash Kumar v. Bihar, 1991)
  • authoritarian misuse (Emergency 1975-77)
  • populist pressures. Philosophy: justice as a living concept (Art. 38)
  • continuing mandamus (Vineet Narain, 1998)
  • conscience of the voiceless via PIL and suo motu

3 · High Courts: organisation, appointment, tenure#

The High Courts are the highest judicial authorities at State level, below the Supreme Court, with superintendence and control over all subordinate courts in their jurisdiction.

Establishment and composition

  • Art. 214: a High Court for each State. Art. 231: Parliament may set up a common High Court for two or more States, or States and a Union Territory.
  • Examples: Punjab and Haryana HC serves both States and Chandigarh; Gauhati HC serves several north-eastern States.
  • 25 High Courts (2025); Delhi, Bombay, Madras, Calcutta and Allahabad are the oldest, from British rule.
  • Art. 216: a Chief Justice and such other judges as the President deems necessary; strength varies with population, pendency and workload and can be altered.
  • Seat: usually the State capital, with permanent or circuit benches elsewhere. Examples: Bombay HC benches at Nagpur, Aurangabad, Goa; Madhya Pradesh HC at Jabalpur with benches at Indore and Gwalior.

Appointment (Art. 217) and qualifications

  • Judges are appointed by the President. For the Chief Justice: consult the CJI and the Governor. For other judges: consult the CJI, the Governor and the Chief Justice of that High Court.
  • In practice the Collegium System (Second Judges Case 1993, reaffirmed by the Third Judges Case 1998): senior Supreme Court judges headed by the CJI recommend names; the President normally follows.

4 · High Courts: powers and independence#

Every High Court has original, appellate, writ, supervisory and administrative jurisdiction plus judicial review.

1. Original jurisdiction

  • Election disputes (MPs and State legislators) under the RPA, 1951.
  • Revenue matters.
  • Enforcement of Fundamental Rights by writs (Art. 226).
  • Transfer of constitutional cases from subordinate courts with a substantial question of constitutional interpretation.
  • Original civil jurisdiction in higher-value cases: Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Delhi. Before 1973 the first three also had original criminal jurisdiction, abolished by the Criminal Procedure Code, 1973.

2. Writ jurisdiction (Art. 226)

  • Writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto.
  • Purposes: (1) enforce Fundamental Rights; (2) any other purpose (ordinary legal rights). So the High Court's jurisdiction is wider than the Supreme Court's Art. 32 and concurrent with it for Fundamental Rights.
  • It can reach persons or authorities outside its territory if the cause of action arises within it.

3. Appellate jurisdiction

4. Supervisory jurisdiction (Art. 227)

  • Superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its territory, except military courts. Powers: call for returns, frame rules and forms, regulate records and accounts, fix fees of clerks, sheriffs, officers and legal practitioners.
  • Covers judicial and administrative supervision; works as revisional jurisdiction; may be used suo motu.
  • An extraordinary power, to be used sparingly, in cases of: excess of jurisdiction; gross violation of natural justice; error of law; disregard of binding precedent; perverse findings; manifest injustice.

5. Control over subordinate courts (Arts. 233-235)

  • The Governor consults the High Court in appointment, posting and promotion of District Judges and in recruitment to the judicial service.
  • Manages posting, promotion, leave, transfer and discipline of judicial officers below District Judge.
  • May withdraw cases involving a substantial constitutional question, decide them, or return them with its ruling.
  • Its decisions bind all subordinate courts in its jurisdiction.

6. Court of Record (Art. 215)

  • Record of proceedings: permanent record and precedent value, cannot be challenged before subordinate courts.
  • Power to punish for contempt of itself and subordinate courts (civil or criminal), under the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971. It cannot take cognisance where the act is already an IPC offence.
  • Inherent power to review and correct its own judgments though not written in the Constitution.

7. Judicial review

  • Examines laws and executive orders of Centre and State; unconstitutional ones are ultra vires. Flows from Arts. 13 and 226; part of the Basic Structure. Grounds: violates Part III; beyond the maker's competence; repugnant to the Constitution.
  • The 42nd Amendment (1976) tried to bar High Courts from questioning Central laws; the 43rd Amendment (1977) restored the position.

8 · Parliament and the composition of the two Houses#

Parliament is the chief legislative authority of the Union and represents the will of the people at the national level. It works in the Westminster-pattern parliamentary system, where the executive is accountable to the legislature.

  • Part V (Articles 79-122) lays down structure, composition, tenure, offices, procedures, privileges and legislative powers of Parliament.
  • The aim is both supremacy of the legislature in lawmaking and responsible government in the Union.

Three elements of Parliament

Parliament = President + Council of States (Rajya Sabha) + House of the People (Lok Sabha).

  • The Hindi names Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha were formally adopted in the 1950s.
  • Rajya Sabha = upper chamber representing States and Union Territories. Lok Sabha = directly elected lower chamber representing the citizens.
  • The President is not a sitting member of either House, yet is an essential part: a law passed by both Houses needs the President's assent.
  • The President also summons and prorogues sessions, dissolves the Lok Sabha, addresses the Houses and promulgates ordinances when Parliament is not in session.

  • Executive is embedded in the legislature ("President-in-Parliament")
  • President is part of Parliament
  • accountability of executive to legislature
  • not strict separation of powers

USA

  • President is a separate, non-legislative head
  • the legislature (Congress) has only two Houses (Senate and House of Representatives)

Composition of the two Houses

Both Houses are built differently: one indirect and federal, the other direct and popular.

Rajya Sabha (Council of States)

  • Sanctioned maximum 250: up to 238 represent States and UTs, 12 are nominated by the President for eminence in literature, science, art and social service.
  • Currently 245: 229 from States, 4 from UTs, 12 nominated. Seats are allotted by the Fourth Schedule.
  • States: members are indirectly elected by the elected members of the State Legislative Assemblies, by proportional representation through the single transferable vote. Seats are broadly population-based (larger States more, smaller States like Tripura have one member), unlike the US Senate where every State has equal representation.
  • Union Territories: also indirect, by a specially constituted electoral college for each UT, same STV method. Only UTs with sufficient population or legislative arrangements are represented.
  • Nominated: 12 persons with distinguished contribution in arts, science, literature and social service; they enrich debate without contesting elections.
  • Result: a federal chamber with indirect, population-weighted representation plus a small expert component.

Lok Sabha (House of the People)

  • Constitution fixes the maximum at 552: 530 from States, 20 from UTs, 2 Anglo-Indians nominated by the President.
  • 104th Amendment Act, 2020 abolished Anglo-Indian nomination and extended SC/ST seat reservation for another decade.
  • Currently 543 elected: 530 from States, 13 from UTs.
  • States: directly elected from territorial constituencies by universal adult franchise. Voting age 18 years, reduced from 21 by the 61st Amendment Act, 1988. Seats are broadly population-based; boundaries are revised by Delimitation Commissions.
  • Union Territories: Parliament decides how UT members are chosen; the Union Territories (Direct Election to the House of the People) Act, 1965 provides direct election. Larger UTs like Delhi get more seats.
  • Nominated (historical): until 2020 up to two Anglo-Indian members if the community lacked representation; initially temporary, periodically extended until the 104th Amendment ended it.

9 · Electoral system of the Lok Sabha#

Framework: Articles 81, 82 and 170, refined by amendments and delimitation. Principles: territorial representation, population-based seat allocation, universal adult franchise.

Territorial constituencies and parity

  • Each State is divided into single-member territorial constituencies.
  • Parity between States: ratio of population to seats broadly uniform (Art. 81(1)); States with population below six million are exempt from strict proportionality.
  • Parity within a State: constituencies carry roughly equal population.
  • "Population" = figures of the latest decennial census.

Delimitation and the freeze

  • Art. 82 lets Parliament readjust constituencies after each census, through a Delimitation Commission. Commissions: 1952, 1962, 1972, 2002.
  • 42nd Amendment (1976) froze inter-State seat allocation and boundaries at 1971 levels until 2000, to encourage population control.
  • 84th Amendment (2001) extended the freeze till 2026; it also allowed rationalising boundaries on population changes (initially 1991 census, updated to 2001 by the 87th Amendment) without altering the total seats per State.
  • Purpose: protect States that have controlled population while keeping proportional representation.

SC and ST reservation (Article 330)

  • Reserved constituencies in the Lok Sabha in proportion to SC/ST population in each State.
  • Reserved seats are delimited after each census, by the same method as general seats.
  • Common electorate: all voters vote, but candidates must belong to the reserved category. SC/ST members may also contest general seats.
  • Reservation was meant for ten years from the Constitution; extended repeatedly, most recently by the 95th Amendment Act (2009) and continued under the 104th Amendment Act (2020), which also ended Anglo-Indian nomination.

Voting method: First-Past-the-Post (FPTP)

  • Single-member constituency; highest votes wins.
  • Merits: direct accountability of MP to constituents; simple; helps stable government formation.
  • Rajya Sabha uses PR with STV (Articles 80 and 66); Lok Sabha prefers simplicity, local representation and clear outcomes.

Problems of PR

  • Complexity for a largely illiterate electorate
  • party fragmentation and unstable coalitions
  • costly and administratively complex
  • voters choose parties not candidates
  • no by-elections (vacancies cannot be filled by individual contests)

What FPTP gives

  • Direct accountability
  • constituency-based representation
  • stability
  • reservation and delimitation correct the representation of marginalised communities

10 · Duration and continuity of the two Houses#

Provisions mainly in Articles 80, 83, 85 and 352, with the Representation of the People Act, 1951.

Rajya Sabha: the permanent chamber

  • First constituted in 1952; not subject to dissolution.
  • One-third retire every two years; vacancies are filled by State Assembly election or by Presidential nomination (Art. 80(3)).
  • Six-year term under the RPA, 1951; the Constitution itself prescribes no fixed term.
  • Retiring members can be re-elected or re-nominated indefinitely.
  • In the first Rajya Sabha the members to retire at the end of the initial term were chosen by lottery to start the rotation.
  • Role: revising chamber, check on hasty or populist measures.

Lok Sabha: the temporary house

  • Normal term five years from its first sitting (Art. 83(2)); it then dissolves automatically.
  • The President on the advice of the Council of Ministers can dissolve it earlier (loss of majority or instability). Such dissolution is not subject to judicial review (handout's statement).
  • Emergency extension: during a national emergency (Art. 352), Parliament may extend its term by law, one year at a time, but not beyond six months after the emergency has ceased.

Why the terms differ (rationale)

1. Stability versus accountability: permanence of the Rajya Sabha shields legislation from short-term swings; the five-year Lok Sabha term lets voters judge the government at regular intervals, with early dissolution if the government loses majority. 2. Federal versus popular representation: Rajya Sabha gives continuity to regional voices (smaller States keep a voice); Lok Sabha carries the direct popular mandate. 3. Adaptation to extraordinary circumstances: Lok Sabha's term flexes in an emergency (Arts. 83(2), 352); Rajya Sabha needs no such provision. 4. Synergy: Rajya Sabha anchors stability, expertise and federal representation; Lok Sabha ensures responsiveness and adaptability. Even if the Lok Sabha is dissolved or in a crisis, Rajya Sabha keeps legislative functioning.

  • Handout's illustration: GST needed long debate aligning federal and State views, aided by the Rajya Sabha's stable composition. Laws with State implications (land acquisition, environmental rules) gain from the Rajya Sabha's federal view.

Statutory qualifications (RPA, 1951)

  • Candidate must be a registered elector in any parliamentary constituency in India.
  • Pre-2003: a Rajya Sabha candidate had to be an elector in the same State. The Representation of the People (Amendment) Act, 2003 removed this; the Supreme Court in 2006 upheld it as not violating the federal character and as broadening democratic choice.
  • Reserved constituencies: only an SC/ST person may contest a seat reserved for SC/ST, but an SC/ST candidate may also contest a general seat.
  • Procedural requirements: proper nomination papers, a prescribed security deposit, self-declarations of educational qualifications, assets and liabilities, and criminal antecedents.

Special and supplementary provisions

  • Art. 75(5): a non-MP can be a minister if elected to either House within six months; otherwise must step down.
  • Art. 80(1)(a): President nominates up to 12 members to the Rajya Sabha (literature, science, art, social service); they must still meet citizenship and allegiance conditions.
  • Eligibility is judged at the time of nomination or election. Challenges come at scrutiny of nominations by the returning officer or later by election petition under Part VI of the RPA; the Election Commission oversees compliance.

4. Absence (Article 101(4))

  • A member absent from all sittings for 60 consecutive days without permission may have the seat declared vacant by the House.
  • Days when the House is prorogued or adjourned for more than four consecutive days are excluded.
  • Leave is granted by the House (ill health, official duties abroad).

5. Other situations

  • Election declared void by a High Court under the RPA (appeal to the SC under Section 116A).
  • Expulsion by the House for conduct unbecoming (Arts. 105 and 122); handout example: the 2005 "cash-for-query" scandal.
  • Elected President (Art. 58) or Vice-President (Art. 66): must vacate the seat.
  • Appointed Governor: Art. 158(2): a Governor cannot be a member of either House.

6. Election of a disqualified candidate

  • Constitution lays down no procedure; the RPA does. Under Sections 80-84 an election petition goes to the High Court, which can declare the election void. The Supreme Court under Art. 136 is the final appellate authority.

14 · Presiding officers: the Speaker and Deputy Speaker#

Each House has its own presiding officers to ensure smooth, impartial and efficient proceedings. Lok Sabha: Speaker, assisted by the Deputy Speaker. Rajya Sabha: Chairman (the Vice President), assisted by the Deputy Chairman. Each House also keeps a panel of chairpersons or vice-chairpersons to preside in their absence.

Speaker of the Lok Sabha: election and tenure

  • Elected by Lok Sabha members from among themselves, as soon as possible after the House's first sitting; the President fixes the date of the election.
  • Office falls vacant if the Speaker: (1) ceases to be a member; (2) resigns in writing to the Deputy Speaker; (3) is removed by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members (effective majority), with 14 days' prior notice.
  • Normally holds office for the life of the House (five years) but continues after dissolution until a new Speaker is elected by the new House.
  • A removal motion can be admitted only if supported by at least 50 members.

  • Head of the Lok Sabha, principal spokesperson, guardian of its privileges and procedures. Authority comes from the Constitution, the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business, and conventions.

  • Presides over joint sittings convened by the President under Art. 108.
  • May permit a secret sitting on the request of the Leader of the House.
  • His certification of a Money Bill under Art. 110 is final and binding; it is endorsed before transmission to the Rajya Sabha or President.
  • Decides defection disqualification under the Tenth Schedule; Kihoto Hollohan (1992) made this subject to judicial review.
  • Ex-officio Chairman of the Indian Parliamentary Group and of the Conference of Presiding Officers of Legislative Bodies in India.
  • Appoints chairpersons of all Lok Sabha committees; himself chairs the Business Advisory Committee, Rules Committee and General Purposes Committee.

Independence and impartiality

  • Security of tenure: removable only by a resolution of a majority of the total membership, motion admitted with 50 members' support.
  • Salary fixed by Parliament under the 1954 Act, charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (no annual vote); stated at ₹4,00,000 per month (revised by the 2018 Rules).
  • Conduct and decisions cannot be discussed in the House except by a substantive motion; his rulings on procedure and discipline are beyond the courts (privilege, Art. 122).
  • Votes only to break a tie.
  • Order of precedence (Ministry of Home Affairs): rank seven, with the Chief Justice of India, above all Cabinet Ministers except the PM and Deputy PM.
  • UK convention: Speaker resigns from party on election. In India the Speaker retains party membership, yet is expected to rise above party.
Why "Speaker"?

He hardly speaks in a session or gives views on a Bill; he speaks only to tell members to sit down. The name comes from Britain, where the Speaker summed up the views of members and communicated them to the King.

"Once a Speaker, Always a Speaker"

Why India should consider it

  • Speaker is from the ruling party and depends on it for re-election
  • protects all MPs' rights (Nehru, Mavalankar)
  • fair anti-defection adjudication (SC ordered Telangana Speaker to decide on 10 defecting MLAs within three months)
  • UK precedent (Sir Lindsay Hoyle re-elected unopposed in 2024)

Challenges

  • Career disincentive and shrinking talent pool
  • weak neutrality conventions in India
  • judicial overhang
  • risk of executive influence over an isolated Speaker
  • reduced accountability
  • Way forward (handout): codify neutrality by convention or law (resign from party, no re-entry into active politics, supermajority removal); transfer anti-defection adjudication to an independent tribunal; build UK-like conventions (the opposition does not contest the Speaker's seat); training in constitutional law and procedure.
  • Global practice: UK Speaker resigns party and is re-elected across governments; Canada Speaker chosen by secret ballot; Australia Speaker party-affiliated but neutral, votes only to break ties.
  • Mavalankar (first Lok Sabha Speaker): the Speaker "belongs to all the members or belongs to none". Nehru: the Speaker is "the symbol of the nation's freedom and liberty".

Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha

  • Elected by the House itself under Art. 93, after the Speaker's election; the Speaker fixes the date.
  • Holds office for the life of the House; vacates if he ceases to be a member, resigns in writing to the Speaker, or is removed by a majority of all the then members after 14 days' notice.
  • Acts as Speaker when the Speaker is absent or the office vacant, with all powers, including presiding over a joint sitting. He is not subordinate to the Speaker; he is directly responsible to the House.
  • Unique feature: whenever appointed a member of a parliamentary committee he automatically becomes its chairman.
  • Does not vote first, casting vote in a tie. During his own removal proceedings he does not preside but may speak and take part in debate.
  • While the Speaker presides he is an ordinary member (debates and votes).
  • Pay: fixed by Parliament, charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
  • Convention: up to the 10th Lok Sabha both Speaker and Deputy Speaker were usually from the ruling party; since the 11th Lok Sabha the Speaker is from the ruling party and the Deputy Speaker is typically from the main opposition.

15 · Chairman, Deputy Chairman and leaders in Parliament#

Chairman of the Rajya Sabha

  • Art. 64: the Vice-President is ex-officio Chairman. This links the executive and legislative organs.
  • Art. 65(2): when the VP acts as President or discharges the President's functions he does not perform the Chairman's duties.
  • Unlike the Speaker, the Chairman is not a member of the Rajya Sabha; so no vote in the first instance, only a casting vote in a tie (Art. 100(1)).
  • Similar to the Speaker: presides, decides points of order, interprets provisions. Two powers belong only to the Speaker: deciding a Money Bill (Art. 110(3)) and presiding over a joint sitting (Art. 118).
  • Removal only by removal as Vice-President (Art. 67(b)): resolution of the Rajya Sabha by an effective majority, agreed to by the Lok Sabha.
  • Pay under the 1953 Act, Consolidated Fund; ₹4,00,000 per month, with housing, travel and medical facilities equal to the Speaker.
  • Art. 65(3): when VP acts as President he gets the President's emoluments, not the Chairman's.
  • Art. 90(b): while a resolution for his removal is under consideration he cannot preside, though he may speak and take part, without voting.

Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha

  • Created by Art. 89(2); the regular day-to-day presiding officer; elected from among members by a simple majority of members present and voting.
  • Holds office while a member; vacates (Art. 90) if he ceases to be a member, resigns in writing to the Chairman, or is removed by a majority of all the then members after 14 days' notice.
  • When presiding has the Chairman's powers; no vote first, casting vote in a tie; may represent the Rajya Sabha in State functions or delegations.
  • Art. 97: pay of Chairman and Deputy Chairman fixed by Parliament, Consolidated Fund.

Panel of Vice-Chairpersons (Rajya Sabha)

  • Chairman nominates it under the Rules; any member presides in the absence of both Chairman and Deputy Chairman. It is a procedural, not constitutional arrangement.
  • If both offices are vacant, no panel member can preside; under Art. 91(1) the President appoints a Rajya Sabha member to perform the Chairman's duties until elections.

Speaker resigns to Deputy Speaker; Deputy Speaker to Speaker. Chairman's deputy resigns to Chairman. A deputy always receives the principal's resignation, and the principal receives the deputy's.

  • Each House has its own independent secretariat to assist presiding officers and members; some posts (security, translation, documentation) may be common.

Leaders in Parliament

These offices come from Rules, statutes and conventions, not from an explicit constitutional mention.

Leader of the House

  • Rule 2 of the Lok Sabha Rules: the Prime Minister if a Lok Sabha member; if the PM is in the Rajya Sabha he nominates another minister who is a Lok Sabha member. In the Rajya Sabha: a minister who is a member of that House, appointed by the PM.
  • Sets the schedule of government Bills, motions and discussions with the presiding officer; chief government spokesperson; works with the Speaker or Chairman to keep order and consensus.
  • The PM may nominate a Deputy Leader of the House. The US equivalent is the Majority Leader.

Leader of the Opposition (LoP)

  • Leader of the largest opposition party with at least one-tenth of the House's strength. Statutory recognition by the Salary and Allowances of Leaders of Opposition in Parliament Act, 1977 (first time).
  • First recognised in 1969; formally in both Houses since 1977.
  • Functions: constructive criticism; alternative policy framework ("government in waiting"); accountability through debates, questions and motions; member of Selection Committees for the CVC, CBI Director, Lokpal.
  • Rank, salary and allowances equal to a Cabinet Minister, charged on the Consolidated Fund.
  • UK: Shadow Cabinet; Ivor Jennings called the LoP an "alternative Prime Minister".

Party Whip

  • No mention in the Constitution, Rules or any statute: a convention from Britain. Every party appoints a Whip in both Houses.
  • Duties: attendance in important debates and votes; direct voting along the party line (confidence motions, Money Bills (Art. 110), Constitution amendments (Art. 368)); discipline; informing members through whip notices.
  • Defying the Whip can lead to disqualification under the Tenth Schedule (52nd Amendment, 1985); decided by the Speaker/Chairman under Paragraph 6, subject to judicial review under Art. 136 or 226.

16 · Sessions, quorum, voting and majorities#

Under Art. 85(1) the President summons each House from time to time, so that no more than six months elapse between two sessions. Parliament therefore meets at least twice a year; typically three sessions.

The three sessions

  • A session begins with the first sitting and runs until prorogation or, for the Lok Sabha, dissolution. The gap between sessions is recess (no business).
  • Each session has many sittings, usually a morning sitting (11 am-1 pm) and an afternoon sitting (2 pm-6 pm). The Speaker or Chairman controls adjournment of sittings.

Four terms that confuse

  • Adjournment: suspends a sitting for a set time (hours, days, weeks) without ending the session.
  • Adjournment sine die: suspends the sitting without fixing a date; the presiding officer can reconvene before or after.
  • Prorogation: formal end of a session (ends the sitting and the session). The presiding officer declares adjournment sine die, but the President prorogues under Art. 85(2).
  • Dissolution: only the Lok Sabha; ends its life irrevocably (Art. 83(2)), automatically after five years (extendable in a national emergency under Art. 352) or prematurely by the President on the Council of Ministers' advice. The Rajya Sabha is permanent.

Who ends what

Speaker/Chairman = sitting (adjourn, suspend). President = session (prorogue) and House (dissolve).

Comparison with other systems

  • UK House of Commons: strong conventions and Speaker's neutrality ensure debate proceeds smoothly without frequent suspensions. Canada: House Leaders' meeting schedules business (collective ownership), unlike India's presiding-officer-centric model.

Quorum (Article 100(3))

Voting procedure

  • Generally majority of members present and voting, excluding the presiding officer; casting vote only on a tie.

Stages of an ordinary Bill (Art. 111 on assent)

From introduction to Act

1. First reading
Introduced by a minister or private member after leave of the House; title and objectives read; no debate; then published in the Gazette. If already gazetted before introduction, leave is not required
2. Second reading
The critical stage with three sub-stages: general discussion (principles; may take it up at once, send to select/joint committee, or seek public opinion); committee stage (clause by clause, report to the House); consideration stage (vote on each clause; amendments incorporated)
3. Third reading
The Bill as a whole; only accept or reject; no amendments; majority of members present and voting passes it
4. Second House
First, second and third readings again; four options: pass without amendments; pass with amendments and return; reject; take no action
Deadlock
Disagreement, or no action for six months: President may summon a joint sitting (Art. 108); majority there passes it
5. President's assent
Grant, withhold, or return for reconsideration (not for Money Bills); if passed again by both Houses the President must assent (suspensive veto)

Money Bill (Article 110)

  • A special class of Bills dealing exclusively with government finance. Art. 110 gives an exhaustive list of matters that make a Bill a Money Bill, singly or together:
  • imposition, alteration or abolition of taxes;
  • regulation of borrowing by the Union;
  • custody, payment into and withdrawal from the Consolidated and Contingency Funds; appropriation from the Consolidated Fund;
  • declaring or increasing expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund;
  • receipt or custody of public money, or audit of Union/State accounts.
  • Provisions only incidentally connected may still make it a Money Bill. Fines, licence or service fees, and local taxes for local purposes are excluded from being Money Bills merely on that account.
  • Speaker's certificate (Art. 110(3)) settles any dispute and is final; it is endorsed when the Bill goes to the Rajya Sabha or the President.
  • Introduced only in the Lok Sabha, on the President's recommendation (Arts. 110 and 117).
  • Rajya Sabha (Art. 109): can only recommend; must return the Bill within 14 days. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject any or all recommendations; if it rejects them, the Bill is deemed passed in the original form. If the Rajya Sabha does not return it within 14 days, it is deemed passed in the form sent by the Lok Sabha.
  • Assent (Art. 111): the President may give or withhold assent; cannot return it.
  • Why special: control of taxation and spending rests with the directly elected house; avoids delay in budgetary work; predictable timetable for fiscal measures. Classification is contentious because it curtails the Rajya Sabha.

Joint sitting of the two Houses (Article 108)

An extraordinary device to break a deadlock between the Houses; balances bicameral scrutiny with decisive lawmaking.

When can a joint sitting be summoned?

Deadlock arises
(a) the other House rejects the Bill; (b) the Houses finally disagree on amendments; (c) the other House takes no action for six months from receipt (time prorogued or adjourned for more than four consecutive days is excluded)
President summons
Discretionary but within constitutional limits and conventions
Vote
A Bill is deemed passed if a majority of members present and voting in the joint sitting approve; in practice favours the larger Lok Sabha

Rules of the joint sitting

  • Presiding: the Speaker; if absent, the Deputy Speaker; if both unavailable, the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. The Chairman of the Rajya Sabha does not preside (he is not a member of either House).
  • Lok Sabha Rules govern the conduct. Quorum = one-tenth of the total membership of both Houses together.
  • Scope: only ordinary Bills and financial Bills; not Money Bills (Lok Sabha overrides) and not Constitution Amendment Bills (each House must pass separately under Art. 368).
  • Amendments: no genuinely new amendments, except those that were the precise cause of the deadlock or those needed because of delay.
  • Once the President gives notice of summoning, neither House can proceed further with the Bill.
  • If the Lok Sabha is dissolved after the notice but before the sitting, the joint sitting can still be held; if dissolved before the notice, the Bill lapses and no sitting can be summoned.

23 · Parliamentary privileges#

Privileges are special rights, immunities and exemptions of the Houses, their committees and members, protecting independence, dignity and functioning. They extend to persons entitled to take part in proceedings, such as the Attorney General and Union Ministers (Art. 105). They do not extend to the President, though he is part of Parliament.

Collective privileges

  • Publication of proceedings: each House controls publication. The 44th Amendment Act, 1978 restored the press's freedom to report true proceedings without prior permission, except in secret sittings.
  • Exclude strangers and hold secret sessions on sensitive matters of national importance.
  • Regulate internal procedure: frame rules, conduct business, adjudicate procedural disputes.
  • Punish breach of privilege (members and outsiders): reprimand, admonition, imprisonment, suspension, expulsion.
  • Immediate information of arrest, detention or conviction of a member.
  • Summon witnesses and papers; hold inquiries.
  • Judicial immunity: courts cannot question proceedings (Art. 122).
  • Legal protection in the House: no one, member or outsider, can be arrested or served legal process within the precincts without the presiding officer's permission.

Individual privileges

  • Freedom from arrest in civil matters: during the session and 40 days before and after; only civil cases, not criminal or preventive detention.
  • Freedom of speech in Parliament, including votes and speeches in committees (Art. 105); no court proceedings for statements in the House, subject to the Constitution and rules.
  • Exemption from jury service and the right to refuse to appear as a witness in court cases during sessions.

  • Breach of privilege = violating the rights or immunities of members individually or the House collectively; punishable by the House (Art. 105).
  • Contempt of the House = any act or omission that obstructs the House, members or officers or undermines the House's dignity, authority and honour. It is broader: e.g. disobedience of a lawful order of the House is contempt though not a specific breach of privilege.

24 · Limits on Parliament: sovereignty and weak oversight#

Parliamentary sovereignty: Britain

  • In Britain Parliament is the supreme legal authority: it can enact, amend or repeal any law without legal limit.
  • A.V. Dicey: three implications: (1) unlimited legislative power (De Lolme: Parliament "can do everything except make a woman a man and a man a woman"); (2) no legal distinction between ordinary and constitutional law, so no separate constituent authority; (3) no judicial review: courts cannot invalidate Acts.

Why parliamentary oversight is weak in practice

  • Size and complexity of government machinery: Parliament cannot scrutinise every detail; technical budget documents hinder financial oversight; ordinary members lack expertise in public finance (Arts. 112, 266).
  • Executive dominance: the government has the Lok Sabha majority, controls the agenda, so Parliament often ratifies rather than shapes; large Houses make coordination and debate cumbersome.
  • Post facto financial control: PAC, Estimates Committee and COPU examine spending after it is incurred; the guillotine curtails debate on demands for grants.
  • Delegated legislation moves detailed lawmaking to the bureaucracy; ordinances (Art. 123) let the President, on the Council of Ministers' advice, bypass Parliament.
  • Political factors: majority support discourages criticism; weak, fragmented opposition; oversight is sporadic, politically motivated or symbolic; decline in decorum and ethics.
  • Reforms needed: committee systems, financial scrutiny, legislative processes and opposition participation.

Recall sheet#

Read once, hide it, then rebuild the chapter from memory.

  1. Judicial review: Art. 13 base, Arts. 32 and 226 doors, ultra vires, part of basic structure; Marbury 1803; Gopalan 1950 versus Maneka 1978.
  2. Ninth Schedule: First Amendment 1951, Art. 31B, 13 Acts then 281 laws; Kesavananda 1973, Waman Rao 1981, Coelho 2007 (impact and effect test).
  3. Activism: Schlesinger 1947; Krishna Iyer, Bhagwati, Chinnappa Reddy; Arts. 32, 226, 142; fears; Aravali 2007; restraint rooted in Montesquieu.
  4. High Court: Arts. 214, 216, 217, 219, 221, 231; 25 HCs; age 62; removal 100 LS or 50 RS; transfer Art. 222; Arts. 223, 224, 224A.
  5. HC powers: original (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, Delhi civil), Art. 226 writs, appeals, Art. 227 (not military courts), Art. 215 court of record, judicial review.
  6. Subordinate courts: District Judge (Governor with HC, 7 years' advocate), Sessions death sentence confirmed by HC, CJM 7 years, First Class 3 years, Second Class 1 year.
  7. Tribunals: 42nd Amendment, Arts. 323-A (Parliament only, service matters) and 323-B (Parliament and States, 8 subjects); CAT 4 years, 70 and 67; Chandra Kumar 1997.
  8. Lok Adalat: Act 1987, Sec 19, chairperson plus two members, award decree no appeal; PLA 22A-22E, public utilities, up to ₹1 crore; NALSA CJI, SLSA High Court CJ, DLSA District Judge.
  9. Gram Nyayalaya: Act 2008, 2 October 2009, Sec 3, Nyayadhikari JMFC, up to 2 years, 30 days appeal. Family Court Act 1984: Sec 7, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14, 19. Consumer: ₹50 lakh, ₹2 crore.
  10. Parliament = President + Rajya Sabha + Lok Sabha (Art. 79); Part V Arts. 79-122.
  11. RS: max 250 (238 + 12 nominated), now 245 (229 + 4 + 12); 6 years; 1/3 retire every 2 years; STV; no dissolution; age 30.
  12. LS: max 552 (530 + 20 + 2), now 543 (530 + 13); 5 years; FPTP; age 25; voting age 18 (61st Amendment, 1988).
  13. Freeze of seats: 1971 levels till 2000 (42nd), till 2026 (84th); Delimitation Commissions 1952, 1962, 1972, 2002.
  14. Art. 102 grounds: office of profit, unsound mind, undischarged insolvent, citizenship, parliamentary law; Art. 103 decided by President on EC opinion.
  15. Tenth Schedule: 52nd Amendment 1985; four grounds; Presiding Officer decides; Kihoto Hollohan 1992.
  16. Vacating seats: 10 days (both Houses), 14 days (Parliament and State Legislature), 60 days absence, Rule 240 resignation.
  17. Speaker: 14 days notice, effective majority, 50 members; Money Bill Art. 110(3); presides at joint sitting; casting vote only.
  18. Chairman: VP ex-officio (Art. 64); not a member; removed only as VP (Art. 67(b)). Deputy Chairman Art. 89(2).
  19. LoP: one-tenth strength, 1977 Act, Cabinet Minister rank. Whip: convention only.
  20. Sessions: gap under six months (Art. 85(1)); adjournment, suspension, prorogation (President), dissolution (LS).
  21. Quorum one-tenth: 55 in LS, 25 in RS. Majorities: simple, absolute, effective, special.
  22. Money Bill: Art. 110; LS only; RS 14 days; Speaker certifies; no joint sitting; President cannot return it.
  23. Financial Bill (I) Art. 117(1) LS only; (II) Art. 117(3) either House. Joint sitting Art. 108: three used (1960, 1977, 2002).
  24. Budget: Art. 112; 1 February; six stages; cut motions (policy Re 1, token Rs 100); vote on account 2 months; Finance Bill 75 days.
  25. Funds: Consolidated 266(1), Public Account 266(2), Contingency 267. Special grants: six kinds.
  26. Privileges: Art. 105 and 122; 40 days civil arrest; 44th Amendment; breach versus contempt.
  27. Limits: written Constitution, federalism, judicial review, fundamental rights.
