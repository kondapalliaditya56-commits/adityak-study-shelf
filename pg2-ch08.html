1 · Article 19(1)(g) · Dance BARS · Online Gaming ACT 2025#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 3-4. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Citizens may practise any trade or profession, but Art 19(6) lets the State restrict it in the public interest, prescribe qualifications, or take over a trade. Dance bars show a blanket ban is disproportionate; online gaming shows skill vs chance and Union vs State power.

Article 19(1)(g) AND 19(6)

Right and restrictions

  • 19(1)(g): practise any profession, carry on any occupation, trade or business
  • 19(6): reasonable restrictions in the interests of the general public
  • State monopoly: partial or complete State monopoly in a trade, business, industry or service
  • professional qualifications: State may prescribe minimum technical or professional qualifications
  • public-interest limits on trades like liquor and arms
Note

State of Maharashtra v. Indian Hotel and Restaurants Association (2013): SC overturned the Mumbai dance-bar ban (adverse moral and social effects). Many women depend on the work for livelihood. Regulation is fine, a discriminatory or disproportionate blanket ban is not.

Skill-based Gaming AND Online Gaming

Framework

  • links profession, public morality, technology and federalism
  • Art 19(1)(g) and 19(6)
  • Entry 34, State List: betting and gambling is a State subject
  • R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala (1957):
    • Competitions of substantial skill are not gambling
    • Rummy and some fantasy formats were treated as games of skill
    • Pure chance gets no protection
  • Karnataka and Tamil Nadu High Courts struck down blanket bans on online skill games as disproportionate
  • The position then changed with the 2025 central law

Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025 (verify)

  • national framework to prohibit online money games and promote e-sports and online social games
  • three categories:
    • E-sports
    • Online social games (no prohibited stakes)
    • Online money games (money stakes for monetary gain, whether skill or chance)
  • Bans offering, advertising and facilitating money games
  • Banks and payment gateways cannot process them
  • An Authority on Online Gaming
  • Blocking powers
  • penalties:
    • Offering: up to 3 years and ₹1 crore
    • Advertising: up to 2 years and ₹50 lakh
    • Search, seizure, corporate liability
Why the ActConcerns
addiction and financial lossoverbroad ban may push users to offshore sites
youth vulnerabilityfederalism: betting is a State subject
fraud, rigged algorithmsjobs and investment hit
money launderingskill vs chance: proportionality for games once held skill-based
State-by-State fragmentationenforcement against offshore platforms and payment channels
Way forward / key point

Way forward: strengthen enforcement against offshore platforms; promote e-sports and social gaming; get judicial clarity on federalism and proportionality under 19(1)(g).

2 · Article 20 · Protection IN Conviction · Self-incrimination#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 4-5. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Art 20 has three shields against arbitrary punishment, for citizens and non-citizens, and cannot be suspended in an Emergency. Art 20(3) bars compelled testimony: Selvi (2010) says narco, polygraph and brain mapping cannot be forced, but blood, DNA and fingerprints can be taken.

Three Safeguards

ClauseProtection
20(1)ex post facto criminal laws
20(2)double jeopardy
20(3)self-incrimination
Value addition

Art 20 applies to citizens and non-citizens and cannot be suspended even during a National Emergency.

Memory aid · EDS

Art 20: Ex post facto · Double jeopardy · Self-incrimination (in clause order). (own mnemonic)

Article 20(3): Self-incrimination

Meaning

  • "No person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself"
  • Protects against compelled testimonial evidence
  • No forced incriminating statements

Scientific techniques

  • Narcoanalysis (sodium pentothal)
  • Polygraph (lie detector)
  • Brain mapping
Note

Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010): these tests cannot be forcibly administered; involuntary use violates Art 20(3) (where applicable) and Art 21. With voluntary and informed consent and safeguards they may be done, but statements are not automatically admissible; material discovered through them may be admissible under evidence rules.

Physical vs testimonial

  • lawful collection of blood, DNA, fingerprints, specimen signatures or handwriting, identification parades is generally allowed without consent
  • Key distinction: 20(3) bars compelled testimony, not every collection of physical or identifying evidence

3 · Article 21 · Golden Triangle · Livelihood · Health · Menstrual Health#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 5-7. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Arts 14, 19 and 21 are the Golden Triangle. Art 21 moved from procedure established by law (Gopalan) to fair, just and reasonable procedure (Maneka), and from a negative duty to positive duties: livelihood (Olga Tellis), health, menstrual health. A "canopy" of rights.

Foundation

Value addition

Golden Triangle: Arts 14, 19, 21. Even if other Part III rights were removed, much protection could be deduced from these three (equality, freedom, life and personal liberty).

Text and early reading

  • "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law"
  • First read as protection from executive, not legislative, tyranny
  • The law's reasonableness is not mentioned
  1. 1950
    A. K. Gopalan: narrow reading of personal liberty and procedure established by law
  2. 1978
    Maneka Gandhi: procedure must be fair, just and reasonable, not arbitrary or oppressive
  3. Later
    livelihood, health, shelter, privacy, dignity, environment, access to justice

Negative to positive obligation

  • Life once meant only physical existence
  • The State's duty has grown from not interfering to protecting and preserving life (health, shelter, education, environment)
  • rights read into 21:
    • Dignity, livelihood, shelter
    • Health, emergency aid, menstrual health
    • Clean environment and climate
    • Privacy, autonomy, reputation
    • Fair and speedy trial, legal aid
    • Protection from custodial violence and inhuman prison conditions
    • Peaceful sleep
  • Art 21 is a canopy of rights

Livelihood AND Shelter

Note

Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985): pavement and slum dwellers evicted as encroachers. Held: right to life includes right to livelihood; eviction needs fair and lawful procedure. It did not create an absolute right to alternative housing. Linked Part III with Part IV, bridging civil-political and socio-economic rights.

Health AND Menstrual Health

Right to health

  • access to healthcare and emergency medical aid is part of life and dignity
  • positive duty, extent depends on constitutional standards, laws and resources

Right to menstrual health

  • manage menstruation safely, affordably, with dignity: clean toilets, sanitary products, medical support, no stigma
  • Linked to life, dignity, health, education
  • SC stressed sanitary products, private clean toilets and safe disposal in schools
  • mandatory menstrual leave: the Court left it to policy, not a nationwide judicial order
Policy dilemmaPractical solutions
severe pain (e.g. endometriosis): no one should be forced to work through itflexible hours, work from home
mandatory employer-paid leave may cause hiring biasclean rest rooms, private sanitation, pain relief
school sanitary products, running water, safe disposal

4 · Article 21 · Clean Environment · Climate Right · Ranjitsinh (2024)#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 7-8. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Courts read a clean environment into Art 21 and added the polluter-pays, precautionary, public-trust and sustainable-development principles. M. K. Ranjitsinh (2024) recognised a right against climate change under Arts 14 and 21 and balanced bustard conservation with renewable energy.

Clean Environment

  1. 1986
    M. C. Mehta (Oleum gas leak): absolute liability
  2. 1991
    Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar: pollution-free water and air under Art 21
  3. 1996
    Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum: precautionary principle and polluter pays part of sustainable development

Four principles

  • polluter pays: the polluter bears the cost
  • precautionary: anticipate and prevent harm
  • public trust: natural resources are held in trust by the State for the public
  • sustainable development: balance growth and protection
Memory aid · PPPS

Environment principles: Polluter pays · Precautionary · Public trust · Sustainable development. (own mnemonic)

Applications

  • firecracker air pollution
  • vehicular emissions in Delhi
  • river pollution oversight by courts and tribunals

Right Against Climate Change

Note

M. K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India (2024): case on the Great Indian Bustard hit by overhead power lines in Rajasthan and Gujarat. Recognised a fundamental right against adverse effects of climate change under Arts 14 and 21; replaced blanket line restrictions with an expert-driven, calibrated approach balancing conservation and renewable energy.

Analysis

  • Green vs green dilemma: save an endangered bird or expand clean energy
  • renewables seen as environmental protection, not mere commerce
  • Targeted protection on expert assessment
  • Institutional humility (experts demarcate)

Significance and criticism

  • Constitutionalises climate rights
  • Recognises impact on health, food, water, dignity
  • Climate equity (poor hit hardest)
  • Positive State duties
  • criticism:
    • Little guidance on enforcing the new right
    • Relaxing line restrictions may weaken bustard protection
Way forward / key point

Shows the Constitution as a living document: Art 21 keeps expanding with new environmental challenges.
