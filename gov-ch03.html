1 · Governance & GOOD Governance#

Handout only · GEG pp. 3-6
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: none quoted in the handout (2016 PYQ on effectiveness and participation sits with Ch 11). Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Governance = delivery of goods and services by state, market and civil society together. Good governance adds a purpose: fair, accountable, inclusive, sustainable. Know the three models and the frameworks (UNDP, DARPG, 4Es, WGI, GGI).
Key point

Governance = comprehensive delivery of goods and services. It has an inverse relationship with research: the more research on it, the more ambiguous it gets; so our understanding of governance and good governance has become more confused instead of more refined. Researchers agree on three basic meanings/models.

Three Models OF Governance

  • 3 MODELS:
    • Triangulation: state, market, civil society
    • World Bank: market first (1989)
    • Good governance: growth with human development

Triangulation (network / third-party governance)

  • governance as a process is untied from the apron strings of government and placed in the middle of society: market and civil society also contribute, not only the State
  • State = 1st party, market = 2nd, civil society = 3rd
  • not in isolation but in combinations:
    • State + market, market + civil society, or all three
    • E.g. State makes policy, market implements, credible NGO evaluates, feedback improves policy

World Bank model

  • Based on market ascendancy
  • First elaborated in a World Bank paper on Sub-Saharan Africa, 1989
  • premise:
    • Market plays the dominant role, government a facilitatory or peripheral one, civil society is marginalised
    • Market is the key entity around which world societies should be organised
  • good governance = exercise of political power to manage a country's resources efficiently
  • features:
    • Accountability to market (predictable policy, lower deficit)
    • Transparency for business (ease of doing business)
    • Empowerment by e-gov and m-gov, not rights
    • Participation as producer and consumer, not citizen
Value addition

Critique: such a perspective would substitute politics with management: "a democracy without politics and citizenship without rights" = a kind of soft authoritarianism.

State vs Market

Market problems

  • consumers vs citizens:
    • Market responds to those able to pay (user-pays principle)
    • Government always responds to citizens
  • Market avoids loss-making ventures
  • Government sees them as part of socio-economic justice (train to a remote area, bank branches in remote areas)
  • market failure: 2008 financial crisis caused by unregulated markets, esp. financial institutions
  • crony capitalism: business houses bribe their way into favourable policy and distort the level playing field
  • environment: profit-oriented, degrades if not regulated
  • not all State activity can be outsourced: external affairs, defence management, law and order

Strong market needs strong state

  • manage public order
  • manage social conflict
  • level playing field
  • sanctity of contract
Note

Decentring the State and replacing it with the market: the real issue is replacing the "bourgeoisie-feudal-bureaucratic" state with a genuine democratic state.

GOOD Governance Model

Key point

Good governance model = an environment that enables all to live fulfilling, healthy, innovative and productive lives, i.e. democratic growth with human development.

Memory aid · SMART

Teacher's: Simple · Moral · Accountable · Responsive · Transparent government.

Value addition

Features: social justice · rule of law with equitable access to justice · SMART government · space for community organisations · empowered local bodies · sustainable development · socially responsible market economy · popular participation.

Way forward / key point

4Es (teacher's): Ethics (honesty, integrity) · Efficiency (results, least waste) · Equity (fair opportunities) · Ethos (public-service values, citizen-centric).

Constitutional Vision

No phrase "good governance", but the base is there

  • Preamble: justice, liberty, equality, fraternity as guiding values of governance
  • DPSP: Arts 38, 39, 41, 47 promote social welfare, reduction of inequalities, better living standards
  • FRs: Arts 14, 19, 21 protect equality, freedom of expression, life and personal liberty
  • Art 50: separation of judiciary from executive strengthens judicial independence
  • Parts IX and IXA: constitutional status to Panchayats and Municipalities: democratic decentralisation
Key point

Judicial contributions: Vineet Narain v UoI 1997 (strengthened institutional accountability and independence of investigative agencies) · Anjali Bhardwaj v UoI 2019 (timely appointments to Information Commissions, to protect effective functioning of the RTI framework).

2 · People's Participation & Local Self-government#

Handout only · GEG pp. 7-9
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: none quoted in the handout. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Participation goes beyond voting: people shape and watch the work. It needs social capital, equality and strong local bodies, and it is blocked by caste, class, gender and powerlessness.
Value addition

Participation goes beyond voting. A person feels liberated when his issues truly get addressed by the authorities responsible: he then truly participates (e.g. Mohalla committees), and people's participation becomes the mainstream paradigm of governance, "almost a re-birth of society". The right to participate should be a fundamental right, especially in aspects of governance affecting citizens' daily lives.

Benefits

  • More information
  • Check fit with ground reality
  • Less policy failure
  • Social acceptance and ownership
  • Sustainable projects
  • two-way learning:
    • Government learns from people, people learn technical aspects from government
    • With time government's responsibility for the project falls and people's involvement rises
  • Capacity building
  • Vigilance, less pilferage and corruption
  • Community feeling, social capital
  • Volunteer resources

Pre-requisites

  • a basic, rudimentary level of social capital before participation can even start
  • equality of wealth and power widens the social base of participation
  • institutional environment: local self-government
  • procedural reforms to involve people

Barriers

  • caste: upper castes dominate
  • class: landed control
  • gender: male-dominated Gram Sabhas
  • powerlessness: people must be prepared to participate, not just asked
  • low capacity: basic education, awareness of rights and legal framework
  • political: lack of decentralisation
Memory aid · CCG LPC

Barriers: Caste · Class · Gender · Lack of motivation · Political constraints · Capacity gap. (own mnemonic)

Urban Local Bodies

Key point

74th Amendment ULBs: critical to democratic decentralisation, but constrained by inadequate devolution of 3Fs: functions, finances, functionaries. Municipal own revenue below 1% of GDP (RBI 2024) vs South Africa ~6%. State dependence: States retain control over municipal finances and functionaries, diluting autonomy. Capacity deficit: administrative, technological and human-resource.

Reforms

  • Empower State Finance Commissions: constitutional mandate for timely reports, transparent devolution formula, monitoring (2nd ARC, 13th FC)
  • Functional autonomy: Model Municipal Law for urban planning, water supply, sanitation, public health
  • E-municipality
  • GIS revenue mapping (BBMP Bengaluru)
  • fiscal powers: municipal bonds, user fees, property-tax reform (geo-spatial mapping in Pune, Hyderabad raised tax base by 40%)
  • institutionalise Ward Committees and Area Sabhas as mandated under 74th Amendment for participatory planning
  • integrated urban data platforms
Best practiceDetail
Ahmedabad Municipal Corporationpioneered India's first municipal bonds, 1998: model for infrastructure financing
Pune Smart GovernanceGIS-enabled property tax: 35% increase in revenue
Delhi Mohalla Sabhaspilot ward participatory budgeting; needs scale and legal backing

GRAM Sabhas

Value addition

73rd Amendment: Gram Sabhas are the foundation of grassroots democracy but under-used: poor attendance (often below 15% in many states, MoPR survey 2023), bureaucratic dominance, resolutions seen as recommendatory, not binding (no statutory enforcement); PRIs lack training in participatory planning, budgeting and social auditing.

Way forward

  • statutory backing to Gram Sabha resolutions, esp. in planning and social audits of schemes like MGNREGA
  • e-GramSwaraj to track attendance, decisions and follow-up
  • reserved discussion slots for women, SC/ST, SHG members in every meeting (Kerala People's Plan Campaign)
  • mandate Gram Sabha-led social audits: downward accountability
  • Panchayat Resource Centres to train PRI functionaries on participatory tools
Practice / reportDetail
Jharkhand Social Audit Unitinstitutionalised Gram Sabha-led MGNREGA audits; exposed over Rs 18 crore irregularities
Kerala People's Plan CampaignGram Sabhas formulated local development plans (rural development index recognition)
Chhattisgarh Jan-ChoupalGram Sabha-level grievance redressal and planning forums; better local responsiveness
13th Finance Commissionperformance-linked grants for PRIs, linking funds to Gram Sabha functionality
Bhuria Committee 1995recommended Gram Sabha's pivotal role in tribal self-governance

Minimum Government, Maximum Governance

Note

Not essentially less bureaucracy but a better bureaucracy. Components: do away with crippling regulations · reduce bureaucrats' discretionary powers · goals over rules · effective facilitation of business and civil society organisations · effective accountability mechanism. Steps: self-certification in place of gazetted-officer certification · drive to identify obsolete rules · labour-law amendments to reduce inspector raj, benchmarking best practices for industrial clearances · single window (ends inter-departmental wrangling) · introduction of e-governance.

In the news (Saarthi IAS CA; verify dates)

3 · E-governance#

Handout only · GEG pp. 9-14
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2025 · 2024 · 2023 · 2021 · 2020* · 2019 · 2018 · 2013. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • E-government digitises the government's own work. E-governance uses ICT to deliver services through state, market and civil society together. Learn stages, four models, ten benefits, and the issues (divide, trust, no re-engineering).
E-GovernmentE-Governance
a more efficient form of government made possible by increasing, extensive use of ICT to manage its own internal processesbased on the triangulation model (state-market-civil society): comprehensive delivery of public goods and services exploiting the synergies between the three, with ICT as the platform

FIVE STAGES (in order)

One-way information (website)
Two-way communication (queries)
Services and payments (bills, returns, GST)
Integration (same server, LAN/WAN)
Participation (e.g. mygov.in)

Stages in detail

  • 1 Simple information dissemination:
    • Website reaches people about programmes
    • One-way data transfer
    • Most basic form
  • 2 Two-way communication:
    • Demand-driven data transfer
    • Extra pages of information
    • A user unable to find information can put a query on the site
  • 3 Services and financial transactions: things people did physically (services, transactions with governmental and non-governmental entities) become digital: bill payment, filing returns, GST or income tax payment
  • 4 Integration:
    • Same server or database connects every employee vertically and horizontally in a department
    • Extended to other departments, ministries and national-state-local governments by LAN and WAN
  • 5 Popular participation: sites designed for flow from people to government too: opinions on proposed legislation or policy (mygov.in)

FOUR Models PYQ 2024

ModelIdea
Broadcastingwithout internet, information was limited; e-governance gives an exponential rise in flow of information; earlier less access meant less informed decisions, now more informed and empowered citizens
Critical Information Flowcritical information needed for a person's growth; farmer needs weather forecast, pesticide use, prevailing price of his produce; earlier cost money, time and energy, now one click, no travel to mandi
E-Mobilisationenforces democratic rights by mobilising people virtually: physical space for assembling is limited; protest in the virtual world and pressure government; democracy-enhancing, citizen-to-citizen engagement

Memory aid · BCEI

Four models in handout order: Broadcasting · Critical information flow · E-mobilisation · Interactive service. (own mnemonic)

BENEFITS (ten)

Benefits

  • 1 Administration (overcomes historic disabilities):
    • E-files cut delay (seconds/minutes between departments)
    • Team work (copy sent to all, immediate comments)
    • Central storage with multiple backups (Adarsh Society scam: documents destroyed)
    • Less paperwork, carbon footprint (fewer trees cut), manpower and space
    • So better intended finance
  • 2 Sound data management for planning and decisions:
    • Manual processing limited, computer analytics better
    • Vikas Darpan (Rajasthan GIS-based decision support)
    • CPGRAMS: aggregated complaints show which departments or procedures need amendment
  • 3 Service delivery:
    • E-FIR (Delhi)
    • Rural: land records, agriculture information (seeds, pests, weather, crop disease, prices)
    • Utility bills
    • Policing (lost and found, e-FIR)
    • Licences, passport, ration card
    • Where is My Train, RailYatri
    • E-filing of returns
    • Single-window ME-SEVA (Andhra), FRIENDS (Kerala: Fast, Reliable, Instant, Effective Network for Disbursement of Services)
  • 4 Lower transaction cost and end-to-end time: simultaneous processing, no department-by-department trail
  • 5 Process re-engineering:
    • NIC support
    • Digitisation delayed by complex procedures
    • Rules must be simplified before digitising
    • Status checkable even on off days
  • 6 Anytime-anywhere (24x7): status and new applications
  • 7 Transparency:
    • Corruption-prone areas lose the physical interface
    • Transparency, accountability up, corruption down
  • 8 Democracy:
    • Democratic deficit in physical space organised digitally
    • Communities, propaganda or protest on Twitter/Facebook
    • Citizen-to-citizen mobilisation, G2C interface, EVM
  • 9 Judicial administration: video-conferencing, judicial data grid
  • 10 Government revenue:
    • Digital payments reflect white money in banks, so taxes cannot be avoided
    • Black money falls

Issues PYQ 2023PYQ 2021PYQ 2019

WHAT Holds E-governance BACK

  • E-GOV ISSUES:
    • Digital divide
    • Digital trust gap
    • No re-engineering
    • No consultation
    • Transparency only incidental
    • Not a panacea
Value addition

Digital divide: 75% of net users are in urban India; digital illiteracy in rural India (an illiterate person cannot use online services even with a smartphone); mirrors the urban-rural divide (COVID: urban middle-class students on Zoom, rural students excluded; imbalance in education); rural people miss income opportunities, telemedicine; elderly more vulnerable; inadequate vernacular content; last-mile connectivity still a problem.

Note

Digital trust: entering personal information for transactions or fee payment makes people insecure about misuse of data.

Memory aid · 6 C's

Teacher's 6 C's of work plan: Content development (revise regularly, relevant to the zone) · Competence building (e.g. bank staff blame "server fault" and wait for an expert: give basic ICT training) · Connectivity, esp. rural (else only the integrated part benefits, majority left on margins) · Citizen's interface (needs phone and internet; internet costly for rural people; awareness; accessible and affordable) · Cyber laws (data protection and security; technology-agnostic; balance privacy so that productive use of technology is not compromised) · Capital (sites need revenue streams; reasonable charges, self-sustaining).

Note

Inadequate re-engineering: e-governance lets government restructure and simplify procedure; if not done, procedure never gets digitised. Delay is not because of support agencies but sometimes govt lacks interest in simplifying, as the corruption point barricades digitisation.

Note

Narrow vision, no consultation: services shifted from physical to digital without asking stakeholders whether it fits their needs; if consulted first and physical-world problems resolved, e-governance matches needs. Check on corruption and transparency only incidental: dip is automatic from removing human interface; design intervention for the twin goals of accountability and transparency.

Way forward / key point

Big data ("Data is the new oil"): with increasing data, problems arise: data security, privacy, unethical usage.

4 · DATA Protection, Privacy & Cybersecurity#

Handout only · GEG pp. 15-20
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: none quoted in the handout. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Data is the new oil, and also a liability. India moved from IT Rules 2011 → Puttaswamy 2017 → Srikrishna Committee → PDP Bill 2019 (a Bill) → Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (an Act). Keep Bill and Act apart.
Value addition

India: about 800 million internet users, 400 million smartphone users, 150 exabytes of data a year (handout). Strategic asset and liability. 132 of 194 countries have data-protection frameworks. Rise of big data, tech giants and data aggregation raises privacy-breach and profiling concerns: even India's poorest are integrated through Aadhaar-linked services. The Parliamentary Committee on the PDP Bill noted data volumes expanding exponentially: opportunities and governance challenges.

Way forward / key point

Need for protective data usage: harness data for growth, better public services and Atmanirbhar Bharat; cross-sectoral data convergence (Aadhaar, GSTN, UPI, BHUVAN) can improve policy through predictive analytics; but privacy and security are imperative to keep public trust while maximising economic benefit.

Issues IN THE DATA Ecosystem

Issues

  • data security and privacy:
    • Prevent misuse for commercial gain
    • Over-regulation stifles innovation, lax control erodes public confidence
    • Privacy by design while enabling economic use
  • cross-border compatibility:
    • Align with global norms so firms operating internationally face no conflict
    • Debate: uniform across private and public sectors or sector-specific
    • Extent of government exemptions
  • medico-legal:
    • Leaks cause not just financial loss but emotional and psychological harm: stress, depression, PTSD (Identity Theft Resource Center study)
    • Law must integrate medico-legal safeguards
  • fake accounts and bots: mimic humans, spread propaganda and misinformation (NYT: 48 million Twitter accounts fake: malicious campaigns, scams)
  • social media abuse by anti-social elements:
    • Extremist and terror outfits (ISIS recruitment)
    • Tension between privacy and national security: needs government-platform cooperation

DATA Localisation

Key point

Data localisation = restrictions on cross-border data flow: local storage with regulated external access. Hard: absolute restriction on data leaving national borders. Soft: stored locally with conditional cross-border sharing. Key challenge: defining clear purpose-specific conditions for permissible transfers.

Benefits (detail)

  • national security: hostile nations cannot access information on our population to manipulate it
  • law enforcement:
    • Easier access for investigation
    • Abroad it is difficult and impedes investigation
  • privacy:
    • Government can impose its own privacy rules
    • Otherwise data flows where regulation may be lenient
  • grievance redressal:
    • Breach can be pursued in an Indian court
    • Not feasible if breach occurs abroad
  • bargaining power: local data is an asset in deals with countries and multilateral institutions (market access, bilateral, technical and economic cooperation)
Memory aid · SLIPB

Benefits: Security (national) · Law enforcement access · India's own privacy rules apply · Privacy grievance in Indian courts · Bargaining power. (own mnemonic)

LEGAL PATH (in order)

  1. 2000 / 2011
    IT Rules 2011 under IT Act 2000 defined Reasonable Security Practices and Sensitive Personal Data Rules; inadequate against rising privacy concerns, so a comprehensive PDP Bill was needed
  2. 2017
    Puttaswamy: Right to Privacy a Fundamental Right, so government must build a robust legal framework; Justice B.N. Srikrishna Committee formed to draft the law
  3. 2018
    Srikrishna report laid the foundation for India's first PDP Bill
  4. 2019
    PDP Bill 2019 (a Bill); several progressive recommendations were omitted or diluted in the final draft, leaving gaps
  5. 2023
    DPDP Act, 2023 (an Act)
Note

Bill vs Act: PDP Bill 2019 was only a Bill (handout: Departmental Standing Committee; a JPC examined it and it was withdrawn in 2022: general knowledge, verify). Law in force: DPDP Act 2023.

GDPR (EU, 2018): "EU avatar" of the Indian PDP Bill

  • informed consent:
    • Lengthy complex online consent causes consent fatigue
    • Terms should be simplified, shortened, understandable
  • breach notification: the moment data is breached, the individual must be informed immediately
  • automated decision-making:
    • AI recommendation algorithms build profiles
    • Right to opt out to prevent profiling
  • citizen awareness: mass citizen-engagement programme

Principles of data protection (7)

  • technology agnosticism:
    • Adaptive, future-proof
    • Avoids frequent overhauls
  • holistic application:
    • Private entities and government
    • Narrow, well-defined exemptions (national security, public order, investigation) to prevent misuse of discretion
  • informed consent: genuine choice, not procedural formality from consent fatigue
  • data minimisation:
    • Collect only what is strictly necessary
    • Purpose-limited use
  • controlled accountability:
    • Fiduciaries proactively use advanced security
    • Accountable for breach under a defined framework
  • structured enforcement: multi-tier: company, independent regulator, tribunals, judiciary (HC, SC)
  • deterrent penalties as envisaged in the PDP Bill, to ensure compliance and trust
Memory aid · SHARP-DD

Principles: Structured enforcement · Holistic cover (private + govt) · Agnostic technology · Responsible fiduciary · Purpose-limited minimisation · Deterrent penalties · Data consent (informed). (own mnemonic)

PDP BILL 2019 (provisions of the Bill)

Bill provisions

  • preamble:
    • Right to privacy is a fundamental right
    • Protect personal data as a facet of privacy
    • Collective culture for a free and fair digital economy
  • applies to: government, companies incorporated in India, foreign companies dealing with personal data of individuals in India
  • personal data:
    • Traits or attributes of identity that identify an individual
    • Sensitive: financial, biometric, caste, religious or political belief, or other category the government specifies with the Authority and sectoral regulator
  • data fiduciary:
    • Entity or individual deciding means and purpose of processing
    • Only for specific, clear, lawful purposes, within collection and storage limits
    • Security safeguards (encryption, preventing misuse), grievance redress, age verification and parental consent for sensitive data of children
  • rights of data principal: confirmation of processing, correction of inaccurate/incomplete/out-of-date data, portability to another fiduciary, restrict continuing disclosure when no longer necessary or consent withdrawn
  • Consent is the basis
  • **** no consent needed for: benefits by the State, legal proceedings, medical emergency
  • social media intermediaries:
    • Enable user interaction
    • Above a notified threshold and able to affect electoral democracy or public order: voluntary user verification
  • Authority:
    • Protects individuals' interests, prevents misuse, ensures compliance
    • Chair + 6 members with at least 10 years in data protection and IT
    • Appeal to Appellate Tribunal, then Supreme Court
  • transfer abroad:
    • Sensitive data only with explicit consent plus conditions, and must still be stored in India
    • Critical personal data (notified) processed only in India
  • exemptions:
    • Centre can exempt its agencies (security of state, public order, sovereignty and integrity, friendly relations, preventing incitement to cognizable offences)
    • Also prevention/investigation/prosecution of offences, personal or domestic, journalistic purposes (still specific, clear, lawful, with safeguards)
  • penalty:
    • Violation Rs 15 crore or 4% of annual turnover, whichever higher
    • Failure of data audit Rs 5 crore or 2%
    • Re-identification without consent: up to 3 years, fine or both

Cybersecurity

Key point

Incidents: UIDAI Aadhaar data leak 2018 (alarm over citizen data privacy) · Kudankulam nuclear plant breach 2019 (Lazarus Group-led; escalated national-security concerns) · AIIMS Delhi ransomware 2022 (fragility of healthcare cybersecurity).

Value addition

Role of cybersecurity: the backbone of India's digital economy; trust in digital governance rests on safeguarding critical infrastructure, preventing intrusions, ensuring data confidentiality: from citizen data in welfare programmes to national security. Threats: ransomware, data breaches, espionage.

Initiatives

  • CERT-In: nodal agency for cyber-threat response, under MeitY
  • National Cyber Security Policy 2013: strategies for securing national digital assets
  • Cyber Swachhta Kendra: botnet cleaning, malware mitigation
  • ISEA programme: cyber awareness among students and government officials
  • Cyber Surakshit Bharat: public-private collaboration for cyber resilience in government institutions
Way forward / key point

Way forward: expand operational capacity of CERT-In and I4C; consider a dedicated Cybersecurity Authority for critical sectors · enforce DPDPA 2023 (robust data governance to uphold privacy) · PPP innovation hubs (e.g. Infosys Cyber Defense Center for capacity building) · cyber education in curricula (Cyber Surakshit Bharat) · home-grown R&D (DRDO secure communication systems set precedent).

5 · Emerging TECH IN Governance: Blockchain · AI · DPI#

Handout only · GEG pp. 20-24
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2020* (Fourth Industrial Revolution and e-governance). Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • New tools (blockchain, AI, DPI) widen what government can do, and bring risks (deepfakes, content regulation, privacy). For each: what, example, challenge, way forward.

Blockchain

Key point

Promise: transparency, immutability, decentralised trust: transforming public service delivery from land records to grievance redressal. CBDC (Digital Rupee): RBI initiative to modernise digital payments and reduce cash dependency. Telangana and Maharashtra pilot blockchain for property registration and digital academic records; regulators TRAI and SEBI exploring it for transparency and efficiency.

CaseImpact
NFTs for land mutation (West Bengal)streamlined land ownership records; eliminated manual processes
Tamil Nadu Blockchain Backboneconsolidates citizen documents into a secure digital wallet for easy access and sharing
Firozabad grievance system (UP)transparent complaint redressal using blockchain verification protocols
Value addition

Challenges: scalability and energy use (high computational demand slows scaling) · legal ambiguity: no robust legal recognition of blockchain records · data privacy: public chains risk exposing sensitive citizen data.

Way forward / key point

Way: comprehensive regulatory framework akin to Estonia's blockchain legal infrastructure · prioritise permissioned blockchains for government use · capacity building and sandbox projects like Dubai's Blockchain Strategy.

AI AND Governance (4IR) PYQ 2020*

Key point

AI is reshaping public administration through automation, predictive analytics and efficient service delivery.

Uses

  • AI-led service delivery:
    • Automation reduces administrative burden
    • E.g. Indian Railways' "Ideal Train Profile" optimises revenue via AI analysis
  • healthcare and education:
    • Telemedicine, personalised learning
    • E.g. NITI Aayog cancer image databases
  • agriculture and inclusivity:
    • AI-based pest control
    • Bhashini project enables language inclusivity

National initiatives

  • IndiaAI Mission: build indigenous AI infrastructure, including 10,000 GPUs
  • BharatGen Project: multilingual AI models for India's linguistic diversity
  • global collaborations:
    • GPAI
    • Bilateral projects like US-India AI partnership
AI challengeSolution
fragmented data governanceimplement National Data Governance Policy; open, interoperable datasets
lack of AI-specific legislationdraft AI-specific regulations ensuring ethical oversight and transparency
skill deficits in AI workforceexpand AI training, especially in rural and underserved regions
privacy and data security concernsenforce DPDPA effectively, focusing on data localisation and protection of sensitive information

Deepfakes

Value addition

Deepfakes blur the line between reality and fabrication: a challenge to governance (elections, national security, public trust). Current law: IT Act 2000 Secs 66C, 66E, 67, 67A + IPC provisions on defamation, obscenity, forgery. IT Rules 2021: flagged deepfake content to be taken down within 36 hours.

Key point

Government response and advisory (2023): directives to intermediaries for timely takedown and user education · engagement with stakeholders for compliance · future IT Rules amendments to enhance platform accountability.

Impact on governance

  • election misinformation: manipulates voter perception
  • national security threats: fabricated military or government communications
  • cybercrime and fraud: scams impersonating officials
  • erosion of public trust: citizens cannot tell genuine from manipulated content
Way forward / key point

Way: platform accountability: detection tools like Microsoft's Video Authenticator · blockchain-based verification to trace media authenticity · comprehensive legislation: frame a Digital India Act addressing deepfakes, learning from international frameworks · public awareness: responsible content sharing, media literacy.

OTT AND Content Regulation

Why and how

  • need:
    • Explosive growth (424 million users in 2022)
    • Obscenity, lack of censorship, minors exposed to inappropriate material
    • Cybersecurity risks: data breaches, fraud
  • Digital Media Ethics Code: mandatory content classification: ratings U, UA 7+, 13+, 16+, A and parental controls
  • three-tier grievance mechanism: publisher-level redressal, self-regulatory bodies, government oversight
  • norm compliance: align digital news with Press Council and Cable TV Programme Codes
  • way forward:
    • Fast-track DPDPA to safeguard OTT user privacy
    • Common regulator for digital, print and broadcast (Singapore model)
    • Age ratings and content tags in regional languages alongside English
    • Independent audit body for content compliance and data security

Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI)

Key point

DPI: open-source, interoperable tech platforms underpinning mass delivery of public services, designed to be scalable, inclusive and secure. India Stack: Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker. Also ONDC, Open Health Stack, NDEAR (education; DIKSHA).

PlatformPurpose
Aadhaardigital identity, presenceless authentication
UPIreal-time interoperable payments
DigiLockerpaperless documents
ONDCopen digital commerce
Open Health Stacksecure health-data sharing
NDEARnational digital education architecture (DIKSHA)

Significance

  • digital inclusion and financial access:
    • ~80% financial inclusion in 6 years vs expected 46-year trajectory
    • UPI brought digital payments to rural India, widening formal credit and savings access
  • economic gain: DPI could add 2.9-4.2% of GDP by 2030, pivotal to the $8 trillion economy ambition
  • govt efficiency and innovation: CSCs extend Digital India to remote areas through local entrepreneurs
  • AP ICJS 2.0 (Interoperable Criminal Justice System): police, forensics, courts, prisons digitised with AI-powered coordination: transparency and efficiency

Challenges

  • digital divide:
    • Disparity across regions, castes, socio-economic groups
    • 70% of rural population lacks reliable connectivity
    • Limited devices and training
  • privacy and trust deficit:
    • Broad digital identity raises surveillance and misuse concerns
    • Unclear redress mechanisms and absent robust legal framework erode trust
  • state capacity and institutional gaps: need strong frameworks and "intelligent customers" (state actors knowledgeable in risk and data governance)
  • implementation disparity across states: Madhya Pradesh only 0.9% schools with digital libraries vs national 6.1%

Way forward

  • bridge connectivity and literacy gap:
    • National Broadband Mission 2.0 (universal rural broadband)
    • Digital literacy via CSCs and PMGDISHA-like schemes
  • institutionalise data trust:
    • Embed privacy, contestability, grievance redress from inception
    • Procedural fairness, not just compliance
  • state capacity and hybrid institutions:
    • Become intelligent DPI customers
    • State-civil society collaboration
  • scale through PPP and innovation:
    • Open-source interoperable platforms
    • E.g. Google's investment in Indian data centres
  • DPI for new sectors: power (India Stack-like for utilities), education (DIKSHA-based NDEAR), health (Open Health Stack)
