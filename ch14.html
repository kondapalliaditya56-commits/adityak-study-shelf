1 · Peak of Expansion: how far and by what means#

By the end of Dalhousie 48-56's tenure the Company State controlled 63% of India, home to 78% of its population. After Dalhousie 48-56 there were no further annexations.

He used the Policy of Paramountcy (PoP 1813-58) in three ways:

  1. Doctrine of Lapse: annexation by refusing to recognise adopted heirs.
  2. Wars: the 2nd Anglo-Sikh War 1848-49 (Punjab annexed) and the 2nd Anglo-Burmese War 1852-53 (Pegu annexed).
  3. Annexation in Lieu of Subsidy: Berar 1853 from Hyderabad.

Besides these: the Maritime Ring Fence, the modernisation infrastructure and the five-policy framework.

Teacher's board

"Annexation of the Indian mainland" means annexation "into British-ruled India, that is British India." He writes "i.e. Br India" and underlines British India. On the Lapse row he supplies the logic of why the British cared about succession: succession disputes (R v R, read as ruler against ruler) → instability → hurt British business interests; and "British interests = Paramount." So the Doctrine of Lapse is not just a legal trick: instability in an Indian State threatens British trade, and British interests are supreme. The small BERAR / HYD circle is the picture for the third instrument: Berar sits at the top of the Hyderabad circle.

Memory hook

"Lapse, War, Lieu": the three roads of Paramountcy.

Decode: Lapse (refuse adopted heirs), Wars (Punjab 1848-49 and Pegu 1852-53), Lieu of subsidy (Berar 1853). Result: 63% of territory, 78% of population, and no more annexations after Dalhousie 48-56.

Why it sticks: Lapse and Lieu both start with L; the war sits between them.

Decode: 63% of India's territory, 78% of its population were under the Company State by 1856.

Why it sticks: the two numbers are the same digits reversed and moved (63, 78: 6+3 = 9, 7+8 = 15).

2 · The Doctrine of Lapse: legal principle against pretext#

The Doctrine of Lapse was formulated by the Court of Directors of the EIC in 1847 but implemented systematically by Dalhousie 48-56.

  • The rule was simple: if an Indian ruler died without a biological heir, his rights lapsed and the State was annexed into British India.
  • Under Hindu law, adoption was a legitimate mechanism for succession: adopted sons inherited with full legitimacy. The Doctrine of Lapse refused to recognise this.
Teacher's board

The Court of Directors is the "top decision-making body of the EIC," that is the Board of Directors, "not a Government body." He writes "PRELIMS" beside it, so the Prelims trap is: who formulated the doctrine (the Court of Directors, 1847) against who implemented it (Dalhousie 48-56). His bracket: "Dalhousie 48-56 # Author of DoL" (he is credited as author in common speech because he applied it). "Rights lapsed" means e.g. R2 Rule, R2 Pension: the right to rule and the right to the pension both lapsed (read R2 as "right to"). And "DoL overrides Hindu Law."

Handout alert

"Author of the Doctrine" is not the same as "formulator." The handout and slide say formulated by the Court of Directors in 1847, implemented systematically by Dalhousie 48-56. The teacher's bracket lets you call Dalhousie 48-56 its author in a loose sense. In a Prelims statement, choose by the verb: formulated = Court of Directors; implemented = Dalhousie 48-56.

Memory hook

"Court Formulates 47, Dalhousie 48-56 Does 48".

Decode: Court of Directors formulates in 1847; Dalhousie 48-56 implements from 1848 (Satara is the first Lapse State, 1848). Rule: no biological heir = rights lapse; adoption under Hindu law is overridden.

Why it sticks: the court writes the law, the Governor General does the deed, one year apart (47, 48).

4 · The Inam Commission (1852)#

Dalhousie 48-56 also targeted privileged landholdings.

  • In 1852 the Bombay Presidency set up the Inam Commission to investigate inams (tax-free grants) made by earlier Indian rulers to Brahmins, temples and others.
  • The Commission demanded proof of title; grants with insufficient proof were confiscated.
  • Around 20,000 estates were seized in the Bombay Presidency alone.
  • The logic: the Inam Commission was the Doctrine of Lapse applied to land titles instead of thrones. If you cannot prove your claim to the Company's satisfaction, the Company takes it. The logic was identical.

Beside the Logic row label he writes "ignore" (underlined): the comparison with Lapse is a handout aid, not something he wants memorised for the exam. 5 · The Doctrine of Lapse applied to pensions: Nana Sahib#

  • Dalhousie 48-56 extended the Doctrine of Lapse to pensions.
  • Peshwa Bajirao II 96-18 died in 1851. Dalhousie 48-56 refused to continue the State pension of Rs 8 lakh per annum to his adopted son Nana Sahib.
  • The argument: adopted sons inherit private wealth, not political titles or State pensions.
  • The refusal made Nana Sahib a key 1857 leader from Bithur, where Bajirao II 96-18 had been pensioned off in 1819 after the 3rd Anglo-Maratha War.
Memory hook

"Eight lakh, fifty-one, Bithur": the Nana Sahib chain.

Decode: pension Rs 8 lakh a year; Bajirao II 96-18 died 1851; pensioned at Bithur in 1819; adopted son Nana Sahib refused, so he became an 1857 leader.

Why it sticks: 8 lakh, 51, 19, 57: four numbers around one town, Bithur.

6 · Annexation in lieu of subsidy: Berar (1853)#

  • Annexation in Lieu of Subsidy is the second instrument (the slide's order) of Dalhousie 48-56.
  • Hyderabad signed the first Subsidiary Alliance in 1798 and had been paying ever-rising subsidy ever since.
  • By 1853 Hyderabad could not pay. Dalhousie 48-56 annexed Berar as permanent payment of the subsidy.
  • Recall: in 1801 half of Awadh was annexed as subsidy (Section 7). The pattern is the same: unpaid subsidy becomes territory.
Memory hook

"98 signs, 53 pays in land": Hyderabad's subsidy line.

Decode: Hyderabad signs the first Subsidiary Alliance (1798), cannot pay by 1853, so Berar is taken as permanent payment. Same trick as Awadh 1801.

Why it sticks: 98 to 53 is a 55-year slide from signature to seizure.

7 · The annexation of Awadh (1856)#

The six steps of Awadh's fall:

  1. 1765: Treaty of Allahabad (by Robert Clive 57-67) established PoIR + PoRF + a mini Subsidiary Alliance in Awadh.
  2. The EIC gradually increased its subsidy demands on Awadh.
  3. 1798: John Shore 93-98 replaced Wazir Ali 97-98 as Nawab with his uncle Sadat Ali Khan II, in return for an annual subsidy of Rs 76 lakh and the cession of Allahabad fort.
  4. 1801: Sadat Ali Khan II defaulted. Wellesley 98-05 imposed the full Subsidiary Alliance by sending Henry Wellesley. Half of Awadh (Gorakhpur, Rohilkhand and the Doab) was annexed as permanent payment of the subsidy, called the Ceded Districts; it was unjust, as the revenue was twice the subsidy.
  5. The EIC kept demanding more subsidy despite the permanent payment. The Nawab had to raise taxes and by 1855 there was a threat of peasant revolts.
  6. 1856: Dalhousie 48-56 annexed the rest of Awadh on grounds of maladministration. By 1855 the British Resident had established Indirect Rule and Nawab Wajid Ali Shah had no real power, hence no power but all responsibility.
Teacher's board

How the three men line up on one timeline: Clive 57-67 (ToA 1765): PoIR initiated, and by 1855 Indirect Rule of the British Resident. The bracket "90 yrs vs 7 yrs in Bengal" compares how long Indirect Rule lasted in Awadh (1765 to 1855, 90 years) against Bengal (about 7 years, 1765 to 1772). He calls the 1765 arrangement "mini SA" or "quasi SA." Wellesley 98-05 (1801 SA): the Doab is the land between two rivers, very fertile, high land revenue; Gorakhpur and Rohilkhand had been with Awadh since 1775. Dalhousie 48-56: rising land revenue, therefore rising subsidy, therefore by 1855 the threat of peasant revolt, therefore British business interests under threat [PoP 1813-58]; the Nawab is accused of maladministration, so Awadh is annexed. 12.3 Policy of Subsidiary Alliance (PoSA, 1798-1858)

  • PoSA (1798-1858): the EIC subordinated Indian rulers as junior alliance partners by taking over their foreign policy and military, both funded by an annual subsidy from the Indian State, while formally promising non-interference in internal affairs.
  • The promise was routinely broken through illegal PoIR. Bajirao II 96-18 signed the SA via the Treaty of Bassein 1802 to regain his throne; the treaty promised non-interference, but British Resident Mountstuart Elphinstone soon bypassed the Peshwa and interfered directly: creeping PoIR through the back door.
  • When Bajirao II 96-18 launched the 3rd Anglo-Maratha War (1817), it was not aggression: it was a ruler trying to shake off interference the treaty had explicitly promised would never happen. No powers, but all the blame: the same pattern as Awadh 1856 (Section 7).
Memory hook

SA: "Promise Non-interference, Practice Interference".

Decode: PoSA takes foreign policy and military, paid by subsidy, promises non-interference; broken by illegal PoIR. Bajirao II 96-18: Bassein 1802 (to regain his throne), Elphinstone bypasses him, 1817 the 3rd Anglo-Maratha War. "No powers, all blame" = Awadh 1856.

Why it sticks: the same two words twice (non-interference then interference): the promise and the break.

12.4 Policy of Paramountcy / Subordinate Isolation (1813-58)

  • Initiated by Francis Hastings 13-23. From 1813 the EIC formally declared itself paramount: the supreme power among all States in India. To protect British interests it would interfere in internal affairs and annex if needed. (Earlier this happened in practice; from 1813 the EIC shed the pretence. Indian States were to have no rights if British interests demanded otherwise.)
  • Diplomatic isolation (cutting off a State's right to deal with other States independently) had existed since early SA treaties. After 1813 every Indian State, large or small, was locked in a sealed bubble: no communication, negotiation or alliance with any other State without the EIC as absolute gatekeeper.
  • Conceptually: Policy of Paramountcy = Policy of SA minus the rights of the Indian State.
Memory hook

"PoP = SA minus rights"; Francis Hastings 13-23 starts it in 1813.

Decode: Paramountcy = Subsidiary Alliance minus the Indian State's rights; sealed bubble: no talk, treaty or alliance without the EIC as gatekeeper; started by Francis Hastings 13-23 in 1813; the teacher: it contributed to the 3rd Anglo-Maratha War 1817-19.

Why it sticks: subtraction sign: SA is the full price, take away the rights and you get PoP.

12.5 Policy of Subordinate Union (1858-1947)

After 1857 the EIC was abolished and the British State took direct control. Indian rulers were now not just subordinate but legally part of the British Empire, hence "union." Paramountcy became the formal legal relationship between the British Monarch and the Indian Prince. (Prince, not King: Indian rulers were now legally junior members of the British imperial family.) Sovereignty and the external sovereign functions (Defence, Communications, Foreign Affairs) were with the British Crown; domestic sovereign functions remained with the Indian Prince.

Conceptually: Policy of Subordinate Union = Policy of Paramountcy + sovereignty formally with the British Crown.

Teacher's board

Two words, two circled numbers: ① SUBORDINATE: Indian kings will be junior to the British State. ② UNION: the British Crown = the monarch of Indian kings (and he draws "not Mughal": the monarch is the British Crown, not the Mughal emperor). That is why from 1858 Indian "Kings" were called Indian "Princes": a Prince is the son of a King, and the King is the British monarch. Therefore the whole of India is part of the British empire, and it splits into British India (ruled by the British) and 565 princely States (ruled by an Indian Prince). Symbolic sovereignty: under Subordinate Union the British hold external sovereignty (D, C, FA: Defence, Communications, Foreign Affairs) and the Indian Prince holds domestic sovereign functions; under SA the same split existed but the Indian king held symbolic sovereignty and the EIC held the external functions (D, C, FA).

Why it sticks: "Prince" literally means junior to a King; the King is the Crown.

13.2 Post-Dalhousie: Canning 56-62 inherits and the Queen ends Lapse

  • Dalhousie 48-56 handed power to Canning 56-62 in 1856, who inherited the empire Dalhousie 48-56 built and the revolt his annexations had sown the seeds of.
  • After the suppression of the 1857 revolt, the Government of India Act 1858 abolished the Company State and transferred power to the British Crown.
  • Queen Victoria's Proclamation of 1858 formally ended the Doctrine of Lapse and pledged that the treaties and rights of Indian rulers would be respected: an explicit reversal of Dalhousie 48-56's core policy. Paramountcy became Subordinate Union (Section 12.5).
  • No further annexations followed, a line Dalhousie 48-56's successors (including Viceroy Lytton 76-80) did not cross, even where Paramountcy-era instruments were tightened (the Treaty of Jacobabad 1876 with Kalat). The empire was complete; it only needed managing.
Memory hook

Decode: 1858: the Government of India Act abolishes the Company State; Queen Victoria's Proclamation ends Lapse and respects treaties; Paramountcy gives way to Subordinate Union. After Dalhousie 48-56 (Canning 56-62 inherits) there are no further annexations.

Why it sticks: three things in one year, each undoing what Dalhousie 48-56 built.

  9. Sikh State: ideal buffer under Ranjit Singh 01-39; after 1839 four factions (royals, Sindhanwaliyas, Dogra Rajputs, Khalsa Army). PoIR list (Nepal 1814-16, Afghanistan 1839, Punjab 1845); annexation list (Assam and lower Burma 1824-26, Punjab 1849, Burma 1852-53).
  10. Treaty of Lahore 9 March 1846: Khalsa reduced and an EIC army stationed; Jalandhar Doab annexed; Rs 1.5 crore indemnity unpaid so Kashmir and Hazara ceded, sold to Gulab Singh for 75 lakh Nanakshahee rupees; Resident Henry Lawrence (PoIR). Treaty of Amritsar 16 March 1846 formalised the sale; 1809 Amritsar = Sutlej boundary.
  11. 2nd Anglo-Sikh War 1848-49: Mulraj (Multan) and Chattar Singh (Haripur) revolted; Punjab annexed from Duleep Singh 43-49 (child of 5); Koh-i-Noor to the Crown; Board of Administration (Lawrence Brothers and Charles Mansel); Chilianwala 1849.
  12. Burma: 1st war 1824-26 (Arakan, Tenasserim, Treaty of Yandabo 1826); 2nd war 1852-53 (Pegu, Dalhousie 48-56); 3rd war Upper Burma (Dufferin 84-88); separated 1937. Sindh 1843 was Napier under Ellenborough 42-44.
  13. Maritime Ring Fence: Bay clockwise (Bengal 1757, Northern Sarkars 1766, Carnatic 1801, Orissa 1803-05, Burma); Arabian Sea anticlockwise (Bombay 1668, Mysore/Malabar 1789-99, Gaikwad-Baroda and Peshwa SA 1802, Travancore 1805, Svarajya 1818, Sindh 1843); beyond India.
  14. Kalat 1854 (EIC, John Jacob, Rs 50,000 paid to the Khan, reverse SA) against Jacobabad 1876 (British State, Sandeman, Lytton 76-80, troops, railways, telegraphs, arbitrator). Gwadar: gifted 1783 to Oman, bought by Pakistan 1958. Persia: war 1856-57, Treaty of Paris 1857 (Herat), 1907 spheres, 1909 oil.
  15. Moderniser: Railway 1853 (Bombay-Thane; Calcutta-Raniganj and Madras-Arakkonam by 1856), Telegraph 1853 (4,000 miles), Post Office Act 1854 (half-anna), PWD civilian and GT Road, Wood's Despatch 1854, Bethune School 1849, Vidyasagar, universities 1857 under Canning 56-62.
  16. Five-policy arc: Relative Isolation (until 1813), PoIR + PoRF (alliances from 1765), PoSA 1798-1858, Paramountcy 1813-58 (Francis Hastings 13-23; SA minus rights), Subordinate Union 1858-1947 (Paramountcy + sovereignty with the Crown; Prince not King; D-C-FA with the Crown). Dates 1600, 1765, 1798, 1813, 1858.
  17. Consolidation at the cost of legitimacy: Lakshmibai (Lapse 1854), Nana Sahib (pension 1851), Zafar (dignity), Awadh court, Sikh soldiers, talukdars, inam-holders. 1858: Act and Queen's Proclamation end Lapse; Canning 56-62 inherited the empire.
