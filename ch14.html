Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Paramountcy made British interests supreme, so Dalhousie 48-56 used Lapse, war and unpaid subsidy to take the Company State to 63% of the land; the same annexations, from Jhansi to Awadh, cost it the legitimacy that 1857 then tested.

  1. A trading company that wanted to be left alone. In 1600 the EIC was a trading company, here for business and not for ruling. Its army was small (the Child's War 1686-90 showed it), so it stayed out of Indian politics, but not absolutely: when an opportunity came, as after Buxar 1764, it interfered, took a benefit and went back. After 1757 its power kept rising, and so did its interference.
    → So by 1813 interference became a declared policy.
  2. Paramountcy: British interests first. Francis Hastings 13-23 began the Policy of Paramountcy in 1813: among all States in India the Company State is supreme, British interests come first, and the EIC will annex if it must. Every State was locked in a sealed bubble with the EIC as gatekeeper. Paramountcy was the Subsidiary Alliance minus the rights of the Indian State.
    → Into this policy walks Dalhousie 48-56.
  3. Three roads to expansion. Dalhousie 48-56 used Paramountcy in three ways: the Doctrine of Lapse, wars (Punjab 1849, Pegu 1852-53) and annexation in lieu of subsidy (Berar 1853). By 1856 the Company State held 63% of India and 78% of its people, and no more mainland was annexed after him. The teacher's logic: a succession dispute means instability, which hurts British business, and British interests are paramount.
    → The first road was a rule about heirs.
  4. The Doctrine of Lapse. The Court of Directors formulated it in 1847; Dalhousie 48-56 implemented it. If a ruler died without a biological heir his rights lapsed, overriding adoption under Hindu law. Seven States fell in seven years: Satara 1848, Sambalpur and Jaitpur 1849, Baghat 1850, Udaipur 1852 (in Chhattisgarh), Jhansi and Nagpur 1854. The Inam Commission of 1852 applied the same logic to land, seizing about 20,000 estates in the Bombay Presidency, and the pension of Bajirao II 96-18 was refused to Nana Sahib.
    → The second road was unpaid subsidy.
  5. Berar and Awadh: subsidy turns into land. Hyderabad signed the first Subsidiary Alliance in 1798; by 1853 it could not pay, so Dalhousie 48-56 annexed Berar. Awadh followed the same chain: Clive 57-67's treaty of 1765, Shore's swap of Wazir Ali 97-98 for Sadat Ali Khan II in 1798 for Rs 76 lakh, half of Awadh taken in 1801 under Wellesley 98-05, and in 1856 the rest, on maladministration. Wajid Ali Shah had heirs, so this was not Lapse.
    → The third road was war, and its first target was the Sikh State.
  6. The Sikh State: from buffer to target. Under Ranjit Singh 01-39 the Sikh State was the ideal stable, strong, friendly buffer. After 1839 four factions tore it apart: the royal family, the Sindhanwaliyas, the Dogra Rajputs and the Khalsa Army. The Treaty of Lahore (9 March 1846) cut the army, took the Jalandhar Doab, and sold Kashmir to Gulab Singh for 75 lakh Nanakshahee rupees against a 1.5 crore indemnity, formalised by the Treaty of Amritsar (16 March 1846).
    → But a Resident and a reduced army were not the end.
  7. Punjab annexed, the Koh-i-Noor taken. In 1848 the Sikh Governors Mulraj (Multan) and Chattar Singh (Haripur) revolted, the opportunity the empire-builder Dalhousie 48-56 needed. Punjab was annexed in 1849 from Duleep Singh 43-49, a child of 5 at accession; the Koh-i-Noor went to the British Crown and a Board of Administration (the Lawrence Brothers and Charles Mansel) ruled. The frontier now touched the Afghan foothills.
    → War also reached the eastern shore.
  8. Pegu and the sea ring. The 2nd Anglo-Burmese War 1852-53 annexed Pegu; with Arakan and Tenasserim (1824-26) the Bay of Bengal became a British lake. The Bay's coast fell clockwise from Bengal 1757 to Pegu 1853, and the Arabian Sea anticlockwise from Bombay 1668 to Sindh 1843 (not Dalhousie 48-56's, but Ellenborough 42-44's). Beyond India came Kalat 1854, Jacobabad 1876, Persia 1856-57, 1907 and 1909.
    → But Dalhousie 48-56 did not only annex.
  9. The moderniser. A follower of Utilitarianism, Dalhousie 48-56 built the skeleton of modern India: railways from 1853 (Bombay-Thane first), the telegraph (about 4,000 miles), the Post Office Act 1854 with a half-anna rate, a civilian PWD and the restored GT Road, and Wood's Despatch 1854. The motives: troops, cheaper administration, raw materials and markets. The three universities came in 1857 under Canning 56-62.
    → Yet each annexation was adding to a hidden bill.
  10. The five policies and the hidden bill. The arc ran from Relative Isolation through PoIR and PoRF, the Subsidiary Alliance 1798 and Paramountcy 1813 to Subordinate Union 1858; each step built on the last, which is why 1857 came when it did. Dalhousie 48-56 consolidated the Company State but destroyed its legitimacy: Rani Lakshmibai (Lapse 1854), Nana Sahib (pension 1851), Bahadur Shah Zafar 37-57, the Awadh court, disbanded Sikh soldiers, talukdars and inam-holders were all aggrieved.
    → And then the Crown had to clean up.
  11. 1858: the reversal. Dalhousie 48-56 handed over to Canning 56-62 in 1856, who inherited the revolt. The Government of India Act 1858 abolished the Company State, and Queen Victoria's Proclamation of 1858 ended the Doctrine of Lapse and pledged respect for the treaties and rights of Indian rulers. Paramountcy gave way to Subordinate Union, Indian Kings became Indian Princes, and there were no further annexations.

2 · The Doctrine of Lapse: legal principle against pretext#

Story link

Because succession disputes hurt trade, a rule about heirs became a tool of expansion. Ask: who formulated it, who implemented it, and what did it override?

  • The Doctrine of Lapse was formulated by the Court of Directors of the EIC in 1847 but implemented systematically by Dalhousie 48-56.
  • The rule was simple: if an Indian ruler died without a biological heir, his rights lapsed and the State was annexed into British India.
  • Under Hindu law, adoption was a legitimate mechanism for succession: adopted sons inherited with full legitimacy.
  • The Doctrine of Lapse refused to recognise this.
Teacher's board
  • Court of Directors = "top decision-making body of the EIC", i.e. the Board of Directors, "not a Government body." "PRELIMS": who formulated the doctrine (Court of Directors, 1847) against who implemented it (Dalhousie 48-56, "Author of DoL" in common speech).
  • "Rights lapsed" = R2 Rule, R2 Pension: the right to rule and the right to the pension both lapsed. "DoL overrides Hindu Law."

4 · The Inam Commission (1852)#

Story link

Because the same logic was turned on land, tax-free grants were tested for proof of title. Ask: where, in what year, and how many estates?

Dalhousie 48-56 also targeted privileged landholdings.

  • In 1852 the Bombay Presidency set up the Inam Commission to investigate inams (tax-free grants) made by earlier Indian rulers to Brahmins, temples and others.
  • The Commission demanded proof of title
  • grants with insufficient proof were confiscated.
  • Around 20,000 estates were seized in the Bombay Presidency alone.
  • The logic: the Inam Commission was the Doctrine of Lapse applied to land titles instead of thrones.
  • If you cannot prove your claim to the Company's satisfaction, the Company takes it. The logic was identical.
Teacher's board
  • Four facts to hold: Bombay Presidency, tax-free grants (inams), proof of title, about 20,000 estates.
  • The Logic row (Lapse applied to land titles) is marked "ignore": a handout aid, not for memorising.
  • The handout repeats it in the legitimacy discussion (Section 13).

5 · The Doctrine of Lapse applied to pensions: Nana Sahib#

Story link

Because Lapse also reached pensions, a Peshwa's adopted son was refused. Ask: why did this make Nana Sahib an 1857 leader?

  • Dalhousie 48-56 extended the Doctrine of Lapse to pensions.
  • Peshwa Bajirao II 96-18 died in 1851.
  • Dalhousie 48-56 refused to continue the State pension of Rs 8 lakh per annum to his adopted son Nana Sahib.
  • The argument: adopted sons inherit private wealth, not political titles or State pensions.
  • The refusal made Nana Sahib a key 1857 leader from Bithur, where Bajirao II 96-18 had been pensioned off in 1819 after the 3rd Anglo-Maratha War.

6 · Annexation in lieu of subsidy: Berar (1853)#

Story link

Because Hyderabad could not pay its subsidy, land became payment. Ask: which province in which year, and which earlier case follows the same pattern?

  • Annexation in Lieu of Subsidy is the second instrument (the slide's order) of Dalhousie 48-56.
  • Hyderabad signed the first Subsidiary Alliance in 1798 and had been paying ever-rising subsidy ever since.
  • By 1853 Hyderabad could not pay. Dalhousie 48-56 annexed Berar as permanent payment of the subsidy.
  • Recall: in 1801 half of Awadh was annexed as subsidy (Section 7).
  • The pattern is the same: unpaid subsidy becomes territory.

7 · The annexation of Awadh (1856)#

Story link

Because Awadh fell over six steps from 1765, it was not a Lapse case. Ask: why could Lapse not apply, and what pretext was used?

The six steps of Awadh's fall:

  • 1. 1765: Treaty of Allahabad (by Robert Clive 57-67) established PoIR + PoRF + a mini Subsidiary Alliance in Awadh.
  • 2. The EIC gradually increased its subsidy demands on Awadh.
  • 3. 1798: John Shore 93-98 replaced Wazir Ali 97-98 as Nawab with his uncle Sadat Ali Khan II, in return for an annual subsidy of Rs 76 lakh and the cession of Allahabad fort.
  • 4. 1801: Sadat Ali Khan II defaulted.
  • Wellesley 98-05 imposed the full Subsidiary Alliance by sending Henry Wellesley.
  • Half of Awadh (Gorakhpur, Rohilkhand and the Doab) was annexed as permanent payment of the subsidy, called the Ceded Districts
  • it was unjust, as the revenue was twice the subsidy.
  • 5. The EIC kept demanding more subsidy despite the permanent payment.
  • The Nawab had to raise taxes and by 1855 there was a threat of peasant revolts.

12.3 Policy of Subsidiary Alliance (PoSA, 1798-1858)

  • PoSA (1798-1858): the EIC subordinated Indian rulers as junior alliance partners by taking over their foreign policy and military, both funded by an annual subsidy from the Indian State, while formally promising non-interference in internal affairs.
  • The promise was routinely broken through illegal PoIR.
  • Bajirao II 96-18 signed the SA via the Treaty of Bassein 1802 to regain his throne
  • the treaty promised non-interference, but British Resident Mountstuart Elphinstone soon bypassed the Peshwa and interfered directly: creeping PoIR through the back door.
  • When Bajirao II 96-18 launched the 3rd Anglo-Maratha War (1817), it was not aggression: it was a ruler trying to shake off interference the treaty had explicitly promised would never happen.
  • No powers, but all the blame: the same pattern as Awadh 1856 (Section 7).

12.4 Policy of Paramountcy / Subordinate Isolation (1813-58)

  • Initiated by Francis Hastings 13-23. From 1813 the EIC formally declared itself paramount: the supreme power among all States in India.
  • To protect British interests it would interfere in internal affairs and annex if needed.
  • (Earlier this happened in practice; from 1813 the EIC shed the pretence.
  • Indian States were to have no rights if British interests demanded otherwise.)
  • Diplomatic isolation (cutting off a State's right to deal with other States independently) had existed since early SA treaties.
  • After 1813 every Indian State, large or small, was locked in a sealed bubble: no communication, negotiation or alliance with any other State without the EIC as absolute gatekeeper.
  • Conceptually: Policy of Paramountcy = Policy of SA minus the rights of the Indian State.

12.5 Policy of Subordinate Union (1858-1947)

  • After 1857 the EIC was abolished and the British State took direct control.
  • Indian rulers were now not just subordinate but legally part of the British Empire, hence "union." Paramountcy became the formal legal relationship between the British Monarch and the Indian Prince.
  • (Prince, not King:
    • Indian rulers were now legally junior members of the British imperial family.) Sovereignty and the external sovereign functions (Defence, Communications, Foreign Affairs) were with the British Crown
    • Domestic sovereign functions remained with the Indian Prince.
  • Conceptually: Policy of Subordinate Union = Policy of Paramountcy + sovereignty formally with the British Crown.
Teacher's board
  • SUBORDINATE: Indian kings will be junior to the British State.
  • UNION: the British Crown = the monarch of Indian kings (not the Mughal emperor).
  • Hence from 1858 Indian "Kings" were called Indian "Princes": a Prince is the son of a King, and the King is the British monarch.
  • Therefore the whole of India is part of the British empire, split into British India (ruled by the British) and 565 princely States (ruled by an Indian Prince).
  • Symbolic sovereignty:
    • Under Subordinate Union the British hold external sovereignty (D, C, FA: Defence, Communications, Foreign Affairs) and the Indian Prince holds domestic sovereign functions
    • under SA the split was the same, but the Indian king held symbolic sovereignty and the EIC held the external functions (D, C, FA)
Handout alert
  • Do not use "Prince" and "King" loosely in answers about 1858 onwards.
  • The handout and the teacher both say:
    • After 1858 the correct word is Indian Prince
    • "King" is the pre-1858 word
  • Paramountcy of the EIC (1813-58) and Paramountcy of the Crown (1858-1947) are two different legal relationships.

12.6 The arc in five steps

The arc: 1600 to 1858 in five steps

  • 1600: a trading company that wanted to be left alone (Relative Isolation)
  • 1765: a military alliance partner (PoIR and PoRF; ToA 1765)
  • 1798: a dominant power controlling neighbours' armies and foreign policies (PoSA)
  • 1813: a State openly declaring all others subservient to its interests (Paramountcy, Francis Hastings 13-23)
  • 1858: a Crown that absorbed 565 Indian princes as legal subordinates (Subordinate Union)

13.2 Post-Dalhousie: Canning 56-62 inherits and the Queen ends Lapse

  • Dalhousie 48-56 handed power to Canning 56-62 in 1856, who inherited the empire Dalhousie 48-56 built and the revolt his annexations had sown the seeds of.
  • After the suppression of the 1857 revolt, the Government of India Act 1858 abolished the Company State and transferred power to the British Crown.
  • Queen Victoria's Proclamation of 1858 formally ended the Doctrine of Lapse and pledged that the treaties and rights of Indian rulers would be respected: an explicit reversal of Dalhousie 48-56's core policy.
  • Paramountcy became Subordinate Union (Section 12.5).
  • No further annexations followed, a line Dalhousie 48-56's successors (including Viceroy Lytton 76-80) did not cross, even where Paramountcy-era instruments were tightened (the Treaty of Jacobabad 1876 with Kalat).
  • The empire was complete
  • It only needed managing.
