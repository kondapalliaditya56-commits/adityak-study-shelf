2020PYQ 1(Prelims 2020) — Indigo Cultivation Decline by Early 20th C

Indigo cultivation in India declined by the beginning of the 20th century because of

Answer: (B)

(B) ✓ — Per the record, the long-term death blow to natural indigo came from chemistry, not peasant resistance. In 1897, the German company BASF developed synthetic indigo, which was cheaper and more consistent than the natural product. As synthetic indigo flooded global markets, the demand for natural indigo collapsed — destroying the economic logic of the plantation system.

Why the other options are wrong:

(A) ✗ — Peasant resistance (Indigo Revolt 1859–63) ended forced cultivation in Bengal and limited Indigo expansion, but did not by itself cause the early-20th-century DECLINE — the synthetic-indigo breakthrough did. Per: the long-term death blow to natural indigo, however, came not from peasant resistance but from chemistry.

(C) ✗ — National leaders' opposition is associated with the 1917 Champaran Satyagraha (Mahatma Gandhi) and the Champaran Agrarian Act 1918, which ended the Bihar Tinkathia System — but the GLOBAL DECLINE of natural indigo was already underway from 1897 (BASF synthetic indigo) and well-advanced by the time of Champaran.

(D) ✗ — Government control over planters (e.g. the Indigo Commission 1860 ruling) did not cause the early-20th-century DECLINE — that ruling addressed contract terms in 1860, decades before the global market collapse.

Note: Indigo cultivation declined by the early 20th century because of its unprofitability in the world market after BASF developed synthetic indigo in 1897. The Bihar Tinkathia System chapter of this story closed only in 1918 with the Champaran Agrarian Act, following Mahatma Gandhi's 1917 satyagraha.

FYQ 1

Why others wrong: (a) 1757 was Plassey, not Indigo loans; (b) 1772 is the year of Warren Hastings' reforms, unrelated; (d) 1793 is the Permanent Settlement, which post-dated the Indigo loans.

FYQ 3

With reference to the Indigo Revolt (1859–63), consider the following statements: 1. The Nij System failed because buying land and arranging labour was expensive and complicated. 2. Under the Ryoti System, the planter himself bought the land and cultivated Indigo directly. 3. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 had made land a commodity that could be sold, rented, leased, or mortgaged. Which of the statements given above are correct?

Answer: (B)

(B)

(B) ✓ — Per, Statement 1 matches The Nij System... faced a practical problem. Buying large tracts of land, organising labour, and arranging bullocks & ploughs was expensive & complicated. Statement 3 matches after the Permanent Settlement of 1793, zamindars were large landowners & land had become a commodity that could be sold, rented, leased, or mortgaged. Statement 2 is wrong — under the Ryoti System the planter LEASED land from zamindars and signed contracts with tenant ryots; he did not buy and cultivate directly (that was the Nij System).

Why others wrong: (a) includes Statement 2 which inverts Nij and Ryoti; (c) includes the same wrong Statement 2 and drops correct Statement 1; (d) includes wrong Statement 2.

Memory hook: Nij = own & cultivate; Ryoti = lease & contract.

FYQ 6

Which of the following Acts gave the Bengal ryot the legal protection he later invoked in court to enforce his occupancy rights against Indigo planters?

Answer: (C)

(C)

(C) ✓ — Per, When planters attempted eviction, ryots went to court to enforce their occupancy rights under the Rent Act of 1859.

Why others wrong: (a) The Permanent Settlement of 1793 turned zamindars into large landowners; it did not grant occupancy rights to ryots; (b) The Bengal Tenancy Act of 1885 came much later, out of the Pabna agitation; (d) The Deccan Agriculturists Relief Act of 1879 dealt with Maharashtra moneylender debt, not Bengal Indigo.

Memory hook: 1859 Rent Act — the ryot's shield in court.

FYQ 7

FYQ 8

The Champaran Agrarian Act of 1918 brought to an end which of the following systems?

Answer: (D)

(D)

(D) ✓ — Per, The Bihar chapter of this story would close only in 1917, when Mahatma Gandhi (MG) launched the Champaran Satyagraha, leading to the Champaran Agrarian Act of 1918, which brought the Tinkathia System to its end.

Why others wrong: (a) The Permanent Settlement of 1793 was not ended by the 1918 Act; (b) The Ryotwari System operated in Madras and Bombay Presidencies, not Bihar; (c) The Nij System had already failed commercially in Bengal long before; the 1918 Act addressed the Bihar Tinkathia arrangement.

Memory hook: Champaran 1918 — Tinkathia's tombstone.

FYQ 1 "The peasant revolts after 1857 were the rehearsal for the methods that the Indian National Movement would later perfect." Examine this view with specific reference to non-cooperation, no-tax, boycott, and legal-challenge methods used in these revolts.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Defend the "rehearsal" framing by mapping each INM method to a specific pre-INM peasant-revolt instance.

Guiding themes:

1. Open with 'A Thread Running Through All These Revolts' verdict — these revolts are "the soil in which that movement would grow" + ◆ box "rehearsal" framing.

2. Non-cooperation — Indigo Revolt 1859-63 (see 'The Indigo Revolt (1859–63)'): social boycott of Indian agents of planters = Non-Cooperation decades before Gandhi.

3. No-tax campaign — Maharashtra 1873-74 (see 'Maharashtra') + Maharashtra 1896-97 (see 'Maharashtra') + Punjab 1907 Canal Colony agitation (see 'Punjab'). Three separate no-tax campaigns before the INM made it a signature method.

4. Boycott — Indigo Revolt social boycott (see 'The Indigo Revolt (1859–63)') + no-rent campaign in court (see 'The Indigo Revolt (1859–63)').

5. Legal challenge — Indigo ryots using Rent Act 1859 occupancy rights (see 'The Indigo Revolt (1859–63)'); Pabna Agrarian Leagues pooling money for court cases ('Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal' ¶2); Poona Sarvajanik Sabha collecting 17,000 powers-of-attorney (see 'Maharashtra').

6. Close: the INM did not invent these methods; it scaled and pan-Indianised what peasants had already demonstrated in regional struggles.

Revise: 'What Changed After 1857' + 'What Did the Ryots Do?' + 'A Thread Running Through All These Revolts'

FYQ 2 "The Indigo Revolt (1859–63) marked the entry of the Western Educated Middle Class into peasant causes." Examine the contribution of the WEMC to the revolt and assess its long-term significance for Indian political life.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Trace the WEMC's specific contributions to the Indigo Revolt + judge what this entry meant for the trajectory of Indian politics.

Guiding themes:

1. Open with 'The Indigo Revolt (1859–63)' framing — the WEMC's involvement represents "the spirit of Fraternity at work here — a key element of Modern Nationalism. The educated class standing up for the oppressed peasant was a new phenomenon in Indian public life."

2. Specific contributions per 'The Indigo Revolt (1859–63)':

(i) Neel Darpan (Blue Mirror, 1860) by DeenBandhu Mitra — public-awareness play. (ii) Michael Madhusudhan Datta's English translation + James Long's publication; libel case + Rs 1,000 fine paid by Kaliprasanna Sinha + 1 month imprisonment — turning the Indigo issue into an international public-opinion event. (iii) Hindu Patriot (English) + Somprakash (Bengali) — sustained editorial advocacy. (iv) British Indian Association 1851 (educated zamindars) — class-interest convergence with peasants against European planter dominance.

3. Long-term significance — 'What Changed After 1857': "The peasant was no longer alone." This is the Fraternity element of Modern Nationalism beginning to take shape in concrete practice.

4. Caveat — 'Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal' ¶3 (Pabna) shows the WEMC was not monolithic; it could side with zamindars against ryots when the landlord was Indian. So WEMC entry into peasant causes was real, but conditional.

5. Close: the Indigo Revolt established the template — WEMC could and did stand for peasant interests — even if subsequent revolts (Pabna) showed the WEMC's commitments were not unconditional. The INM would later have to negotiate this same WEMC-vs-zamindar tension.

Revise: 'What Changed After 1857' + 'The Role of the Western Educated Middle Class (WEMC)' + 'Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal' for the WEMC-divided contrast

FYQ 3 "Post-1857 peasant resistance was characterised by an awareness of the instruments of oppression, not just the wielders of oppression." Discuss with reference to specific revolts in Bengal, Maharashtra, and Punjab.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Show how peasants targeted SYSTEMS (debt bonds, contracts, fiscal mechanisms) rather than PERSONS (moneylenders, planters); cite specific revolts.

Guiding themes:

1. Open with the Chapter's analytical insight — 'Action': "The ryots did not attack moneylenders' lives. They burned debt bonds. (An awareness of the instrument of oppression, not just its wielder.)" This framing recurs across the chapter.

2. Bombay Deccan Riots 1875 — 'Maharashtra': ryots burned the debt bonds, not the moneylenders themselves. Specific target: the legal instrument (debt bond) that perpetuated the cycle of dispossession via the Limitation Law 1859 workaround.

3. Indigo Revolt 1859-63 — 'The Indigo Revolt (1859–63)': ryots refused contracts (the legal instrument of forced cultivation) + went to court under Rent Act 1859 (a different legal instrument that gave them occupancy rights). Two legal instruments — one rejected, one wielded — both consciously chosen.

4. Pabna Agrarian Leagues 1873 — 'Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal': ryots organised legal-action structures (Agrarian Leagues + pooled-money court funds) rather than attacking zamindars directly. The instrument targeted was the legal-rights mechanism in Rent Act 1859 (already won) plus the new Bengal Tenancy Act 1885 (still being shaped).

5. Punjab 1907 Canal Colony — 'Punjab': peasants targeted the proposed BILL (legal instrument) through a no-tax campaign — and the British withdrew the bill. The instrument was the law itself.

6. Close: this systemic instrument-awareness is the precise quality that distinguishes post-1857 peasant resistance from pre-1857 revolts (which had targeted persons + property of oppressors per Chapter 20: 'Understanding the Character of Pre-1857 Revolts'). It also previews the INM's later focus on laws + institutions as the levers of change.

Revise: 'What Did the Ryots Do?' + 'Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal' + 'Bombay Deccan Riots (1875)' + 'Punjab Canal Colony Agitations (1907)'

FYQ 4 "The Bengal Tenancy Act 1885 was the legislative resolution of a peasant struggle that had begun decades earlier." Trace this from the Rent Act 1859 onwards, with reference to the Indigo Revolt, Pabna, and Ripon80-84's 1880 Rent Commission.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Walk the legislative arc from Rent Act 1859 to Bengal Tenancy Act 1885, showing how peasant agitation drove each step.

Guiding themes:

1. Open with the legislative arc verdict — 'Result'. The BTA 1885 closed the same-plot loophole in Rent Act 1859 that zamindars had exploited via eviction-before-12-years.

2. Rent Act 1859 — set up after decades of peasant agitation. Granted occupancy rights to ryots who cultivated the SAME plot continuously for 12+ years. 'Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal' ¶1.

3. Indigo Revolt 1859-63 (see 'The Indigo Revolt (1859–63)') — ryots wielded Rent Act 1859 in court against eviction by planters. The Act's protective potential demonstrated.

4. Zamindar workaround — eviction before 12-year continuity completed ('Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal' ¶1, 'The Indigo Revolt (1859–63)' background). Rent Act 1859 became a loophole-vulnerable shield.

5. Pabna Agrarian Leagues 1873 ('Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal' ¶2) — formal organisation + court cases + Ishan Chandra Roy replication. Sustained legal pressure highlighting the Rent Act 1859 enforcement failures.

6. Ripon80-84's Rent Commission 1880 (see 'Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal') — the British response. Commission led to Bengal Tenancy Act 1885 — closing the loophole by extending occupancy rights to DIFFERENT plots in same village + 12-year threshold.

7. Implementation difference — 'Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal' closes: "The Act was properly implemented and finally brought genuine relief to Bengal's ryots." The 1885 Act succeeded where the 1859 Act had been defeated by zamindar workarounds, because the political pressure built up over two decades made enforcement unavoidable.

8. Close: BTA 1885 is the canonical case of peasant struggle driving legislative reform in colonial India. The pattern — agitation → law → loophole → renewed agitation → revised law — repeats throughout the INM era.

Revise: 'What Did the Ryots Do?' + 'Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873), Bengal' + 'Result'

FYQ 5 "The 1907 Punjab Canal Colony agitations were withdrawn not out of liberal sympathy but out of strategic calculation about the Indian Army." Examine this proposition with reference to the post-1857 Army recruitment policy.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Show why the British backed down in 1907 — anchor in the post-1857 army restructuring's Punjab-recruitment focus.

Guiding themes:

1. Open with 'Punjab' framing — the British response was "twofold — repression, and then quiet withdrawal of the bill" — and the closing strategic calculation: "post-1857, the Indian Army was heavily recruited from Punjab, particularly from the Sikh community. Disaffection among Punjab's peasantry could spread to the army — a risk the British simply could not afford."

2. Post-1857 Army recruitment context — cross-reference Ch 21 'Changes in the Army — Peel Commission'. Post-1857 army was deliberately recruited from "martial races" (Jats, Rajputs, Sikhs, Gurkhas) from REGIONS THAT STAYED LOYAL IN 1857. Punjab was the centrepiece of this recruitment policy.

3. The risk in 1907 — if Punjab's peasantry rose in agitation, the Sikh soldiers (whose families were peasants) would face conflicting loyalties between their farmer kin and their British employer. A repeat of the Bengal Army's 1857 fate, with the dominant martial race playing the part the Bengal Army had played 50 years earlier.

4. Bill contents ('Punjab' ¶1) — government control of inheritance + water rate hikes + fine increases. Each provision directly affected peasant land + peasant household economics. The bill was carefully designed to maximise revenue — but ill-considered against the Punjab-Army-recruitment lens.

5. Three organisations ('Punjab' ¶2) — Lahore Indian Association 1877 + Arya Samaj 1875 + Singh Sabha 1873. The presence of the Singh Sabha — a Sikh religious-reform body — particularly worried the British because it intersected directly with the Sikh-soldier community.

6. Outcome — the bill was withdrawn. Not because the British conceded the principle, but because the strategic cost of pursuing it exceeded the revenue benefit.

7. Close: the 1907 Canal Colony withdrawal is a textbook case of post-1857 British policy being constrained by post-1857 British recruitment policy. The very army structure designed to prevent another 1857 had created a new constraint on British fiscal autonomy in Punjab — an unintended consequence of the "martial races" policy.

Revise: 'Punjab Canal Colony Agitations (1907)' + Ch 21 'Changes in the Army — Peel Commission' — particularly 'Martial Races' for the recruitment-policy cross-reference
