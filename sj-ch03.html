1 · Scheduled Castes · Status · Atrocities · Manual Scavenging · Pemsr ACT 2013 · Namaste#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2024 (affirmative action benefits not reaching underprivileged) · Social Justice handout pp. 21-24. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • SCs are 16.6% of the population with lower literacy (66.1%), land ownership and health indicators. Atrocities persist (about 57,000 cases under the SC/ST Act in 2022; conviction about 30%). Manual scavenging is the starkest caste-based occupation: 98% Dalits, 95% women. Law: PEMSR Act 2013; programme: NAMASTE (2023) covering 4,800 ULBs with Rs 349.70 crore.

Status AND KEY Challenges OF SCs

Key point

Scheduled Castes are communities historically subjected to untouchability, social exclusion and economic deprivation. They form 16.6% of India's population (Census 2011), with highest concentration in Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Bihar and Tamil Nadu.

Key data on SCs

  • Population share 16.6% (Census 2011).
  • Literacy rate 66.1% against national 73%.
  • 71% of SC farmers work as agricultural labourers on land they do not own.
  • IMR among SCs is 83 per 1000 live births against national average of 61.8.
  • Only 28% of SCs had access to electricity in 2011 against national 48%.

Key challenges

  • Caste-based discrimination in temples, schools, public spaces and workplaces.
  • Untouchability continues in subtle forms despite Article 17.
  • Landlessness: most SCs are agricultural labourers without land ownership.
  • Educational gap due to poor school infrastructure, drop-outs and hidden discrimination.
  • Occupational segregation: SCs concentrated in stigmatised jobs like sanitation.
  • Internal disparities: some SC sub-groups corner most benefits while others remain excluded.
  • Political under-representation beyond reserved seats.

Atrocities Against Scheduled Castes

Key point

Despite constitutional protection, atrocities against SCs continue to rise. NCRB 2022 recorded around 57,000 cases under the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act.

Forms of atrocities

  • Physical violence including lynching and rape.
  • Social boycotts and denial of public services.
  • Caste-based humiliation in schools and workplaces.
  • Honour crimes against inter-caste couples.
  • Burning of houses and crops in villages.
In the news (Saarthi IAS CA; verify dates)

Hathras, Una and Bhima Koregaon incidents have shown that caste violence persists. Conviction rates remain low at around 30% under the SC/ST Act, reflecting weak prosecution.

Mechanisms to tackle atrocities

  • SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 with 2015 and 2018 amendments.
  • Exclusive Special Courts under Section 14 of the Act.
  • National Commission for Scheduled Castes under Article 338.
  • Compensation under SCSP for victims.

Manual Scavenging IN India

Key point

Manual scavenging is the starkest symbol of caste-based occupational segregation. It is officially banned but continues in practice.

Scale and impact

  • 98% of manual scavengers are Dalits and 95% are women (UN Women 2014, Shah et al. 2015).
  • From 1993 to 2020, 1013 sewer deaths were officially recorded with rare convictions.
  • 40 plus deaths reported in just six months of 2024 (Safai Karmachari Andolan).
  • Out of 775 districts, 456 are now declared manual-scavenging free (January 2025).

Legal framework: Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act, 2013 (PEMSR)

  • Bans manual scavenging in all forms.
  • Mandates mechanisation of sewer cleaning.
  • Provides for rehabilitation including cash assistance, scholarships, housing and skill training.
  • Replaced the weaker 1993 Act.

Initiatives to end manual scavenging

  • NAMASTE Scheme (National Action for Mechanised Sanitation Ecosystem) trains sewer and septic tank workers and provides PPE kits.
  • National Safai Karamcharis Finance and Development Corporation (NSKFDC) for livelihoods.
  • National Commission for Safai Karamcharis (NCSK).
  • Rashtriya Garima Abhiyan for restoring dignity.

Structural reasons for persistence

  • Entrenched caste system and social stigma.
  • Lack of alternative livelihoods and education.
  • Poor sanitation infrastructure and slow mechanisation.
  • Weak enforcement and apathy from authorities.
Way forward / key point

The way forward demands strict enforcement, full mechanisation, robust rehabilitation including land and skill, and sustained societal change through education and media.

Namaste Scheme

Key point

Launched in 2023 by the Ministries of Social Justice and Empowerment and Housing and Urban Affairs, it seeks to ensure the safety, dignity and livelihood security of sanitation workers. It covers over 4,800 Urban Local Bodies with an outlay of ₹349.70 crore up to 2025-26.

Key objectives

  • Achieve zero sanitation fatalities.
  • End direct contact with human faecal matter.
  • Replace hazardous cleaning with mechanised sanitation.
  • Promote trained workers, self-employment and sanitation enterprises.

Major components

  • Profiling of sewer and septic tank workers.
  • Safety training, PPE kits and mechanised equipment.
  • Health coverage under Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY.
  • Safety devices for Emergency Response Sanitation Units.
  • Skill training, credit and capital subsidy under the Swachhata Udyami Yojana.
  • Coordination among urban bodies, ministries and sanitation schemes.

Inclusion of waste pickers

  • Waste pickers were included in 2024. The Waste Picker Enumeration App aims to profile 2.5 lakh workers and provide identity cards, health insurance, PPE kits, training and livelihood support. It also recognises their contribution to the circular economy.
Way forward / key point

Significance: NAMASTE shifts sanitation from unsafe manual work to a mechanised and professional system. It promotes occupational safety, social security, human dignity, environmental justice and sustainable livelihoods.

In simple words
  • Safeguards run through Articles 14, 15(4), 16(4), 17, 23, 46, 330, 332, 335, 338 and 341. The Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955 punishes untouchability; the 1989 Atrocities Act has Special Courts (s.14), new offences in 2015 and the 2018 restoration of immediate arrest. Schemes cover scholarships, PM-AJAY, SHRESHTA, ASIIM, the Venture Capital Fund, VISVAS and more.

KEY ACTS FOR Protection OF SCs

Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955

  • Made the practice of untouchability in any form a punishable offence.
  • Covers denial of access to shops, public places of worship, hotels and educational institutions.
  • Punishment of imprisonment and fine.

Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989

  • Defines specific offences as atrocities with stringent punishment.
  • Special Courts for speedy trial under Section 14.
  • 2015 Amendment added new offences including social boycott, garlanding with footwear and forcing to consume inedible substances.
  • 2018 Amendment restored the original provision of immediate arrest without preliminary inquiry after the Subhash Kashinath Mahajan judgment created concerns.
Key point

Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act, 2013: covered under manual scavenging in the previous chapter.

3 · Scheduled Tribes · Status · Constitutional Provisions · PESA · Forest Rights ACT · MSP FOR MFP#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2024 (affirmative action benefits not fully reaching underprivileged) · Social Justice handout pp. 25-28. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • STs (8.6%, 10.45 crore) are defined under Articles 366(25) and 342. They face land alienation, displacement, forest conflicts and health gaps. The framework: Articles 15(4), 16(4), 19(5), 244 (Schedules V and VI), 275(1), 330, 338A, 342; PESA 1996 and the FRA 2006 (51.2 lakh claims, 25.1 lakh titles); and MSP for 87 minor forest produce items through TRIFED.

Status AND KEY Challenges OF Tribal Population

Key point

Scheduled Tribes are constitutionally defined under Article 366(25) read with Article 342. They form 8.6% of India's population. Madhya Pradesh has the highest absolute number while Lakshadweep has the highest percentage of STs.

Key data on STs

  • Population of 10.45 crore (Census 2011).
  • 40.6% ST population below poverty line.
  • Literacy rate of 59% against general population's 73%.
  • Sex ratio of 990 (better than national 940).
  • IMR significantly higher than national average.

Criteria for identifying STs (1965 Lokur Committee)

  • Primitive traits.
  • Geographical isolation.
  • Distinct culture.
  • Shyness of contact with the general community.
  • Economic backwardness.
Memory aid · PGDSE

Five Lokur criteria: Primitive traits, Geographical isolation, Distinct culture, Shyness of contact, Economic backwardness. Hook: "PGDSE" = "People Guarding Different Secluded Ecosystems" (order is free). (own mnemonic)

Key challenges

  • Land alienation: tribal lands are taken for mining, dams and industry.
  • Displacement: STs form over 40% of development-induced displaced persons despite being only 8.6% of population.
  • Forest conflicts between conservation laws and customary rights.
  • Health vulnerabilities: sickle cell disease, malnutrition and high maternal mortality.
  • Educational gaps despite EMRS and tribal hostels.
  • Cultural erosion: forced assimilation threatens tribal languages and customs.
  • Naxalism in tribal belts reflects governance failure.
  • Trafficking of tribal women and children.

Key point

Schedule V covers tribal areas in 10 States with a Tribes Advisory Council. Schedule VI covers tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram with Autonomous District Councils.

KEY Legislations FOR Tribal Rights

Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA)

  • Extends Panchayati Raj to Schedule V tribal areas with adaptations.
  • Mandates that the Gram Sabha has powers over land, water, forest, minor minerals and consultation in land acquisition.
  • Tribal customs and traditions get statutory recognition.
  • Implementation remains weak as many States have not framed PESA rules.

Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 (FRA)

  • A landmark law that aims to undo historical injustice against forest-dwelling communities by recognising their rights over forest land and produce.
  • Individual rights for self-cultivation and habitation up to 4 hectares.
  • Community rights for grazing, fishing and access to water bodies.
  • Community Forest Resource Rights for management of forests.
  • Habitat Rights for Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups.
  • Gram Sabha as the authority to determine rights.

FRA implementation status

  • 51.2 lakh claims filed as of May 2025.
  • 44.3 lakh (85.4%) claims processed.
  • 25.1 lakh titles distributed including 23.9 lakh individual and 1.21 lakh community titles.
  • 69.2 lakh acres of forest land distributed from 2023 to 2026.

Challenges in FRA implementation

  • Lack of political will: projects override community rights.
  • Bureaucratic resistance with high rejection rates and rigid evidence demands like satellite imagery.
  • Conflict with Indian Forest Act, 1927 creating legal ambiguity.
  • Forest Conservation Amendment Act, 2023 raised concerns about diluting Gram Sabha consent.
  • Greater emphasis on individual rights over community forest rights.
  • Digital barriers like VanMitra software in low-connectivity areas.
Key point

The SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 also applies to STs with the same protections discussed in the SC chapters.

MSP FOR Minor Forest Produce

Key point

Minor Forest Produce (MFP) sustains around 100 million people, mostly tribals. The Mechanism for Marketing of MFP through MSP and Development of Value Chain was launched in 2013.

Key features

  • MSP for 87 MFP items including tendu leaves, mahua, sal seeds, tamarind and bamboo.
  • TRIFED as the nodal agency.
  • Van Dhan Vikas Kendras for value addition.
  • 15 SHGs per cluster with around 300 beneficiaries.
  • 100% central funding with Rs 15 lakh per cluster.
  • Bamboo was reclassified as MFP in 2017, enabling free harvest by tribals, a major income booster.

Challenges

  • State procurement agencies under-equipped for large-scale procurement.
  • Low awareness among tribals about MSP rates.
  • Exploitation by middlemen.
  • Inadequate storage and processing infrastructure.

4 · Denotified, Nomadic AND Semi-nomadic Tribes · PVTGs · Tribal Schemes · Nehru's Panchsheel#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Social Justice handout pp. 26-28. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • DNTs were branded "born criminal" by the Criminal Tribes Act 1871. They remain the most neglected, often outside SC/ST/OBC lists. PVTGs (75 groups across 18 States and UTs, Odisha 13) were identified by the Dhebar Commission (1973). Flagship: Dharti Aaba Janjatiya Gram Utkarsh Abhiyan (2024, Rs 79,156 crore, 63,000 villages). Nehru's Panchsheel still guides tribal policy.

Denotified, Nomadic AND Semi-nomadic Tribes

Key point

Denotified Tribes (DNTs) are communities that were notified as "born criminal" under the Criminal Tribes Act, 1871 during British rule. They were denotified after Independence but the stigma continues.

Key facts

  • DNTs are the most neglected, marginalised and deprived communities.
  • Many do not figure in SC, ST or OBC lists, creating exclusion from welfare.
  • Idate Commission (2014) identified 1262 denotified, nomadic and semi-nomadic communities.
  1. 1949
    Ayyangar Committee recommended repeal of the Criminal Tribes Act.
  2. 1953
    Kalelkar Committee suggested calling them Denotified Communities.
  3. 2008
    Renke Commission estimated DNT population at around 10 to 11 crore.
  4. 2014
    Idate Commission prepared draft lists for inclusion in SC, ST, OBC.

Government initiatives

  • National Commission for Denotified, Nomadic and Semi-Nomadic Tribes (NCDNT).
  • Development and Welfare Board for DNTs, NTs and SNTs.
  • SEED (Scheme for Economic Empowerment of DNT Communities): free coaching, health insurance, livelihood support.
  • Dr Ambedkar Pre Matric and Post Matric Scholarships.
  • Nanaji Deshmukh Scheme for construction of hostels.

Demands

  • Constitutional recognition through a separate schedule.
  • Separate Census column in the proposed 2027 caste enumeration.
  • Identity documents for accessing rights and services.

Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs)

Key point

PVTGs are a sub-category of STs identified as the most marginalised tribal communities. The Dhebar Commission (1973) first identified them as Primitive Tribal Groups; they were renamed PVTGs in 2006.

Key facts

  • 75 PVTGs spread across 18 States and UTs.
  • Odisha has the highest number with 13 PVTGs.
  • Andaman tribes like Great Andamanese, Jarawa, Onge, Sentinelese and Shompen are among the most vulnerable.

Identification criteria

  • Pre-agricultural level of technology.
  • Low literacy levels.
  • Economic backwardness.
  • Declining or stagnant population.
Memory aid · PLED

PVTG criteria: Pre-agricultural technology, Low literacy, Economic backwardness, Declining population: "a PLED for help" (order is free). (own mnemonic)

Issues concerning PVTGs

  • Declining population: Great Andamanese declined from 3500 in 1858 to just 43 in 2001.
  • Poor health with high IMR and malnutrition.
  • Low literacy ranging from 10% to 44%.
  • Livelihood crisis due to deforestation and conservation policies.
  • Exploitation by middlemen in MFP trade.

Recent positive developments

  • Bharia PVTG (MP), Kamar and Baiga (Chhattisgarh) received Habitat Rights under FRA.
  • Pradhan Mantri PVTG Development Mission launched with Rs 15,000 crore allocation for 3 years.

Schemes FOR Tribal Welfare

Dharti Aaba Janjatiya Gram Utkarsh Abhiyan (launched 2024; flagship, focus on saturation of basic facilities)

  • Targets 63,000 tribal villages benefiting around 5 crore tribal people.
  • Coverage of 17 ministries for convergence.
  • Outlay of Rs 79,156 crore over 5 years.
  • Focus on infrastructure, social development, livelihoods and skill development.

Panchsheel FOR Tribal Development (nehru)

Five principles

  • 1. People should develop along the lines of their own genius.
  • 2. Tribal rights in land and forest should be respected.
  • 3. A team of their own people should be trained for development work.
  • 4. Tribal areas should not be over-administered.
  • 5. Results should be judged not by statistics but by the quality of human character.
Way forward / key point

These principles remain remarkably relevant today.

5 · Other Backward Classes · Article 340 · NCBC · 102ND AND 105TH Amendments · Challenges · Schemes#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2024 (affirmative action benefits not fully reaching underprivileged) · Social Justice handout pp. 28-29. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • OBCs are identified under Article 340; the Mandal Commission put them at 52% (the handout says current figures may be higher). The 102nd Amendment (2018) gave the NCBC constitutional status (Article 338B); the 105th (2021) restored States' power to identify SEBCs after the Maratha judgment. Problems: heterogeneity, internal disparity, no caste data since 1931, creamy layer, weak representation.

Status AND Challenges OF OBCs

Key point

OBCs are socially and educationally backward classes identified under Article 340. They form the largest backward group in India, with the Mandal Commission estimating their share at 52% of population, though current figures may be higher.

Constitutional framework

  • Article 15(4) for educational provisions.
  • Article 16(4) for employment reservation.
  • Article 340 allowing the President to appoint commissions to investigate OBC conditions.
  • Article 338B established the NCBC with constitutional status.

Key Acts and bodies

  • 102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018 granted constitutional status to the National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC).
  • 105th Constitutional Amendment, 2021 restored States' power to identify SEBCs after the Maratha reservation judgment.
  • National Backward Classes Finance and Development Corporation (NBCFDC) for credit and training.

Key challenges

  • Heterogeneity: OBCs include hundreds of castes with varying status.
  • Internal disparity: dominant OBC groups corner most benefits.
  • Lack of caste data: Mandal Commission used 1931 Census estimates.
  • Creamy layer issues with criteria not uniformly applied.
  • Educational backwardness with lower GER in higher education.
  • Limited representation in higher bureaucracy and judiciary.
