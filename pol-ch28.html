1 · Part I: Articles 1 to 4#

Part I lays down the political identity and territorial structure of India. Articles 1 to 4 set the legal foundation for territorial integrity and evolution.

Article 1: Union of States

  • "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States." The compound name reconciles the Constituent Assembly's views, some preferring the traditional name and others the contemporary one.
  • "Union of States" was preferred to "Federation of States" (as explained by Ambedkar and the Assembly debates): India is not a compact among sovereign units as in the American model, but a single, indivisible sovereign whole whose divisions exist for administrative convenience.
  • So States have no unilateral right to secede; the Union is indestructible.
Territory of India: three categories (Art. 1)
CategoryWhat it is
(i)Territories that constitute the States
(ii)Union Territories named in the First Schedule
(iii)Territories that may be acquired by the Government of India

Union of India

  • The collective of States

Territory of India

  • Broader: States, Union Territories and any future acquisitions
  • At present there are 28 States and 8 Union Territories.
  • The First Schedule is the formal register of States and UTs.
  • States share powers with the Centre; UTs and acquired territories are ordinarily run directly by the Centre. Part XXI has special provisions for specified States, and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules create separate regimes for scheduled and tribal areas.

Acquiring territory

India can acquire territory by modes recognised in international law: cession by treaty (including purchase, gift or plebiscite), occupation of territory not under a recognised sovereign, or other lawful means. Examples integrated since 1950: Dadra and Nagar Haveli, Goa, Daman and Diu, Puducherry and Sikkim.

3 · Article 3: Parliament's power to reorganise States#

Article 3 empowers Parliament to make law for:

  • Forming a new State by separating territory from a State, uniting two or more States or parts, or merging any territory with a part of a State.
  • Increasing a State's area.
  • Reducing a State's area.
  • Changing the boundaries of a State.
  • Changing the name of a State.

The procedure under Article 3

Step 1
The Bill needs the President's recommendation before it is introduced in Parliament
Step 2
The President refers the proposal to the State legislature for its views within a specified time
Views
Not binding on the President or Parliament
Amendments
Even if the Bill is amended in Parliament, no fresh consultation is needed
UT
For a Union Territory, Parliament can act on its own without referring to any legislature
Reading note

The handout says both that the Bill needs the President's recommendation to be introduced and that the President must forward it to the State legislature before granting this recommendation. The point is the same: consultation is mandatory in form, not decisive in substance.

Nature of the Union

India

  • Parliament holds complete power to redraw the map
  • States have no guaranteed territorial integrity: "An indestructible Union of destructible States"

USA

  • Federal structure protects State boundaries
  • no change without the States' consent: "An indestructible Union of indestructible States"
Article 3 verbs
FIRCC

Form, Increase, Reduce, Change boundaries, Change name: "Five Indian Rivers Cross Cities".

Article 4: ordinary law

  • A law under Article 2 or Article 3 is not an amendment under Article 368.
  • It can pass by simple majority and ordinary legislative procedure.
PYQ 1994 shortcut

Parliament can increase, diminish, alter boundary and alter name. So the statements "cannot alter the boundary" and "cannot alter the name" are the incorrect ones.

4 · Cession of territory: Berubari and boundary disputes#

Cession and the courts

Question
Does Art. 3's power to diminish a State's area allow ceding Indian territory to a foreign State?
1960
Supreme Court: No. Cession is not covered by Art. 3 and needs a constitutional amendment under Art. 368 (case: part of Berubari Union, West Bengal, proposed for Pakistan)
Result
9th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1960 passed to implement the transfer
1969
Supreme Court: settling a boundary dispute with another country does not need an amendment, as it is not a cession of Indian territory

Cession (1960)

  • Giving away Indian territory: needs Art. 368 amendment

Boundary settlement (1969)

  • Resolving a dispute over the line: no amendment needed
Court rulings
CB

Cession needs amendment (1960), Boundary settlement does not (1969): "Cede = Change the Constitution, Boundary = Bypass".

Handout alert

The handout says the 9th Amendment (1960) was passed to implement the Berubari transfer, but also that it could not be operationalised for territories concerning former East Pakistan because of political complexities and litigation. So it was enacted in 1960, and the 100th Amendment (2015) finally settled the matter.

6 · Integration of princely States and the 1950 map#

7 · Language as the basis of reorganisation#

In independent India the main basis for reorganising States was language, a break from the colonial pattern of administrative convenience.

For linguistic States

  • 1920s Congress consensus
  • Gandhiji organised Pradesh Congress Committees on linguistic lines
  • language is more secular than religion or caste
  • mother-tongue education
  • masses participate better
  • regional languages preserved and enriched

Against linguistic States

  • Nehru, after seeing Partition, feared Lilliputian States
  • focus should be national identity
  • language binds strongly so secession demands may follow, as in Europe
  • may raise demands for jobs and subsidies and xenophobia against people from other States

Commissions and committees

8 · States Reorganisation Act, 1956#

The Commission recommended abolishing Parts A, B, C and D and creating 16 States and 3 centrally administered territories. The Government accepted with minor modifications.

Handout alert

The Commission proposed 16 States + 3 territories, but the 1956 outcome is 14 States and 6 Union Territories on 1 November 1956. Use 16 + 3 for the recommendation, 14 + 6 for the result.

10 · Second SRC and name changes#

Case for a 2nd State Reorganisation Commission

  • Demands persist for Koshal (Odisha), Kodagu (Karnataka), Vidarbha and Konkan (Maharashtra), Bodoland (Assam), Maru Pradesh (Rajasthan), Mithilanchal (Bihar), Gorkhaland (West Bengal).
  • The Union views these demands through a political lens instead of rational, scientific criteria.
  • A 2nd SRC would evolve rational, scientific and objective criteria.
  • State reorganisation is an evolving exercise, not one-time.
  • 30 years of LPG reforms brought demographic, economic, social and regional imbalances and allegations of regional neglect.
  • Some States have grown very large in population, raising governance concerns.
Case for 2nd SRC
DRELP

Demands many, Rational criteria, Evolving process, LPG imbalances, Population size: "Don't Rush Every Little Proposal".
