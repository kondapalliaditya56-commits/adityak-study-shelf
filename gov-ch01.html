1 · Civil Services: UPSC and SPSC#

Handout only · M1 p.3-4
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • FPSC set up in 1926 (Lee Commission, 1924); Madras 1929 first provincial PSC; Sir Ross Barker first chairman
  • Article numbers 315-323 and what each covers
  • Seniority convention for UPSC Chairman broken in 2015
  • 57 deviations (1950-84) vs 82 (1984-88)

Mains hooks

  • Why the UPSC does not enjoy equal status with EC, CAG and SC (no separate oath, easier removal)
  • Negative recruitment; two 2nd ARC models for compressing the exam cycle
  • From UPSC to a Central Personnel Agency

Independent on paper, advisory in practice: the Commission needs both status and a positive recruitment mission.

Part XIV (Arts 308-323) gives the country its Public Service Commissions. They began with the 1919 Act and the 1926 FPSC, and are protected by constitutional safeguards, yet governments deviate from their advice and the UPSC lacks the status of the other constitutional bodies.

  • Civil services are dealt with in Part XIV, Articles 308 to 323. Public Service Commissions: UPSC (Centre) and SPSCs (states).

Historical context

  • GoI Act 1919: first provided for a Federal Public Service Commission. It did not make Provincial PSCs mandatory; provinces could set up their own by law. The FPSC was delayed and set up in 1926 on the recommendation of the Lee Commission (1924).
  • Madras (1929): first province to set up a Provincial PSC.
  • GoI Act 1935: three types: Federal (FPSC), Provincial (PPSC), Joint (JPSC). Carried into the Constitution under Article 315.
  • First Chairman: Sir Ross Barker, of the FPSC, in 1926.

Provisions securing independence

  • Conditions of service cannot be reduced to a member's disadvantage after appointment.
  • Salaries and allowances are charged expenditure on the Consolidated Fund (India or the State).
  • Removal only on the grounds and in the manner the Constitution provides.
  • Post-retirement disabilities bar certain later government appointments.
  • Convention: the senior-most member is elevated as UPSC Chairman; this convention was violated in 2015.

Issues in working

  • Deviation from recommendations without adequate reasons: 57 times between 1950 and 1984, against 82 instances between 1984 and 1988.
  • One-size-fits-all recruitment does not suit different services.
  • Article 320(3): abuse of its provisions and influence of three categories of officials: technical, managerial, political.
  • No specific educational qualification is prescribed for members. Institutional conflict with the CVC.
  • Unequal status: regarded as one of four important constitutional institutions (with EC, CAG, SC), yet UPSC has no separate oath and its removal process is relatively easier.
  • Exam structure and duration; negative recruitment (eliminating the unsuitable rather than finding the most suitable); archaic philosophy of recruitment.

Second ARC: two models to compress the exam cycle

  • Weaknesses of the interview: too short; desultory conversation; artificial atmosphere; element of luck; tendency to reproduce oneself; subjectivity and bias.

State PSCs

  • Some commissions have become centres of corruption; poor quality of members; blind imitation of UPSC; may function like subsidiaries of UPSC; no formal linkage between UPSC and SPSCs.

First ARC recommendations on PSCs

  • One SPSC member from outside the state. Members to hold a graduation degree. UPSC and SPSC Chairmen consulted in appointing SPSC members; UPSC Chairman consulted for UPSC members and successor. Two-thirds of UPSC members drawn from SPSC Chairpersons. Separate qualifications for official and non-official members.

Role of UPSC today

  • Evolve into a Central Personnel Agency; move from negative to positive recruitment; develop the psycho-social profile of candidates; involve outside experts; be the principal R&D body in civil services management; abandon one-size-fits-all.

2 · Civil Services: Meaning and Lateral Entry#

Handout only · M1 p.5-7
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: 2014. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • 1765 term coined; excluded groups (political executives, judiciary, defence, PSU staff)
  • 3 AIS; Third Pay Commission renamed Class 1-4 as Group A-D
  • Lateral entry: 2018 (10 JS posts, 3 yrs extendable to 5), 2021 (31 posts), Aug 2024 (45 posts, cancelled 20 Aug)
  • Type I and Type II error argument

Mains hooks

  • Aristocratic personnel system and its consequences
  • Generalist vs specialist; need for domain expertise after 10-12 years of service
  • Lateral entry: need, issues, the 2024 reservation controversy

Lateral entry fixes a skills gap but must be built on reservation, field exposure and skilled permanent staff.

The East India Company coined 'civil services' around 1765. India's closed, cadre-based, generalist system produced a conservative, status-quoist bureaucracy. Lateral entry is the attempt to bring in specialists, but it has hit reservation and field-experience objections.

  • The East India Company coined 'Civil Services' around 1765 to separate combatant from non-combatant personnel.
  • Civil servants exclude political executives, judiciary, defence forces and PSU employees. These all fall within the broader idea of public servants.
  • The State is the largest employer in India; almost 3% of GDP is spent on civil servants.

Aristocratic personnel system (features)

  • Sharp divide between grades; recruitment confined to a narrow age band; exams test only general ability; may reflect class preferences; cadre-based services.

Consequences of a closed career system

  • Conservative values; status quoist; non-responsive; rule fetishism; placement depends on rank, not competence; elitist mindset; obstacles to change.

Reasons for slow pace of social change

  • Education (traditional religious and social complexes); conservative attitudes, especially towards women; limited social and spatial mobility; failure of the political system; cadre-based civil services and aristocratic personnel system; high poverty.

Generalist vs specialist

  • Urgent need for specialisation in financial, infrastructural, social and regulatory administration after 10-12 years of service. Lateral entry tries to bridge the gap.

3 · All India Services#

Handout only · M1 p.7-9
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • 9 AIS in 1909 -> 4 in 1924 -> 2 in 1947; today 3 (IAS, IPS, IFoS)
  • Article 312; Patel as Father of AIS; Lloyd George coined 'Steel Frame' for the ICS
  • 25 cadres incl. 2 joint: AGMUT and Assam-Meghalaya
  • Entry 70 Union List; 2:1 outsider-insider ratio
  • Arts 309, 310, 311, 323A; T.S.R. Subramanian (2013)

Mains hooks

  • Rationale for continuing AIS vs states' concerns
  • Committee views: Rajamannar vs Sarkaria
  • Doctrine of Pleasure; 2nd ARC on Article 311

AIS bind the federation together, but authority and responsibility must be re-balanced between Centre and states.

AIS began under British rule, survived Constituent Assembly opposition through Patel's insistence, and rest on Article 312. States now question their logic, while commissions mostly want them strengthened.

  • AIS originated under British rule. Officers are recruited and trained by the Union but serve under both Centre and States.
  • 1909: nine AIS; 1924: four; 1947: two. Abolishing an AIS is called provincialisation. India has three today: IAS, IPS, IFoS.

Constituent Assembly debate

  • Opposition: Nehru Committee Report (1928) not keen; regional interests feared Centre's influence; British policy had moved to reduce their number; Nehru initially not in favour.
  • Retained and given constitutional status under Article 312 largely through Sardar Patel (Father of All India Services). Arguments: states lacked capacity to recruit; AIS the Steel Frame maintaining unity. David Lloyd George used 'Steel Frame' for the Imperial Civil Service.
  • Article 312 provides for the creation of AIS. 25 cadres, including two joint cadres: AGMUT (Arunachal Pradesh, Goa, Mizoram, Union Territories) and Assam-Meghalaya.

Rationale for continuing

  • Best talent joins; promotes national unity; wholesome effect on federalism; Union stays in touch with ground realities; wider national outlook in states; Centre can act through AIS in emergencies; less political consideration in key postings; integrated infrastructure development; Centre-State coordination on law and order; day-to-day coordination of bureaucracies.

Critique (states' concerns)

  • Changed circumstances: unity not threatened as at Independence; unity needs a stronger base than administration; authority-responsibility mismatch (states have limited control over AIS officers); demoralised state civil services (second-in-command); 2:1 outsider-insider ratio seen as central influence; 'sons of the soil' demands; federal concern: AIS is joint responsibility yet falls under Entry 70 of the Union List; officers spend most of their careers in one cadre state.

  • Doctrine of Pleasure: public employment cannot be equated with contract-based private employment. Civil servants must be responsive to people and responsible to the government. Termination allowed subject to constitutional safeguards.
  • 2nd ARC on Art 311: growing procedural protection made it hard to dismiss even those who deserved it. Recommended repealing Article 311, a comprehensive Civil Services Law, and clear definition of reasonable opportunity.
  • Articles 309, 310, 311, 320 govern recruitment, tenure, protection and PSC consultation.

Service rules (restrictions)

  • Cannot join a political party, give to political funds or contest elections; can vote but not disclose preference; limits on family's political involvement, adverse criticism of government and media interaction; annual declaration of assets and liabilities; report gifts.
  • T.S.R. Subramanian case (2013): SC recommended minimum guaranteed tenure in key positions; Civil Services Boards to advise on postings and transfers; avoid acting on verbal orders; enact a comprehensive Civil Services Law.

4 · Role of Civil Services in Democracy#

Handout only · M1 p.9-12
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: 2017. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • Neutrality = class, cultural, political; three forms of confidence
  • Policy neutrality is not political neutrality
  • Bhaskar Ghose's three bureaucrat types
  • Max Weber on bureaucracy as an institution
  • Lakhina Experiment, Ahmednagar (colour-coding of files)

Mains hooks

  • Why neutrality is hard to secure (4Cs, multi-party pressure)
  • Anonymity vs social accountability tools
  • Factors hindering professionalism
  • Case for and against civil service activism

Understand politics, do not play politics: neutrality in loyalty, not in intellect.

The handout lists six roles (neutrality, commitment, anonymity, instrumentality, professionality, impersonality). Each is under strain: class bias, social audits eroding anonymity, bureaucracy becoming an institution, poor training, personalised posts. Activism is a symptom of reform neglect.

  • Six roles are envisaged: neutrality, commitment, anonymity, instrumentality, professionality, impersonality.

Neutrality

  • Class neutrality: officers come mostly from the middle class, whose mindset can dominate; elite capture of state institutions (example given: National Rifle Association, USA).
  • Cultural neutrality: language, caste, religion, region and other identities.
  • Political neutrality shows through three forms of confidence:
Confidence ofMeaning
PeopleServe whichever party the people elect with diligence and commitment
MinistersPerform duties with commitment irrespective of the minister's ideology
Civil servantsPostings, careers, promotions on objective merit, not political affiliation
  • Policy vs political neutrality: ministers may lack specialised knowledge, so civil servants advise. Political neutrality is not policy neutrality. Involvement in policy must be intellectual, never emotional. Civil servants must understand politics, not play politics.
  • Problems: a thinking person is never fully neutral; the 4Cs of the political environment; parties may demand loyalty; no agreement on fundamentals (extreme right, extreme left, secular parties); multi-party democracy brings pressures at Centre and state.

Neither Wilt nor TiltFirm; refuse orders deviating from policy or law; may be transferred but keep honesty

Weather Report ReadersShift loyalty with political circumstances

Commitment

  • Impartial rule of law; code of ethics; settled policies and programmes; probity and transparency; sense of duty; welfare of Daridra, the poorest of the poor.

Anonymity

  • Civil servants work behind the scenes; the minister is the public face. Credit and major failure both go to the minister. Anonymity goes hand in hand with ministerial responsibility; ministers must protect civil servants. The concept is changing with social audits, RTI, Citizens' Charters, time-bound service delivery laws.

Instrumentality

  • Civil services are an instrument of the people, but a full-fledged bureaucracy can become an independent power structure, an institution with elitist values. Max Weber: once bureaucracy becomes an institution it is one of the hardest social structures to dismantle.

Professionality

  • A professional keeps improving knowledge and skills. Hindrances: generalists in specialist services; poor training (confined to Group A and B; only one in five civil servants can expect formal training; mid-career training often absent); ineffective appraisal (no quantifiable targets); weak incentives ('hire, but no fire'); IAS dominance with limited lateral entry.

Impersonality

  • Do not personalise the position (DC, SP). Impersonal attitude to superiors, colleagues and juniors; excessive personal relations compromise objectivity (example: overlooking a friend's corrupt behaviour).

Civil services activism

  • A civil servant becomes a crusader. Forms: going to the media; calculated leaks; going to courts; whistleblowing; grassroots activism; post-retirement activism (NGOs, YouTube channels).
  • Case study: Dr. Lakhina, Ahmednagar (1980s): colour-coded files by year so older files could be found. Called the Lakhina Experiment; example of grassroots activism.
  • Issues: may violate service rules and set a bad precedent; personalises institutional issues (e.g. targeting the Chief Minister); exposes civil services to manipulation by parties and media; erodes credibility of institutions.
  • Conclusion in handout: activism cannot be encouraged, but it is a sign of our times; civil service and electoral reforms must be implemented more effectively.

5 · Working and Performance of Civil Services#

Handout only · M1 p.12-13
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • Average District Collector tenure about one year
  • Street-level bureaucracy defined
  • The 3Ms: MTV, Macintosh, McDonald's
  • SPVs: Solar Energy Corporation, National Skill Development Corporation

Mains hooks

  • Performance table: regulatory, rule of law, developmental, facilitation
  • Causes of poor performance
  • 21st-century challenges for bureaucracy

Doer, facilitator and regulator: the bureaucracy must now play all three roles while being held to global standards.

Civil servants formulate, implement and evaluate policy and adjudicate, but performance is mixed: good on regulation, weak on facilitation. Short tenures, corruption and thin training explain much of it, and 21st-century problems demand new roles.

Functions

  • Policy formulation: channel between grassroots and top; background facts, court judgments, laws; technical considerations; policy advice with value judgments; drafting legislation; balanced policies.
  • Policy implementation: rule-making, financial management, HR management, monitoring and supervision.
  • Policy evaluation: feedback to leadership, but a conflict of interest arises when officers evaluate programmes they implemented. This raises the importance of social audits.
  • Administrative adjudication: quasi-judicial powers (District Collectors decide certain disputes through revenue courts). Continuity in administration despite political change.

Causes of poor performance

  • Political interference; dual control over AIS; reluctance to use constitutional protection (high personal and financial cost); arbitrary transfers and short tenures (average DC tenure about one year; T.S.R. Subramanian stressed tenure security); corruption in street-level bureaucracy (lower-level officials who deal directly with citizens); poor capacity building; wage compression; poor performance evaluation; lack of people's participation.

Challenges of the 21st century

  • Globalisation of domestic problems (COVID-19, money laundering, terrorism, drugs) needing multilateral coordination and negotiation skills.
  • Changing roles: doer (more cost-efficient), facilitator (local bodies, NGOs), regulator (new skills for law and order).
  • Changing accountability: global yardsticks (Transparency International, HDI, SDGs, Ease of Doing Business) plus direct citizen tools (Citizens' Charters, social audits).
  • Continuous (not episodic) reforms; professionalism; expanding horizon of rights (environment, consumer, disability, LGBT, privacy); global monoculture (the 3Ms: MTV fast music, Macintosh fast computers, McDonald's fast food); tailor-made institutions and SPVs (Solar Energy Corporation, National Skill Development Corporation); crisis of State credibility after LPG reforms and WTO participation.

6 · Bureaucracy and Development#

Handout only · M1 p.13-15
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • Rajiv Gandhi: 16 paise of every rupee reaches the people
  • Weber's four characteristics; five features of development administration
  • Four named minister-bureaucrat conflicts (Mundhra 1957, Nanda-L.P. Singh, Hanumanthaiah-Ganguli 1973, Rajiv-Venkateswaran 1987)
  • Terms: Hakim culture, Babu culture, goal displacement, politico-administrative no-man's land

Mains hooks

  • How bureaucracy promotes vs threatens democracy
  • Why traditional bureaucracy suits development administration poorly
  • Paradoxes between Indian bureaucracy and development

Like fire, bureaucracy is a good servant and a bad master.

Weber's bureaucracy (rules, hierarchy, role specificity) clashes with development administration (change, results, client focus). Indian bureaucracy's colonial, file-bound behaviour shows up as paradoxes such as the 16-paise leakage.

  • Max Weber: 'Democracy has to promote what reason demands but what democratic sentiments hate.' Democracy has a love-hate relationship with bureaucracy; bureaucracy is like fire: invaluable as servant, destructive as master.

How bureaucracy promotes democracy

  • Institutionalises rule of law; conducts free and fair elections; fulfils the six role expectations; balances policymaking; implements socio-economic justice.

Bureaucracy as a threat

  • Instrument becomes institution (resists citizen-centric reform); concentrates power oligarchically; secrecy (an 'uninformed parliament'); suppresses NGOs, pressure groups, local bodies; no representative bureaucracy (mixed class, caste, religion, occupation); corruption and lack of neutrality; state oppression (agencies used against rivals; examples ED and CBI); power struggle creating a politico-administrative no-man's land.

  • Bureaucracy is a form of organisation of work and can exist in private bodies too; civil service and bureaucracy are used interchangeably because the problems became pronounced in the public sector.
  • Conflicts: goal displacement; low adaptability (built to maintain the status quo); historical mismatch (development administration emerged after World War II); political dimension (society must be ready for development); excessive formalism, unsuitable for rural development.

Features of Indian bureaucracy

  • Structural: British pattern; long hierarchies; concentration and centralisation; cadre-based; instability of tenure (DCs 'constantly packing and unpacking'); standard departmental structure.
  • Behavioural: colonial mindset (master, not servant); non-responsive; Hakim culture; file and rule fetishism (Babu culture) with creative inertia; corruption; red tape; secrecy.

Paradoxes

  • Advanced technology amid backwardness; government spends more on itself (Rajiv Gandhi: only 16 paise of each rupee reaches the people); archaic values; authority-responsibility mismatch; unstable tenure; poor inter-agency coordination; centralised, non-participatory administration; learning disorders (example: repeated dengue outbreaks in Delhi); greater importance to generalists (specialists need rehabilitation); wage compression.

7 · Pay, 8th CPC and the Right to Strike#

Handout only · M1 p.16-17
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • 8th CPC reference date: 1 January 2026; Revised Basic Pay = Existing Basic x Fitment Factor
  • Essential Defence Services Bill 2021: 41 ordnance factories, about 84,000 workers
  • First ARC (1966) wanted unlawful strikes made a penal offence; ESMA mentioned

Mains hooks

  • Arguments for and against civil servants' right to strike
  • Fiscal and state-level impact of a pay commission
  • Way forward: regular revisions, performance-linked incentives

Fair pay and a no-strike norm for essential services protect both the employee and the citizen.

Pay is lower than the private sector but offset by security and status. The 8th CPC (reference date 1 Jan 2026) raises fitment-factor, DA and fiscal questions. On strikes, the handout settles on a balance: no strikes that disrupt essential services.

  • Bureaucrats' salaries are often lower than the private sector, but they get job security, fringe benefits, higher social status and the chance of public service. Balance: performance-linked incentives.

8th Central Pay Commission

  • 8th CPC constituted; reference date 1 January 2026. Fitment factor is the multiplier: Revised Basic Pay = Existing Basic Pay x Fitment Factor; higher factor raises allowances and pension liabilities.
  • Issues: minimum-wage model (earlier three consumption units; demand to include dependent parents); DA merger demand (but DA is absorbed then reset, so part of the rise is inflation compensation); fiscal impact (committed expenditure, less capex); pressure on states to follow.
  • Way forward: regular smaller revisions; performance orientation without hurting neutrality; workforce rationalisation; digital governance; link revisions to inflation, revenue and fiscal capacity.

Right to strike

  • Trigger: Essential Defence Services Bill, 2021 prohibiting strikes in essential defence services including ordnance factories (41 factories, nearly 84,000 workers), to ensure uninterrupted supply amid a possible two-front conflict.

8 · Civil Services Reforms and Nolan Principles#

Handout only · M1 p.17-18
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: 2017, 2020. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • Satish Chandra Committee: separate technical exams
  • NIPAs (2nd ARC): IIT/AIIMS-style entry, 3-4 years' training; one-year bridge course for other graduates
  • 360-degree appraisal used for AIS empanelment for central deputation; 2nd ARC feared victimisation
  • Nolan Committee (1995): seven principles
  • 2nd ARC drafted a Civil Services Law

Mains hooks

  • Reforms in recruitment, training, appraisal
  • Comprehensive Civil Services Law; domainisation
  • Nolan Principles as an ethical foundation

Reform is continuous: recruit for competence, train for roles, appraise for results, protect for neutrality.

Reform needs to run through recruitment, training, appraisal, tenure, law, transparency, anti-corruption and the status of specialists. The Nolan Principles give the ethical frame.

Recruitment

  • Mainstream lateral entry and reduce dependence on cadre-based services. Overhaul exams: Satish Chandra Committee recommended separate exams for technical services.
  • NIPAs (2nd ARC): after higher secondary, aspirants compete for entry like IITs and AIIMS, with 3-4 years of public administration training; other graduates could compete after a one-year bridge course.
  • Psycho-social profile assessment, possibly an SSB-style interview. Probation to weed out unsuitable recruits.

Training

Transparency is the precursor to accountability; privacy and inclusion are its limits.

Transparency means openly known criteria, processes and decisions. It is a precursor to accountability, fights corruption and enables entitlements, but has costs (burden, misuse, privacy). NDSAP 2012 and the OGD platform are the proactive tools.

  • OECD: an environment where objectives, frameworks, decisions and rationale are provided in a comprehensible, accessible, timely manner. UNESCAP: decisions and enforcement follow rules; information freely and directly accessible to those affected.
  • People have the right to know; criteria, processes and systems are openly known (example: eligibility, benefits and conditions under the Skill India Mission).

Three metaphors

  • Public value (counter corruption); open decision-making (balancing secrecy and privacy); tool of good governance (with accountability, efficiency, effectiveness).
  • Other readings: Tandon (2002); Transparency International (the mechanisms and processes behind facts and figures); open government; public scrutiny.

Objectives

  • Accountability; combating corruption (TI Global Corruption Report 2003); access to entitlements; effective service delivery; informed citizens; democratic participation; public trust.
  • Transparency is a precursor to accountability, integrity and probity. Mechanisms: RTI, e-governance, Citizens' Charters.
  • Social accountability tools: social audits (MGNREGA), public expenditure tracking, Citizen Report Cards, public hearings.
  • E-governance streamlines processes and reduces discretion but faces digital divide, privacy, cybersecurity and low literacy.
  • Challenges due to transparency: administrative burden from non-serious applications; misuse by anti-social elements; questionable authenticity; wrong interpretation; privacy and confidentiality.
  • Key measures: RTI Act 2005, NDSAP 2012, Citizens' Charters, e-governance, CPGRAMS.

Prelims hooks

Mains hooks

  • Problems with the Indian programme and suggestions
  • ARC-II recommendations

Commit only to what you can deliver, say who is accountable, and measure it through someone outside.

A Charter promises quality and time for existing rights. India's charters fail because of limited consultation, no penalties, low awareness, over- or under-tight standards and jargon. ARC-II and Sevottam (2006) supply the fix: separate charters, realistic commitments, redressal, feedback, outside review.

  • Launched in 1991 by the John Major government (UK). A declaration of an organisation's commitment to clients on qualitative and temporal entitlements. It does not create new rights; it seeks enforcement of existing rights and includes grievance redress.

Purposes served

  • Clarifies expectations; makes government responsive; service orientation over master mindset; people's participation via consultation; strengthens accountability (quality standards and timelines let performers be distinguished); signals pro-active good governance; improves image and credibility; empowerment and democracy; instrument for development goals.

Elements of the Indian programme

  • Vision and mission; details of business transacted; details of clients served; services to each client group; grievance redressal machinery and how to access it; expectations from clients (documents, fees); compensation details. Regular internal and external monitoring. By 2011 end: 131 central charters, 729 state charters. Website goicharters.nic.in.

Problems

  • Bureaucratic mindset; limited consultation (a manager's charter); no penal provisions; low awareness; standards too lax or too strict; too detailed or too brief; names and addresses of redressal functionaries missing; no incentives (UK Charter Mark; DARPG hired an outside agency); brochures and manuals treated as charters (DARPG now directs separate lists); needs of senior citizens and the disabled ignored; poor review and updating; inadequate devolution to street-level staff; technical jargon, no vernacular; same format for every service.

Suggestions

  • DARPG hand-holding for first charters; awareness (credible NGOs); attitudinal change; mandatory involvement of user groups and staff; built-in monitoring, preferably by an outside agency; prioritise services; incentivise at organisation and individual level; showcase best practices; simple language; 5-year master plan with budget.

ARC-II recommendations

  • One size does not fit all (separate charter per unit and service); widest consultation; commit to what you can deliver; reform internal processes; appropriate redressal; periodic evaluation with end-user feedback; hold officers accountable for results (penal provisions).

Sevottam (Seva + Uttam)

  • Conceptualised by the 2nd ARC, launched 2006; piloted in ten departments (2009-10); IS 15700:2005 by the Bureau of Indian Standards certifies the Sevottam symbol.

13 · RTI Act 2005 and the 2019 Amendment#

Handout only · M1 p.31-35
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: 2020. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • Sweden first RTI (1766); USA second (1966); Shourie Group 1997; FOI Bill 2002 not notified; Tamil Nadu first state 1997
  • Penalty Rs 250/day up to Rs 25,000
  • 16 types of information within 120 days; PIOs within 100 days; transfer in 5 days; third party notice 10 days; 30 days to respond
  • Copyright is the only absolute exemption; Act is retrospective; exempt categories released after 20 years
  • CIC (2013) on political parties; BCCI public authority (CIC, 2018); judiciary covered (SC, 2019)

Mains hooks

  • RTI 2019 amendment: autonomy of Information Commissions (negative and positive)
  • Public authority disputes: judiciary, political parties, PPP, BCCI
  • Rights conferred on citizens

Maximum disclosure, minimal exemption: the amendment tests whether independence survives executive control.

RTI is not codified but read into Arts 19(1)(a), 21, 39(b), 39(c). The MKSS movement (Janawad, 'Humara Paisa, Humara Hisab') fed the NAC-drafted 2005 Act: maximum disclosure, minimal exemption, appeals, penalties, proactive disclosure. Section 8 exemptions, public authority disputes and the 2019 amendment are the live questions.

  • Sweden: first RTI in 1766; USA second in 1966. Sweden's ombudsman: 1809; India's central ombudsman: 2013 (per this module).
  • Constitutional locus: not categorically codified; Preamble ideals support it; SC read it into Art 19(1)(a), Art 21, Art 39(b) and 39(c) ('data is the new oil'). Reference cases: Rajnarain 1975; S.P. Gupta 1982. James Madison quote on popular government and popular information.

Why demand grew

  • Spread of democracy; advocacy (World Bank, IMF, Transparency International, NCPRI 1996 led by Aruna Roy, PUCL, ADR). MKSS at Janawad, Rajasthan: slogan 'Humara Paisa, Humara Hisab'.
  • Benefits: Monopoly + Discretion - Transparency = Corruption; corrects power asymmetry; realises other rights (e.g. Food Security entitlements); citizen participation; corrects trust deficit (information within 30 days); better record keeping and decisions; shields civil servants from unwarranted pressure (documentary proof).

RTI in India

  • 1997 working group under H.D. Shourie produced the Freedom of Information Bill, 2002 (enacted, not implemented/notified; seen as feeble). States acted first: Tamil Nadu 1997. The 2005 bill was prepared by the National Advisory Council, not bureaucrats.
  • Criteria for a sound regime: wide access (minimal fees, free for BPL); penalties Rs 250 a day up to Rs 25,000 plus disciplinary action; independent appeal (CIC and SICs); maximum disclosure, minimal exemption.
  • Preamble philosophy: transparency, check corruption, accountability, empowerment, democracy, balanced with genuine confidentiality and efficiency.

Main features

  • All citizens have the right; broad definition of information (records, memos, e-mail, opinions, advice, press releases, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, samples, models, electronic data, private-body information held by a public authority). Right includes inspecting work, notes and certified copies, samples, and electronic formats.
  • Public authority: bodies under the Constitution, Parliament or state law, or by notification; bodies owned, controlled or substantially financed; NGOs substantially financed (term 'substantially' not defined).

Exemptions (Section 8)

  • Sovereignty, security, strategic, scientific, economic interests, foreign relations, incitement; court-forbidden or contempt; breach of legislative privilege; commercial confidence, trade secrets, IP (unless larger public interest); fiduciary relationship; foreign government confidences; endangering safety or identifying sources; impeding investigation; Cabinet papers (decisions, reasons and material to be made public after the matter is complete); personal information unrelated to public activity.
  • Public interest override: access allowed where disclosure outweighs the harm, notwithstanding the Official Secrets Act 1923. Copyright of a person other than the State is the only absolute exemption. Information that cannot be denied to Parliament cannot be denied to citizens.
  • Second Schedule: intelligence and security agencies are outside the Act, but corruption and human rights violation information must be given (human rights only with the Information Commission's approval). CBI and NIA were added by executive order.
  • Act overrides the OSA; courts barred from suits other than appeals under the Act; wholly retrospective; exempted information released after 20 years (except Cabinet decisions, legislative privilege, and sovereignty, security, strategic, scientific, economic, foreign relations and incitement categories).

Rights conferred

  • Choose the medium and the language (English, Hindi, local); help in writing; no reason need be given; immediate notice of transfer; assistance for disabled; fees waived for BPL and if time limits are missed; reasons for rejection, appeal period and appellate authority; complaint to CIC/SIC for no PIO/APIO access, refusal, no response in time, unreasonable fee, incomplete, misleading or false information.

RTI is a means; access is the end, and the gap between them is capacity, will and records.

CIC composition, selection and removal are spelled out, with the 2019 Rules making tenure 3 years or age 65 and salary Rs 2.5 lakh. The weakness is delivery: pendency, penalty gaps, vacancies, awareness and record keeping. 2nd ARC prescribes structure, funding and secrecy reform.

Central Information Commission

  • One Chief Information Commissioner and up to ten Information Commissioners; appointed by the President on a committee of the PM (Chair), Leader of Opposition (LS), a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by PM. Eminent persons from law, science, social service, journalism, mass media, public administration. No office of profit, party link or business.
  • Tenure (RTI Rules 2019): notified by the Centre: 3 years or age 65, whichever is earlier; no reappointment; an IC elevated as CIC cannot exceed 5 years in total.
  • Salary (Rules 2019): delinked from CEC/EC parity. CIC Rs 2,50,000 per month; IC Rs 2,25,000.
  • Removal: proved misbehaviour or incapacity after a Supreme Court inquiry (if referred by the President). Without SC inquiry: insolvency, conviction involving moral turpitude, paid employment, unfitness of body or mind, prejudicial financial interest.

Issues and challenges

  • Gap between RTI as a means and access as an end; bureaucratic mindset; no specific budget (2nd ARC: 1% of flagship funds); absence of political will; limited awareness (APWC study: 12% of women, 26% of men, 13% rural, 33% urban); vague terms; ICs dominated by retired bureaucrats.
  • Penal provisions rarely applied: Satark Nagrik Sangathan: penalty not imposed in 95% of cases where due. No organic CIC-SIC relationship. PIO duties added without incentive; juniors appointed as PIOs; poor record keeping; abuse of Schedule II; IC orders not implemented (no contempt powers).
  • Pendency: an estimated 40-60 lakh RTI requests a year; 2.55 lakh pending in 26 ICs; vacancies; average disposal 1 year 11 months; COVID worsened it. 21 of 29 ICs had not submitted the 2019-20 report; AP and Telangana SICs none since 2017; Odisha since 2015-16. Waning interest of media and activists.

Impact

  • A grievance redressal tool as well; exposed corruption, wasteful expenditure, scams, false narratives, recruitment results and marks; rural access largely through NGOs; became a national movement aiding the Right to Food and Education; more successful for personally relevant information than for controversial public-interest information.

Flow charts#

Why Indian civil services resist change (Section 2)

Aristocratic personnel system: sharp grade divide, narrow age band, general-ability exams, cadre-based
Closed career: conservative, status quoist, rule fetishism, rank over competence
Elitist mindset: obstacles to change
Plus: traditional education, limited mobility, political failure, poverty
Result: slow pace of social change
Fix: lateral entry, specialisation after 10-12 years

An RTI request, step by step (Section 13)

Citizen files a request in English, Hindi or local language; no reason needed; BPL free
Received by the PIO/APIO (designated within 100 days of the Act)
Misdirected request transferred within 5 days; applicant told immediately
Third-party information: notice and hearing, 10 days
Information due in 30 days; fees waived if time limits are missed
Refusal needs reasons, appeal period and appellate authority
Complaint to CIC/SIC; penalty Rs 250 a day up to Rs 25,000

Sevottam: seven steps (Section 12)

Define services and identify clients
Set standards and norms
Develop organisational capability
Perform to achieve standards
Monitor performance
Evaluate impact
Continuous improvement

How corruption is produced and checked (Sections 13, 15, 16)

Monopoly + Discretion - Transparency = Corruption
Add transparency: RTI (information in 30 days), proactive disclosure, NDSAP
Add participation: social audit, Citizen Report Cards, jan sunwai
Add enforcement: CBI, CVC, Lokpal, PCA 1988 (2018)
Add protection: Whistleblower Act 2014
Result: corruption is controlled, not eliminated ('wicked problem')

Who can use which accountability tool (Section 11)

Within the hierarchy: managerial and intra-organisational accountability
Between state institutions: horizontal (CAG, EC, CVC, CIC, ombudsman)
Citizens and civil society: vertical (elections, report cards, social audits)
Citizens engaging institutions directly: diagonal (PIL)
Beyond the state: global standards (TI, HDI, SDGs, Ease of Doing Business)

2024 · 15 marks · The Citizens' charter has been a land mark initiative in ensuring citizen-centric administration. But it is ye…

Question. The Citizens' charter has been a land mark initiative in ensuring citizen-centric administration. But it is yet to reach its full potential. Identify the factors hindering the realisation of its promise and suggest measures to overcome them.

A Charter commits an organisation to quality and time standards for existing rights (UK, 1991); it creates no new rights.

  • Factors: limited consultation (manager's charter), no penal provisions, low awareness, standards too lax or strict, no names of redressal functionaries, no incentives, weak review, no devolution to street-level staff, jargon and no vernacular
  • Measures: separate charter per unit and service, widest consultation, commit only to what can be delivered, redressal mechanism, periodic evaluation with end-user feedback, penal provisions
  • Institutional: outside-agency review, Charter Mark-type incentives, Sevottam (IS 15700:2005), digital link with CPGRAMS

Close. A charter works when promises are realistic, owned by frontline staff and measured by someone outside.

2020 · 150 words marks · "Institutional quality is a crucial driver of economic performance". In this context suggest reforms in Civil …

Question. "Institutional quality is a crucial driver of economic performance". In this context suggest reforms in Civil Service for strengthening democracy.

Quality civil services (a key institution) support performance and democracy.

  • Recruitment: lateral entry, psycho-social profile, probation
  • Training and competency: mid-career training, Mission Karmayogi (rules to roles)
  • Appraisal and tenure: outcome-based, 360-degree, stable tenure
  • Openness: RTI, Citizens' Charters, social audit; Nolan Principles

Close. A competent, neutral and accountable service is the institution that carries democracy to citizens.

2020 · 10 marks · "Recent amendments to the Right to Information Act will have profound impact on the autonomy and independence …

Question. "Recent amendments to the Right to Information Act will have profound impact on the autonomy and independence of the Information Commission". Discuss.

The RTI Amendment Act 2019 moved tenure, salary and service conditions of the CIC and ICs from the Act to executive rules.

  • What changed: Centre decides tenure (five-year term removed); fixes salary and conditions, delinked from CEC and ECs; CIC clarified as statutory
  • Negative: executive overreach, public distrust, federal dilution of SICs
  • Positive: administrative flexibility, fiscal discipline, policy alignment
  • Now: tenure 3 years or age 65; CIC Rs 2.5 lakh, IC Rs 2.25 lakh; removal still needs SC inquiry

Close. The impact turns on how responsibly the government uses its new powers.

2018 · 15 marks · Citizens' Charter is an ideal instrument of organizational transparency and accountability, but it has its own…

Question. Citizens' Charter is an ideal instrument of organizational transparency and accountability, but it has its own limitations. Identify the limitations and suggest measures for greater effectiveness of the Citizens Charter.

A Charter makes standards and timelines public, so performers can be told from non-performers.

  • Limitations: no penal provisions, limited consultation, unrealistic standards, awareness gap, no incentives, brochures taken as charters, poor review
  • Measures: hand-holding by DARPG for first charters, mandatory user and staff involvement, outside monitoring, simplified language, 5-year master plan with budget
  • Sevottam adds three modules (charter, grievance redress, service capability)

Close. Transparency of promise must be matched by consequence for failure.

2017 · 250 words marks · Initially Civil Services in India were designed to achieve the goals of neutrality and effectiveness, which se…

Question. Initially Civil Services in India were designed to achieve the goals of neutrality and effectiveness, which seems to be lacking in the present context. Do you agree with the view that drastic reforms are required in Civil Services? Comment.

Neutrality (class, cultural, political) and effectiveness are both strained.

  • Neutrality: middle-class mindset, political pressure, Ghose's 'weather report readers', politicised transfers
  • Effectiveness: average DC tenure about one year, poor training (one in five trained), no quantifiable appraisal, 'hire, but no fire'
  • Reform: tenure security and Civil Services Boards (T.S.R. Subramanian 2013); comprehensive Civil Services Law; 360-degree appraisal; domainisation; lateral entry
  • Mission Karmayogi: roles-based competency, iGOT, outcome-based appraisal

Close. Reform needs to be continuous and structural, not drastic: protect neutrality, reward results.

2016 · 12.5 marks · Effectiveness of the government system at various levels and people's participation in the governance system a…

Question. Effectiveness of the government system at various levels and people's participation in the governance system are inter-dependent. Discuss their relationship with each other in context of India.

Participation tools turn citizens into monitors of government performance.

  • Social audit, Citizen Report Cards, public hearings, public expenditure tracking improve service quality and deter corruption
  • Charters and RTI give citizens the information to participate; MKSS jan sunwais led to RTI
  • AP SSAAT and Bengaluru report cards show effectiveness gains; participation in Charter design is a 'key element of democracy'
  • Limits: low awareness (RTI), no trained auditors, digital divide

2014 · not printed marks · Has the cadre-based Civil Services organization been the cause of slow change in India? Critically examine.

Question. Has the cadre-based Civil Services organization been the cause of slow change in India? Critically examine.

Cadre-based, closed career services are part of what the handout calls an aristocratic personnel system.

  • Features: sharp grade divide, narrow age band, general-ability exams, class preference, cadre-based services
  • Consequences: conservative, status quoist, non-responsive, rule fetishism, rank over competence, elitist, obstacles to change
  • Other causes listed: traditional education, conservative attitudes to women, limited mobility, failure of the political system, poverty
  • Fixes: lateral entry, specialisation after 10-12 years, domainisation

Close. The cadre system is a major brake on change, but not the only one; reform of services must go with political and social reform.

2013 · 10 marks · Though Citizen's charters have been formulated by many public service delivery organizations, there is no corr…

Question. Though Citizen's charters have been formulated by many public service delivery organizations, there is no corresponding improvement in the level of citizens' satisfaction and quality of services being provided. Analyze.

By end 2011 there were 131 central and 729 state charters, but numbers are not outcomes.

  • Charters without penalties or incentives do not change behaviour
  • Standards set without checking resources and workload lose public confidence
  • Charters not known to citizens; jargon; same format for all services
  • Weak review and updating; grievance functionaries not named
  • Fix: ARC-II 'commit to what you can deliver', reform internal processes, hold officers accountable

Close. Satisfaction follows delivery capacity, not the number of charters issued.

Practice · Evaluate Mission Karmayogi as a shift from rules-based to roles-based civil services management.

Launched in 2020 as the National Programme for Civil Services Capacity Building.

  • Components: iGOT Karmayogi, competency frameworks, outcome-based performance management, SPV Karmayogi Bharat, Capacity Building Commission
  • Gains: competency over tenure, real-time feedback beyond ACRs, on-demand training
  • Challenges: lateral-entrant integration, mindset shift, Centre-State coordination, digital access
  • Link to Aarambh: Commitment, Character, Conduct

Close. Success depends on continuous implementation, not a one-time launch.

Practice · Should civil servants have the right to strike? Discuss with reference to the Essential Defence Services Bill 2021.

The Bill barred strikes in essential defence services including 41 ordnance factories.

  • For: collective protest, protection from exploitation, last resort
  • Against: public interest (loaded gun), model citizens, government as fair employer
  • First ARC (1966): penal offence; SC: no fundamental, statutory or equitable right

Close. Distinguish essential from non-essential services rather than ban or permit across the board.

Practice · Sevottam is more than a certification. Discuss its significance and the challenges in implementation.

2nd ARC concept, launched 2006; BIS standard IS 15700:2005.

  • Three modules and seven steps
  • Significance: structured quality management, citizen-centric culture, continuous improvement, self-assessment
  • Challenges: seen as compliance, fragmented adoption, weak digital integration
  • Way forward: training, link to CPGRAMS and MyGov, third-party audits

Close. Sevottam works when it is a management system, not a badge.
