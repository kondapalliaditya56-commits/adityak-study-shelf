1 · Civil Services: UPSC and SPSC#

Handout only · M1 p.3-4
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • FPSC set up in 1926 (Lee Commission, 1924); Madras 1929 first provincial PSC; Sir Ross Barker first chairman
  • Article numbers 315-323 and what each covers
  • Seniority convention for UPSC Chairman broken in 2015
  • 57 deviations (1950-84) vs 82 (1984-88)

Mains hooks

  • Why the UPSC does not enjoy equal status with EC, CAG and SC (no separate oath, easier removal)
  • Negative recruitment; two 2nd ARC models for compressing the exam cycle
  • From UPSC to a Central Personnel Agency

Independent on paper, advisory in practice: the Commission needs both status and a positive recruitment mission.

In simple words
  • The Public Service Commissions are the Constitution's recruiters: the UPSC for the Centre and an SPSC for each state (Part XIV, Articles 308-323).
  • They began under British rule. The 1919 Act provided for a Federal PSC, which came in 1926 after the Lee Commission (1924). Madras (1929) set up the first provincial commission. The 1935 Act created three kinds: Federal, Provincial and Joint.
  • The Constitution protects their independence: service conditions cannot be cut after appointment, salaries are paid directly from the Consolidated Fund, and removal follows only the route the Constitution lays down.
  • In practice the shield is thinner. Governments set aside the Commission's advice (57 times in 1950-84, then 82 times in 1984-88). The UPSC has no separate oath and an easier removal process than the EC, CAG or Supreme Court. Recruitment is 'negative': it screens people out instead of finding the best fit.
  • The reform direction in the handout: turn the UPSC into a Central Personnel Agency that recruits positively and runs research on civil-service management.

  • no educational qualification fixed for members
  • Unequal status:

    • Removal considered relatively easier
  • conflict with the CVC
  • Exam: concerns over structure and duration of exams
  • Negative recruitment policy:
    • Focus on eliminating unsuitable candidates rather than finding the most appropriate
    • Archaic or obsolete philosophy of recruitment

2 · Civil Services: Meaning and Lateral Entry#

Handout only · M1 p.5-7
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: 2014. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • 1765 term coined; excluded groups (political executives, judiciary, defence, PSU staff)
  • 3 AIS; Third Pay Commission renamed Class 1-4 as Group A-D
  • Lateral entry: 2018 (10 JS posts, 3 yrs extendable to 5), 2021 (31 posts), Aug 2024 (45 posts, cancelled 20 Aug)
  • Type I and Type II error argument

Mains hooks

  • Aristocratic personnel system and its consequences
  • Generalist vs specialist; need for domain expertise after 10-12 years of service
  • Lateral entry: need, issues, the 2024 reservation controversy

Lateral entry fixes a skills gap but must be built on reservation, field exposure and skilled permanent staff.

In simple words
  • 'Civil Services' was a term coined by the East India Company around 1765 to separate combatant from non-combatant staff.
  • Civil servants sit inside the larger circle of public servants. Political executives, the judiciary, defence forces and PSU employees are public servants but not civil servants. Groups A and B are the management cadre; Groups C and D the staff side.
  • The Indian system has been closed and cadre-based: sharp grade divides, a narrow entry age band, exams that test general ability. The handout says this breeds conservative, status-quo, rule-bound officers and slows social change.
  • Lateral entry is the fix on offer: bring private-sector specialists into government. It answers Type I error (hiring the wrong person) and Type II error (rejecting the right one), but raises worries about field experience, reservation and favouritism.
  • The generalist-vs-specialist debate is the same story: holistic coordination on one side, techno-economic complexity on the other. The 2nd ARC wants broad orientation plus domain expertise.

3 · All India Services#

Handout only · M1 p.7-9
Exam lens

Prelims hooks

  • 9 AIS in 1909 -> 4 in 1924 -> 2 in 1947; today 3 (IAS, IPS, IFoS)
  • Article 312; Patel as Father of AIS; Lloyd George coined 'Steel Frame' for the ICS
  • 25 cadres incl. 2 joint: AGMUT and Assam-Meghalaya
  • Entry 70 Union List; 2:1 outsider-insider ratio

Mains hooks

  • Rationale for continuing AIS vs states' concerns
  • Committee views: Rajamannar vs Sarkaria
  • Doctrine of Pleasure; 2nd ARC on Article 311

AIS bind the federation together, but authority and responsibility must be re-balanced between Centre and states.

In simple words
  • All India Services are recruited and trained by the Union but serve under both Centre and States. India has three today: IAS, IPS and IFoS (25 cadres, two of them joint).
  • They shrank over time: 9 (1909), 4 (1924), 2 (1947). Abolishing one is called provincialisation.
  • In the Constituent Assembly some doubted them, but Sardar Patel (Father of the AIS) argued for them as the 'Steel Frame' that holds the country together; Article 312 gave them constitutional status.
  • Supporters say AIS bring the best talent, national unity and Centre-State coordination. States reply that the authority-responsibility link is broken: they have limited control over officers they must work with.
  • The same part of the handout gives the protections of civil servants: Articles 309, 310, 311, the Doctrine of Pleasure, and the T.S.R. Subramanian (2013) call for fixed tenure and Civil Services Boards.

4 · Role of Civil Services in Democracy#

Handout only · M1 p.9-12
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: 2017. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

Prelims hooks

  • Neutrality = class, cultural, political; three forms of confidence
  • Policy neutrality is not political neutrality
  • Bhaskar Ghose's three bureaucrat types
  • Max Weber on bureaucracy as an institution
  • Lakhina Experiment, Ahmednagar (colour-coding of files)

Mains hooks

  • Why neutrality is hard to secure (4Cs, multi-party pressure)
  • Anonymity vs social accountability tools
  • Factors hindering professionalism
  • Case for and against civil service activism

Understand politics, do not play politics: neutrality in loyalty, not in intellect.

In simple words
  • The handout expects six qualities from civil servants: neutrality, commitment, anonymity, instrumentality, professionality, impersonality.
  • Neutrality has three layers: class (officers are mostly middle class), cultural (language, caste, religion, region) and political. Political neutrality rests on three confidences: the people's, the ministers' and the civil servants' own.
  • Political neutrality is not policy neutrality: civil servants advise on policy intellectually, never emotionally. They must understand politics, not play politics. The 4Cs (criminalisation, casteism, communalism, corruption) make this hard.
  • Bhaskar Ghose's three types show the choices: Neither Wilt nor Tilt (firm), Incorrigibly Corrupt (find godfathers) and Weather Report Readers (shift with circumstances).
  • Anonymity means the minister takes credit and blame, but RTI, social audits and Citizens' Charters are loosening it. Activism (media, leaks, courts, whistleblowing, the Lakhina Experiment) is a sign of the times, though the handout says it cannot be encouraged.

1 · Neutrality PYQ 2017

Three layers

  • Class:
    • Civil servants mostly come from the middle class, whose mindset often becomes the dominant mindset of the bureaucracy
    • Elite capture of state institutions (example: National Rifle Association, USA) makes neutrality hard
  • Cultural: neutrality on the basis of language, caste, religion, region and other social identities
  • Political: neutral towards all political parties, expressed through 3 confidences ↓
Confidence ofMeaning
Peoplewhichever party the people elect, civil services serve the elected government with diligence, dedication and commitment
Ministersirrespective of the ideology of the minister's party, civil servants perform duties with commitment

Value addition

Policy vs political neutrality: ministers may lack specialised knowledge of policy and governance, so civil servants advise them on the right course. Political neutrality ≠ policy neutrality: an officer may prefer one policy over another while staying within legality and constitutionality. Involvement in policy = purely intellectual, never emotional. Civil servants must understand politics, not play politics, and must consider the political implications of policies they recommend.

Note

Problems: a thinking person can never be completely neutral · the 4Cs of the political environment (criminalisation, casteism, communalism, corruption) make neutrality difficult · parties may demand loyalty instead of encouraging neutrality · no complete agreement on fundamentals such as the country's vision and direction (extreme right, extreme left, secular parties) · rise of multi-party democracy exposes officers to pressure from different parties at Centre and state.

2-6 · THE Other FIVE

Commitment

  • commitment towards:
    • Impartial implementation of the rule of law
    • Established code of ethics
  • Settled policies
  • Probity
  • Transparency
  • Duty
  • welfare of the Daridra (poorest of the poor)

Anonymity

  • Works behind the scenes
  • Minister = public face
  • Credit for good goes to the minister
  • In a major failure the minister must also accept responsibility: anonymity goes hand in hand with ministerial responsibility
  • it is the duty of ministers to protect civil servants
  • changing with social accountability mechanisms: social audits, RTI, Citizens' Charters, time-bound public service delivery laws

Instrumentality

  • instrument of the people, but a full bureaucracy can become an independent power structure with elitist values
  • Max Weber: once an institution, bureaucracy is among the hardest structures to dismantle

Professionality

  • A professional keeps improving knowledge and skills, stays updated, undertakes training
  • Civil service is a profession
  • hindrances:
    • Generalists in specialist services
    • Poor training (largely Group A and B; only 1 in 5 can expect formal training; mid-career training absent or inadequate)
    • Weak appraisal (no quantifiable targets)
    • Weak incentives and disincentives: "hire, but no fire"
    • Cadre-based dominance of IAS, limited lateral entry

Impersonality

  • don't personalise the post (DC, SP):
    • Impersonality = not personalising the position
    • The job is to discharge its duties faithfully
  • Impersonal attitude to superiors, colleagues and juniors
  • Over-personal ties dull objectivity (example: overlooking a friend's corruption)

Civil Services Activism

Key point

Activism = a civil servant takes the role of a crusader to address problems inside and outside government.

Forms

  • Going to the media
  • Calculated leaks
  • Going to courts
  • whistleblowing = publicly exposing wrongdoing
  • grassroots activism = initiatives that make people's interactions with district administration easier
  • post-retirement: NGOs, civil society organisations, YouTube channels and similar platforms
Way forward / key point

Lakhina Experiment (Dr. Lakhina, IAS, Ahmednagar, 1980s): officials needed older files but finding them in heaps was hard; he colour-coded files by year, making retrieval easy. Example of grassroots activism.

Issues

  • may violate civil service rules and set a bad precedent
  • may personalise institutional issues (e.g. targeting the CM personally over corruption)
  • exposes civil services to manipulation by parties and media
  • can erode credibility of public and democratic institutions
Note

Verdict: activism as such cannot be encouraged, but it is a sign of our times. Civil service and electoral reforms, discussed for decades, must be implemented more effectively to ensure an appropriate political class and civil services.

Exemptions (Sec 8) AND Override

Exempt

  • info prejudicial to sovereignty and integrity, security, strategic, scientific, economic interests of the State, relations with a foreign state, or inciting an offence
  • Expressly forbidden by a court or tribunal
  • Contempt of court
  • breach of privilege of Parliament and state legislatures
  • commercial confidence, trade secrets, IP that would harm a third party's competitive position, unless a competent authority finds larger public interest
  • info held in a fiduciary relationship (same larger-public-interest test)
  • info received in confidence from a foreign government
  • would endanger physical safety or reveal a confidential source for law enforcement or security
  • would impede investigation, apprehension or prosecution
  • Cabinet papers incl. deliberations of CoM, Secretaries, officers
  • But decisions of the CoM, reasons and material become public after the matter is complete (exempt matters stay exempt)
  • personal information with no link to public activity or interest, or an unwarranted invasion of privacy, unless larger public interest justifies it
Value addition

Public-interest override: despite the Official Secrets Act 1923 and the Sec 8(1) exemptions, access may be allowed if the disclosure outweighs the harm to protected interests. Only absolute exemption: copyright held by a person other than the State. Parliament test: what cannot be denied to Parliament cannot be denied to citizens. Second Schedule: Act does not apply to listed intelligence and security agencies, but info on allegations of corruption and human-rights violations must still be given (human-rights info only after the Information Commission's approval); investigating agencies were not listed, but CBI and NIA were added by executive order. Third-party info: notice and hearing first (10 days). Act prevails over the OSA 1923 and any other inconsistent law; no court can entertain a suit about an order under the Act except by appeal under the Act; wholly retrospective (covers info held before the Act too). Exempt info is released after 20 years, except Cabinet decisions, legislature-privilege matters, and info affecting sovereignty and integrity, security, strategic, scientific, economic interests, foreign relations or inciting an offence.

Issues AND Challenges

Gaps

  • means vs end:
    • RTI Act is a means to information
    • There is a gap between the right to information and actual access
  • bureaucratic mindset: RTI is an accountability tool, so bureaucracy resists it
  • no specific budget: no separate funds (2nd ARC: 1% of flagship scheme funds to upgrade RTI infrastructure)
  • absence of political will: bill well drafted and globally appreciated, but hesitancy in bureaucracy and later politicians weakened will to empower it (many exposed scams hurt the govt of the day)
  • limited awareness (APWC study: only 12% of women, 26% of men, 13% rural, 33% urban aware)
  • vague terms and phrases in the law hamper implementation
  • ICs dominated by retired bureaucrats: impartiality affected

Enforcement

  • penal provisions rarely applied:
    • Satark Nagrik Sangathan found no penalty in 95% of cases where one was due
    • Retired bureaucrats allegedly have a soft corner for the govt machinery
  • no organic CIC-SIC relationship: a national collaboration platform is needed
  • PIO duty added without incentive: no separate person hired, an officer with other principal duties gets extra burden, so low motivation
  • juniors appointed PIOs to interpret the law: officers of sufficient seniority needed
  • poor record keeping causes delay (suggested: specialised record-keeping agency to advise and train)
  • abuse of Schedule II: many bodies listed against the spirit of the law
  • IC orders not implemented: ICs have no contempt powers

Pendency

  • An estimated 40-60 lakh RTI requests are filed a year
  • Delay defeats the spirit of the law because RTIs serve contemporary needs
  • 2.55 lakh cases pending in 26 ICs
  • Vacancies in IC posts add delay
  • Average disposal about 1 year 11 months
  • COVID worsened it
  • annual reports (to be laid before the legislature):
    • 21 of 29 ICs did not submit for 2019-20
    • SICs of AP and Telangana have not published since 2017 and Odisha since 2015-16
  • waning interest: media, activists, civil society must keep generating awareness but interest is fading

2024 · 15 marks · The Citizens' charter has been a land mark initiative in ensuring citizen-centric administration. But it is ye…

Question. The Citizens' charter has been a land mark initiative in ensuring citizen-centric administration. But it is yet to reach its full potential. Identify the factors hindering the realisation of its promise and suggest measures to overcome them.

A Charter commits an organisation to quality and time standards for existing rights (UK, 1991); it creates no new rights.

  • Factors: limited consultation (manager's charter), no penal provisions, low awareness, standards too lax or strict, no names of redressal functionaries, no incentives, weak review, no devolution to street-level staff, jargon and no vernacular
  • Measures: separate charter per unit and service, widest consultation, commit only to what can be delivered, redressal mechanism, periodic evaluation with end-user feedback, penal provisions
  • Institutional: outside-agency review, Charter Mark-type incentives, Sevottam (IS 15700:2005), digital link with CPGRAMS

Close. A charter works when promises are realistic, owned by frontline staff and measured by someone outside.

2020 · 150 words marks · "Institutional quality is a crucial driver of economic performance". In this context suggest reforms in Civil …

Question. "Institutional quality is a crucial driver of economic performance". In this context suggest reforms in Civil Service for strengthening democracy.

Quality civil services (a key institution) support performance and democracy.

  • Recruitment: lateral entry, psycho-social profile, probation
  • Training and competency: mid-career training, Mission Karmayogi (rules to roles)
  • Appraisal and tenure: outcome-based, 360-degree, stable tenure
  • Openness: RTI, Citizens' Charters, social audit; Nolan Principles

Close. A competent, neutral and accountable service is the institution that carries democracy to citizens.

2020 · 10 marks · "Recent amendments to the Right to Information Act will have profound impact on the autonomy and independence …

Question. "Recent amendments to the Right to Information Act will have profound impact on the autonomy and independence of the Information Commission". Discuss.

The RTI Amendment Act 2019 moved tenure, salary and service conditions of the CIC and ICs from the Act to executive rules.

  • What changed: Centre decides tenure (five-year term removed); fixes salary and conditions, delinked from CEC and ECs; CIC clarified as statutory
  • Negative: executive overreach, public distrust, federal dilution of SICs
  • Positive: administrative flexibility, fiscal discipline, policy alignment
  • Now: tenure 3 years or age 65; CIC Rs 2.5 lakh, IC Rs 2.25 lakh; removal still needs SC inquiry

Close. The impact turns on how responsibly the government uses its new powers.

2018 · 15 marks · Citizens' Charter is an ideal instrument of organizational transparency and accountability, but it has its own…

Question. Citizens' Charter is an ideal instrument of organizational transparency and accountability, but it has its own limitations. Identify the limitations and suggest measures for greater effectiveness of the Citizens Charter.

A Charter makes standards and timelines public, so performers can be told from non-performers.

  • Limitations: no penal provisions, limited consultation, unrealistic standards, awareness gap, no incentives, brochures taken as charters, poor review
  • Measures: hand-holding by DARPG for first charters, mandatory user and staff involvement, outside monitoring, simplified language, 5-year master plan with budget
  • Sevottam adds three modules (charter, grievance redress, service capability)

Close. Transparency of promise must be matched by consequence for failure.

2017 · 250 words marks · Initially Civil Services in India were designed to achieve the goals of neutrality and effectiveness, which se…

Question. Initially Civil Services in India were designed to achieve the goals of neutrality and effectiveness, which seems to be lacking in the present context. Do you agree with the view that drastic reforms are required in Civil Services? Comment.

Neutrality (class, cultural, political) and effectiveness are both strained.

  • Neutrality: middle-class mindset, political pressure, Ghose's 'weather report readers', politicised transfers
  • Effectiveness: average DC tenure about one year, poor training (one in five trained), no quantifiable appraisal, 'hire, but no fire'
  • Reform: tenure security and Civil Services Boards (T.S.R. Subramanian 2013); comprehensive Civil Services Law; 360-degree appraisal; domainisation; lateral entry
  • Mission Karmayogi: roles-based competency, iGOT, outcome-based appraisal

Close. Reform needs to be continuous and structural, not drastic: protect neutrality, reward results.

2016 · 12.5 marks · Effectiveness of the government system at various levels and people's participation in the governance system a…

Question. Effectiveness of the government system at various levels and people's participation in the governance system are inter-dependent. Discuss their relationship with each other in context of India.

Participation tools turn citizens into monitors of government performance.

  • Social audit, Citizen Report Cards, public hearings, public expenditure tracking improve service quality and deter corruption
  • Charters and RTI give citizens the information to participate; MKSS jan sunwais led to RTI
  • AP SSAAT and Bengaluru report cards show effectiveness gains; participation in Charter design is a 'key element of democracy'
  • Limits: low awareness (RTI), no trained auditors, digital divide

Close. Effective government invites participation, and participation keeps government effective. (Partial fit: notes cover participation tools, not a theory of the relationship.)

2014 · not printed marks · Has the cadre-based Civil Services organization been the cause of slow change in India? Critically examine.

Question. Has the cadre-based Civil Services organization been the cause of slow change in India? Critically examine.

Cadre-based, closed career services are part of what the handout calls an aristocratic personnel system.

  • Features: sharp grade divide, narrow age band, general-ability exams, class preference, cadre-based services
  • Consequences: conservative, status quoist, non-responsive, rule fetishism, rank over competence, elitist, obstacles to change
  • Other causes listed: traditional education, conservative attitudes to women, limited mobility, failure of the political system, poverty
  • Fixes: lateral entry, specialisation after 10-12 years, domainisation

Close. The cadre system is a major brake on change, but not the only one; reform of services must go with political and social reform.

2013 · 10 marks · Though Citizen's charters have been formulated by many public service delivery organizations, there is no corr…

Question. Though Citizen's charters have been formulated by many public service delivery organizations, there is no corresponding improvement in the level of citizens' satisfaction and quality of services being provided. Analyze.

By end 2011 there were 131 central and 729 state charters, but numbers are not outcomes.

  • Charters without penalties or incentives do not change behaviour
  • Standards set without checking resources and workload lose public confidence
  • Charters not known to citizens; jargon; same format for all services
  • Weak review and updating; grievance functionaries not named
  • Fix: ARC-II 'commit to what you can deliver', reform internal processes, hold officers accountable

Close. Satisfaction follows delivery capacity, not the number of charters issued.

Practice · Evaluate Mission Karmayogi as a shift from rules-based to roles-based civil services management.

Launched in 2020 as the National Programme for Civil Services Capacity Building.

  • Components: iGOT Karmayogi, competency frameworks, outcome-based performance management, SPV Karmayogi Bharat, Capacity Building Commission
  • Gains: competency over tenure, real-time feedback beyond ACRs, on-demand training
  • Challenges: lateral-entrant integration, mindset shift, Centre-State coordination, digital access
  • Link to Aarambh: Commitment, Character, Conduct

Close. Success depends on continuous implementation, not a one-time launch.

Practice · Should civil servants have the right to strike? Discuss with reference to the Essential Defence Services Bill 2021.

The Bill barred strikes in essential defence services including 41 ordnance factories.

  • For: collective protest, protection from exploitation, last resort
  • Against: public interest (loaded gun), model citizens, government as fair employer
  • First ARC (1966): penal offence; SC: no fundamental, statutory or equitable right

Close. Distinguish essential from non-essential services rather than ban or permit across the board.

Practice · Sevottam is more than a certification. Discuss its significance and the challenges in implementation.

2nd ARC concept, launched 2006; BIS standard IS 15700:2005.

  • Three modules and seven steps
  • Significance: structured quality management, citizen-centric culture, continuous improvement, self-assessment
  • Challenges: seen as compliance, fragmented adoption, weak digital integration
  • Way forward: training, link to CPGRAMS and MyGov, third-party audits

Close. Sevottam works when it is a management system, not a badge.
