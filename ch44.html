Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • Origin facts: Cripps failure, 8 Aug 1942 Bombay resolution, Gowalia Tank, "Do or Die", Yusuf Meherally coined "Quit India" (Prelims 2005, 2009, 2013, 2021).
  • Gandhi's speech list: servants do not resign, soldiers do not fire on countrymen, princes accept their people's sovereignty (Prelims 2005: only statement 3 is correct).
  • People and places: Aruna Asaf Ali (underground), Usha Mehta (Congress Radio), Ballia (Chittu Pandey), Midnapore (Jatiya Sarkar), Satara (Patri Sarkar) (Prelims 2009, 2011).
  • "Not true" trap: QIM was NOT non-violent; labour participation was limited "in general" (Prelims 2011).
  • Sequence: August Offer 1940, QIM 1942, INA trial late 1945, RIN revolt 1946 (Prelims 1998, 2017).
  • Linlithgow A/R: "most serious revolt after 1857" and the peasant upsurge: both true, R explains A.

Mains hooks

  • Causes of QIM (Cripps failure, war inflation, Burma-Malaya abandonment) and its results (the five-point Sec 6 list).
  • Spontaneous or not: "leaderless at the top, rootless no" (provincial leaders and Gandhi's speech).
  • Parallel governments: character and what they proved about self-rule.
  • Utility of fasts (teacher's FYQ board) and Gandhi's theory vs practice in 1942 (national interest).

Hot chapter: ten prelims stems, a mains question and a teacher board on almost every idea. Learn the speech, the five phases, the three parallel governments and the five results.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: By 1942 the Cripps Mission had failed, war inflation was biting and Britain had run away from Burma and Malaya, so Gandhi drafted a Quit India resolution that the INC passed in Bombay on 8 August 1942. Within hours the top leadership was jailed, so the movement ran leaderless at the top but rooted below: the mass phase was angry and crushed in six weeks, then it went underground and later threw up parallel governments at Ballia, Midnapore and Satara. Gandhi's 1943 fast carried the cause abroad, and by June 1945 the leaders were freed for the Simla Conference. The movement won no instant freedom, but it proved India ungovernable by force and made the British know they could not stay.

  1. The last door shuts. The Cripps Mission (1942) offered only vague post-war promises and nothing on real transfer of power during the war. It showed that Britain meant to rule on its own terms whatever Indians thought. The last hope of a negotiated settlement was gone.
    → But anger was already rising outside the talks.
  2. Prices and fear. War inflation squeezed ordinary people while British policy ignored the rising cost of living. Britain had also abandoned its colonial subjects in Burma and Malaya when Japan invaded, and Indians feared the same fate.
    → So Gandhi asked how a frightened people could resist an invader.
  3. Gandhi's logic. The INC did not want Indians to meet a Japanese invasion in low morale and passive helplessness. MG's idea: a high-spirited, nationalist people would resist far better than a demoralised one. He drafted a resolution demanding Quit India and non-cooperation against Japan if it invaded.
    → Now the party had to authorise him.
  4. Wardha and Bombay. The CWC accepted the idea at Wardha in July 1942. On 8 August 1942 the INC held a special session in Bombay and passed the QIM resolution demanding immediate Purna Swaraj. The same evening at Gowalia Tank (Azad Maidan) Gandhi gave his "Do or Die" speech.
    → But the government struck that same night.
  5. The night of 9 August. The entire top INC leadership was arrested. The detailed resolution could not be made public, so the public knew only what MG said in the speech. Everything not in the speech, such as the land-revenue plan, stayed a private strategy.
    → So the crowds acted on what they understood.
  6. Six angry weeks. The mass struggle was not non-violent: crowds pulled up railway tracks, blew up bridges, cut telegraph wires and attacked stations, police stations, courts and post offices. Provincial leaders led, villagers joined, and peasants were the soul of the movement.
    → But Britain had prepared its weapons.
  7. The harshest repression. Under the Revolutionary Movement Ordinance and Defence of India Rules the press was gagged, aircraft carried machine guns, villages were fined and burnt, and 60,000 were arrested by the end of 1942. The mass phase was crushed in six weeks.
    → Yet the movement refused to die.
  8. Underground. The Congress Socialist Party leaders ran an underground network: Achyut Patwardhan, Lohia, Aruna Asaf Ali, JP and others. Bombs, arms, couriers, leaked raid plans and rich donors kept it alive, and Usha Mehta ran the Congress Radio.
    → Meanwhile Gandhi gave the cause a moral boost from jail.
  9. The fast. Held at Aga Khan Palace, Pune, Gandhi refused to condemn the movement's violence and began a 21-day fast (1943) against British violence. Indian members of the Viceroy's Executive Council resigned and world media took notice. He lost Mahadev Desai (15 Aug 1942) and Kasturba (22 Feb 1944) in the same palace.
    → And in places British authority collapsed outright.
  10. People's governments. Ballia (Aug 1942, Chittu Pandey) was the first. Jatiya Sarkar at Midnapore (Dec 1942 to Sep 1944) had courts, school grants, rice, cyclone relief and the Vidyut Vahini. The Patri Sarkar of Satara (mid-1943 to 1945) was the most successful.
    → Then the war ended and the jails opened.
  11. The end and the verdict. Gandhi was released in May 1944 on health grounds and turned to constructive work. In June 1945 all INC leaders were freed for the Simla Conference, which formally ended QIM. India had proved ungovernable by force; after 1942 the British knew they could not stay.

In simple words#

  • QIM was the first mass movement to include the Princely States, so it was truly all-India. Gandhi's call was Do or Die.
  • It began because Cripps failed, inflation hurt and Burma-Malaya were abandoned. Gandhi wanted a brave people before Japan came.
  • Everyone was arrested on 9 August, so the public knew only the speech: leaderless at the top, but not rootless.
  • The mass phase attacked symbols (tracks, bridges, wires, stations) and was crushed in six weeks; 60,000 were arrested.
  • It then went underground under Congress Socialist Party leaders, and parallel governments ran at Ballia, Midnapore and Satara.
  • CPI and Muslim League stayed out: CPI followed the Soviet line; the League bargained with the British.
  • It ended with the June 1945 release for the Simla Conference. Result: India proved ungovernable, and independence became a matter of when, not whether.

0 · The chapter on one board#

Story link

Because every route to a negotiated freedom had closed by 1942, the INC chose a mass movement in the middle of a world war. Ask: why did Gandhi launch it when Japan was at the door?

Teacher's board
  • Why launched: context of WW II: Britain wanted Indian support; the INC wanted Purna Swaraj, top unity and democracy in the 565 Princely States. Also the August Offer 1940 and Cripps 1942 failure, war miseries, and Britain running away from Malaya and Burma, which raised the fear of losing the progress of the national movement.
  • Goal: immediate Purna Swaraj. Methods: not non-violent; government symbols targeted.
  • Phases: 6 weeks of mass struggle, then repression, then underground activity, and here the parallel governments.
  • Impact: participation (rural and urban); the first movement to reach the 565 Princely States, which brought unity and independence; the Do or Die call.

  • The underground phase (3.4) is like revolutionary activity.

1 · What Was the Quit India Movement?#

Story link

Because the movement was meant to be total, it reached beyond British India. Ask: who joined, who stayed out, and who named it?

  • The QIM (1942-45) holds a special place in the Indian National Movement.
  • It was the first mass movement to include the Princely States, making it truly all-India in character. For the first time the people of those states stood alongside British India demanding freedom.
  • The phrase "Quit India" was coined by Yusuf Meherally, the same man who had earlier coined "Simon Go Back".
Teacher's board
  • First mass movement to include the Princely States: this is the line to remember (the board elsewhere writes it as the first to reach the 565 Princely States).
  • Yusuf Meherally coined both "Quit India" and "Simon Go Back".

2 · Reasons for Launching the QIM#

Story link

Because the war had closed every door, three pressures met in 1942. Ask: which of the three made action urgent rather than merely desirable?

  • Three pressures came together in 1942 to make action unavoidable.
  • First: the Cripps Mission failed (1942).
    • It removed the last hope of a negotiated settlement.
    • It offered vague post-war promises but nothing on real transfer of power during the war.
    • It proved that Britain wanted to keep ruling India on its own terms regardless of Indian opinion.
  • Second: popular anger was building fast.
    • War-related inflation squeezed ordinary people hard, with no relief in sight.
    • The cost of living kept rising while British policy ignored it.
  • Third: Burma and Malaya were abandoned.
    • Britain abandoned its colonies' people in Burma and Malaya when Japan invaded; Indians feared the same fate.
    • The INC did not want Indians to face a Japanese invasion in low morale and passive helplessness.
    • MG's idea: a high-spirited, nationalist people would resist a Japanese invasion far better than a demoralised, colonially exhausted one.
Teacher's board
  • Three pressures: the failure of Cripps removed the last hope of a negotiated settlement; inflation was the second pressure; the INC did not want Indians to face Japan in low morale, so a brave people would resist a Japanese invasion better.

3.1 The INC Quit India Resolution

  • After the Cripps Mission collapsed, MG drafted a resolution demanding Quit India and committing the INC to non-cooperation against Japan if India were invaded.
  • July 1942, Wardha: the Congress Working Committee (CWC) accepted the idea.
  • 8 August 1942, Bombay: the INC held a special session, followed by a massive public meeting at Gowalia Tank (Azad Maidan).
  • The QIM resolution was passed at the session with the demand for immediate Purna Swaraj.
  • At the public meeting the same evening MG gave his historic speech, including "Do or Die" (Karo ya Maro), laying out the plan of action for the public.
  • Night of 9 August 1942: the entire top INC leadership was arrested. The detailed resolution could not be made public, so the public only knew what MG said in his speech. Everything not mentioned in it became effectively the private strategy of the movement.
Teacher's board
  • From the mains perspective: QIM was not truly leaderless, because provincial leaders provided leadership; it was not truly spontaneous, because of MG's speech.
  • Top leaders were arrested; however provincial leaders were active, so as per mains it was not 100% leaderless.
  • The board dates it: 8 Aug 1942 (the date on the Prelims 2005 stem).

What MG told the public in his speech

AudienceInstruction
i) Government servantsDo not resign. Stay in your posts but declare your loyalty to the INC and to the people of India.
ii) SoldiersSupport Britain against the Fascists (the Allied war effort) but do not fire on your own countrymen.
iii) StudentsLeave your studies and join the QIM.
iv) PrincesAccept the sovereignty of your own people and stop supporting the British.
v) People of the Princely StatesDeclare that you are part of India. Support your prince only if he is anti-British.

Teacher's board
  • The menu of what Gandhi could say: two branches, NC (non-cooperation) and CD (civil disobedience).
  • NC: boycott (titles, education, and the L, E, J options) and Swadeshi. CD: no land-revenue campaign and other laws.
  • Why not the others: the L option is ineffective in an emergency; the ministries option is already used, since the ministries resigned in 1939. Civil servants and soldiers are the remaining targets in the speech.
  • Check your notebook for the exact expansion of the letters L, E, J, CS and MS.
Teacher's board
  • Civil servants: if they resign, the British state in India would collapse and Japan would win easily. Imagine Japan removes the British: the British government is gone, the INC must form a government and needs the civil servants. Theory vs practice of MG: theory is a stateless society; practice is that civil servants do not resign. Reason: national interest.
  • Soldiers: if they leave the British Indian army it is an easy victory for Japan. Theory vs practice: theory is non-violence; practice is "keep killing Fascists", i.e. fighting. Reason: national interest.
Teacher's board
  • The QIM resolution has 2 parts: how to oppose the British; how to oppose the Japanese if they come.
  • The teacher asks: is justice not on the side of the British in 1942? More so in 1942 than in 1939. That is why soldiers were told to keep supporting the Allied fight against Fascists.
  • Geopolitics (world affairs): when means rank above ends you get a Morarji Desai, like a PM; Gandhi in 1942 ranked national interest first.

What the public was NOT told: the plan kept private

AudienceInstruction
i) PeasantsDo not pay land revenue.
ii) TenantsPay only a fair rent, and pay it only if your zamindar is anti-British. (First time the INC explicitly raised a no-rent campaign as part of its programme.)
iii) INC's positionLand belongs to its actual tiller.
  • Why it matters: the last line was a significant ideological step. The INC was stating for the first time that its vision of a free India included Ryotwari for all, not Zamindari. Quiet, but important.
Teacher's board
  • First time the INC raised a no-rent campaign as programme: "in INM vs Br", i.e. within the national movement against the British.
  • Why it was kept off the platform: wars need money, and if people stop paying land revenue it is a big negative for the British war against the Fascists, i.e. an easy victory for Japan.
  • Thought: choose the lesser evil in the national interest.

3.2 The Mass Struggle Phase: six weeks from 9 August 1942

  • The mass struggle phase was not non-violent. With the entire leadership under arrest, people acted on their own understanding, raw and angry.
  • The primary target was the symbols of British authority: crowds removed railway tracks, blew up bridges, cut telephone and telegraph wires, attacked railway stations, police stations, courts and post offices.
  • The Tricolour flag was hoisted on trains and public buildings.
Teacher's board
  • Not non-violent: the violence was against non-living things, i.e. British symbols (bombs, tracks, wires), not against people such as zamindars.

Who participated?

GroupRole
i) Common peopleShowed unparalleled heroism and faced the harshest repression yet seen in the entire INM.
ii) LeadershipPassed to provincial and local INC leaders, since the top leadership was behind bars.
iii) VillagersJoined the urban protesters as news of the arrests spread.
iv) PeasantsThe soul of the QIM, especially in eastern UP, Bihar, Midnapore (Bengal) and Satara (Maharashtra). Rich and poor peasants both took part; anger was directed entirely at the British, with no violence against zamindars or moneylenders.
v) ZamindarsBig zamindars stayed neutral and did not help the British either. Smaller zamindars actively participated, especially in UP and Bihar.
vi) WorkersThe CPI (1925) did not participate, yet many worker strikes broke out independently. The Ahmedabad mills strike lasted 3.5 months. Hundreds of individual Communists participated despite the CPI's official stand.
vii) WomenSchool and college girls played a vital role. Aruna Asaf Ali and Sucheta Kriplani were active organisers in the underground phase. Usha Mehta ran the Congress Radio to coordinate underground activities.
viii) StudentsLeft studies, led processions, distributed illegal patrikas (leaflets) and acted as couriers for the emerging underground networks.
ix) MuslimsOverall participation was not high since the ML (1906) opposed QIM. Yet even ML supporters gave shelter to underground activists and did not act as informers. There was a total absence of communal violence.
x) Most active regionEastern UP and Bihar: the INC had done the highest level of constructive work there in recent years, which became organised mass participation.
Teacher's board
  • Rural and urban curve: the poor may not be western-educated, but they have clarity of goal (the British are the enemy) plus ethics, so their anger was not aimed at the zamindar or moneylender.
  • Mass movements usually need an emotive issue and/or good organisation and/or a charismatic leader. With the top organisers jailed, the driver here was passion for Swaraj; INC plus MG had won the hearts of the people. Check your notebook for the middle step.
  • Mind of a Communist: split between communism (do not join QIM) and nationalism (join QIM). That is why the party stayed out while many Communists joined.
  • Handout aside: the handout jokes that Jinnah was wrong, since there was no communal violence.
Handout alert
  • Handout alert (Prelims 2011): the slide wording of option (d) is "did not attract the labour class in general"; the companion prints "in particular". "In general" makes (d) true. The slide records that UPSC marked (b), which the author thinks wrong. Use the teacher's board: (a), because the movement used bombs and sabotage.

3.3 British repression

  • Repression was immediate and severe: the harshest response to any movement in the history of the INM.
  • Legal tools already in place: the Revolutionary Movement Ordinance and the Defence of India Rules (powers of preventive detention).
  • Methods: the entire top INC leadership arrested on 9 August itself; the press gagged; machine guns on aircraft; collective fines on villages; whole villages burnt.
  • Scale: by the end of 1942, 60,000 people had been arrested.
  • Outcome: the mass struggle phase was crushed in six weeks. But by going underground the movement refused to die.
Teacher's board
  • 1940: the Defence of India Rules operated under the DoI Act 1939, an emergency for the period of WW II.
  • Arithmetic: 60,000 arrests in 5 months is 12,000 per month, about 400 per day.

Mirabehn (Madeleine Slade): Gandhi's English disciple

  • Daughter of a British Admiral, Madeleine Slade renounced her life in England to live in Gandhi's Sabarmati ashram from 1925; Gandhi renamed her Mirabehn. She became one of his closest confidants.
  • In QIM (1942): a key campaigner, imprisoned alongside Gandhi and other Congress leaders.
  • After independence: she took the localised Gandhian ideal into rural development, animal husbandry and environmental sustainability in the Himalayan foothills around Rishikesh and later the Kumaon region. She returned to Europe in 1959.

3.4 The Underground Phase

  • When the mass phase was suppressed, the movement shifted underground. Leadership came primarily from the Congress Socialist Party.
  • The network was led by: Achyut Patwardhan, Ram Manohar Lohia, Biju Patnaik, Aruna Asaf Ali, Sucheta Kriplani, Chhotu Bhai Puranik, Ramnath Goenka (founder of the Indian Express) and Jayaprakash Narayan (JP).

What the underground network did

  • Supplied bombs and arms to activists.
  • Pilots and train drivers delivered these supplies: the underground had penetrated critical British infrastructure.
  • Students acted as couriers; villagers refused to give information to the police.
  • Government officials and police officers leaked details of impending raids: a sign of how far British authority had eroded within its own apparatus.
  • Businessmen donated generously. Sumati Morarjee (later one of India's leading women industrialists) gave Achyut Patwardhan a different car every single day so he could not be tracked.

The boost from MG's fast (1943)

  • MG was held at Aga Khan Palace, Pune. The British wanted him to condemn the movement's violence. He refused and began a 21-day fast as a protest against British violence. The British had imprisoned the man but could not imprison the moral argument.
  • Two personal losses: secretary Mahadev Desai died on 15 August 1942 in Aga Khan Palace (MG called it losing his right hand); wife Kasturba Gandhi died on 22 February 1944 in the same palace. Both deaths deepened demands for his release.
  • Impact of the fast:
    • A mass upsurge of demonstrations and strikes across the country.
    • Indian members of the Viceroy's Executive Council resigned, not wanting to be held responsible for MG's death.
    • International media focused on British repression in India.
    • Even those who had opposed QIM now demanded MG's release: the fast brought unity the movement itself had struggled to generate.
Teacher's board
  • FYQ: what was the utility of fasts? (1) Force arbitration (Ahmedabad 1918, Poona Pact 1932), both giving moral pressure. (2) Awareness: the 1932-34 two fasts against untouchability, and international awareness in 1943. (3) Courage to the people (1943). (4) Unity: the 1932-34 fasts, between upper and lower castes. (5) A method of protest. (6) Opposing communal violence (verb unclear, check your notebook).

3.5 The parallel governments

  • The most dramatic consequence of the collapse of British authority in certain areas: people's administrations that replaced British rule locally and actually functioned.

  • The Vidyut Vahini was not just symbolic: it was the enforcement arm of a functioning parallel state, a direct challenge to British sovereignty in the region.
  • Why "Patri": the harsh punishment given to those who opposed the parallel government, i.e. government officials and informers who collaborated with the British.
  • What the Patri Sarkar did:
    • Set up people's courts and village libraries.
    • Enforced prohibition on alcohol.
    • Organised Gandhi Marriages where Dalits were invited and given proper respect: a direct challenge to caste hierarchy.
    • Functioned continuously from mid-1943 to 1945.
  • Handout's amusing detail: Nana Achyut Chavan once attended a Gandhi Marriage organised by the Patri Sarkar and brought a book on Patri justice as a gift, because gifting alcohol was prohibited under the Patri Sarkar's own rules.

4 · Was the QIM Spontaneous?#

Story link

Because the leaders vanished on night one, it looked spontaneous. Ask: if the leaders were gone, what kept the people moving?

  • Yes and no.
  • It appeared spontaneous: the arrests of 9 August meant the public did not know the full plan, so it was leaderless at the top.
  • But not unorganised from below: since the Nagpur Session of 1920 MG had systematically strengthened the INC at the grassroots; the Individual Satyagraha of 1940-41 kept the tempo alive; decades of constructive work trained ordinary people to act.
  • Verdict: leaderless at the top, yes. Rootless, no.
Teacher's board
  • On the board the answer is split two ways: not truly leaderless (provincial leaders gave leadership) and not truly spontaneous (Gandhi's speech). Also: leaderless and spontaneous because the resolution was not published.
  • Prelims 2011: the movement was a spontaneous one of people who were unaware of the QIM resolution, so that observation is true.

5 · How Did the QIM End?#

Story link

Because the war was ending and Japan's threat had faded, the cause for a violent revival weakened. Ask: why did Gandhi choose constructive work after his release?

  • May 1944: MG was released from detention on health grounds. He turned to constructive work.
  • June 1945: with the end of WW II all INC leaders were released to take part in the Simla Conference (June 1945). This formally marked the end of the QIM.
  • Germany surrendered in May 1945; Japan surrendered in September 1945. Britain's position in India was never the same again.
Teacher's board
  • Why constructive work, not resuming QIM? Violence alone was not satisfactory. QIM began out of fear of Japan, and by 1944 that fear had fallen as the war turned (the board marks the turn at 1944 or late 1943; check your notebook).
Teacher's board
  • Sequence boards: AO 1940, QIM 1942-45, INA trial late 1945-46, RIN revolt 1946 (Prelims 1998 answer 1-3-2-4). Second RTC 1931 (1930, 31, 32), QIM Aug 1942, RIN mutiny Feb 1946 (Prelims 2017 answer 3-2-1).

6 · Results and Significance of the QIM#

Story link

Because the movement was crushed yet changed British minds, its result was psychological. Ask: how does a movement that fails on the ground still win?

  • The QIM did not achieve immediate independence, but it produced a psychological paradigm shift, both within India and in the British mind, that made the end of colonial rule inevitable.
  • i) India proved ungovernable by force.
    • Parallel governments functioned for months, underground networks could not be dismantled, ordinary people chose arrest over submission: no repression could hold India indefinitely.
    • The British administrative machinery, stretched thin by war, could no longer sustain the fiction of stable governance.
  • ii) The loyalty of the British administrative machinery broke.
    • Officials leaked raid plans, police turned a blind eye, Indian members of the Viceroy's Executive Council resigned.
    • The steel frame that held British India together for nearly a century was rusting from within.
  • iii) Parallel governments showed Indians could self-govern.
    • Jatiya Sarkar, Patri Sarkar and even brief Ballia proved ordinary Indians could set up courts, distribute relief, enforce law and administer territory.
    • The British argument that India was "not ready" for self-rule lost its credibility.
  • iv) International attention shifted decisively.
    • MG's fast, 60,000 arrests by end of 1942, and aircraft machine guns against civilians got global coverage.
    • The United States, an ally of Britain but uncomfortable with colonialism, began applying pressure; Britain's moral authority was publicly questioned as never before.
  • v) The movement unified forces that had been fragmented.
    • Even earlier opponents, including some who criticised MG for launching it, rallied behind the demand for his release and an end to repression.
    • Post-1942 consensus: independence was no longer a matter of whether, but when.

Teacher's board

Key takeaway
  • Closing verdict: if the CDM (1930-34) made the world notice India's demand, the QIM made the world accept that it could not be denied. The British left in 1947 not out of generosity but because after 1942 they knew they could not stay.
