1 · Freedom OF THE Press · ROLE OF Media · Issues · Trial BY Media#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights handout pp. 29-32. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Press freedom is inferred from Art 19(1)(a). Media is a civic forum and watchdog; Sen's China example shows what censorship costs. Problems: ownership concentration, paid news, conflicts of interest, trial by media (Sahara, postponement), TRP-driven sensationalism.

Constitutional Basis AND ROLE

Basis

  • Press freedom is not named in the Constitution
  • It is inferred from Art 19(1)(a)
  • NCRWC recommended explicit recognition in Art 19

Role of media in a democracy

  • civic forum:
    • A "parliament of citizens"
    • Public discussion, debate, criticism
  • watchdog:
    • Speaks truth to power
    • More press freedom, more accountability
    • Modern democracy is mediated
  • adversarial relationship (slightly) with government is a sign of health
  • protective: voice to the voiceless, helps welfare of weaker sections
  • information: censorship leaves citizens, then government, less informed and weakens feedback
  • value creation: brings attention to disasters and neglected issues (health facilities, malnutrition, road accidents, schools) and shapes what the government treats as important
Memory aid · CWAPIV

Roles: Civic forum · Watchdog · Adversarial · Protective · Information · Value-setting. (own mnemonic)

Note

Amartya Sen's example: in the Great Chinese Famine (1958-61), censorship meant even officials lacked reliable information on the crisis; absence of free information and criticism worsened the disaster. A free press helps governments see and answer suffering.

Issues AND Challenges

Press freedom in India

  • many registered publications and channels suggest a vibrant space, but numbers do not prove freedom
  • World Press Freedom Index (RSF) ranks countries
  • India's ranking raises questions on journalist safety, political pressure, institutional conditions. Rank figures in the lecture are year-specific, not the present rank (check current RSF)

Concentration of ownership

  • Readership and viewership concentrated in a few entities
  • Easier for the powerful to influence
  • owners have other businesses and dealings with government (contracts, approvals) and links with parties
  • Murdochisation:
    • One entity dominating print, TV and radio
    • Reduces diversity
    • Limits on cross-media concentration argued for

Media corruption

  • Paid news:
    • Payment-influenced content presented as news
    • Candidates and outlets collude
    • Small papers vulnerable
    • Raises election-expenditure questions
    • Not a separate offence under the RPA 1951 by that label, though conduct may fall under other laws
  • Conflict of interest: outlets holding equity in businesses they then cover

Trial BY Media

Meaning and rights hit

  • media acts as judge, jury and executioner:
    • A Kangaroo Court (body without legitimate authority acting as a court)
    • Khap Panchayats criticised similarly
  • reputation (Art 21 dignity): guilty before the verdict
  • privacy: excessive reporting of personal details
  • fair trial: pressure on the judiciary, loss of presumption of innocence, interference with administration of justice
Note

Sahara India Real Estate v. SEBI (2012): Doctrine of Postponement. If immediate reporting creates a real and substantial risk to a fair trial, the court may order a temporary postponement, with strict safeguards: necessary and proportionate. Criticism: may deny the right to know, does not curb frivolous media behaviour, can shield influential accused; balance fair trial with public information.

Commercialisation OF Media

Advertisement-driven journalism

  • "Good news is no news": chase for ads and TRPs fuels sensationalism
  • the Chinese Wall between editorial and marketing has been breached (it should stop commercial pressure on editorial decisions)

Sensationalism: five forms

  • exclusive stories chased for TRPs
  • breaking news on unfinished stories: inaccuracy
  • commodification: serious news packaged as entertainment
  • yellow journalism: little researched content, sensational headlines
  • gonzo journalism:
    • Reporter becomes part of the story
    • Deliberately subjective, not necessarily unethical
  • post-truth: emotion and belief outweigh objective facts
Memory aid · EBCYGP

Sensationalism: Exclusives · Breaking news · Commodification · Yellow · Gonzo · Post-truth. (own mnemonic)

Poor quality of journalism

  • No uniform professional licence
  • Course quality varies
  • need better education and training standards without hurting independence

2 · Regulation OF Media · Press Council · FDI · IT Rules, 2021#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights handout pp. 32-37. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Self-regulation is toothless (Katju); government regulation is risky; the ideal is independent external regulation. Know the bodies, the Press Council make-up, FDI limits, the Programme Code, and the IT Rules 2021 (intermediaries, SSMIs, traceability, three-tier grievance, OTT).

NEED AND Bodies

Why independent regulation

  • media is meant to be adversarial, so government regulation can harm media and society
  • self-regulation is the ideal on paper, but Justice Markandey Katju: self-regulation equals no regulation
  • so external regulation independent of government is more prudent
  • Leveson Inquiry (UK, 2012) after the News of the World phone-hacking scandal backed stronger independent press regulation
BodyScope
BCCC (Broadcasting Content Complaints Council)self-regulation of non-news broadcasting content
NBDSA (News Broadcasting and Digital Standards Authority, earlier NBSA)self-regulation of news broadcasting
ASCI (Advertising Standards Council of India)self-regulation of advertising standards
Value addition

Critics call self-regulatory bodies toothless: weak enforcement powers.

Press Council OF India

Composition

  • Statutory body under the Press Council Act, 1978
  • Chairperson + 28 members
  • Chair traditionally a retired SC judge
  • 20 represent the press
  • 5 from Parliament
  • 1 Bar Council of India
  • 1 UGC
  • 1 Sahitya Akademi
Memory aid · 20-5-1-1-1

PCI 28: 20 press + 5 Parliament + one each from Bar Council, UGC, Sahitya Akademi (20+5+3 = 28). (own mnemonic)

Functions and limits

  • two functions: preserve press freedom and maintain standards and ethics of journalism
  • Scope mainly newspapers and news agencies
  • Cannot directly regulate TV and online
  • Limited enforcement, so demands for stronger comprehensive regulation

FDI IN NEWS Media

CategoryFDI limitRoute
Print (news and current affairs)26%Government approval
Digital news media (specified activities)26%Government approval
TV news and current affairs channels49%Government approval

Case for liberalising

  • First Press Commission (1955) feared excessive foreign control
  • but print still has monopolies and concentration
  • more investment could help small newspapers survive, with editorial control kept domestic

Government Suppression OF Media

Pressure routes

  • legislation, executive action, licensing, advertising policy, regulatory pressure

Illustrations

  • Yakub Memon coverage (2015): notices reportedly sent to ABP News, Aaj Tak, NDTV under the Programme Code of the Cable Television Networks Rules, 1994
  • Programme Code bars:
    • Offences to good taste or decency
    • Obscene or defamatory content
    • Falsehoods, innuendoes, half-truths
    • Content inciting violence or public disorder
    • Content affecting the integrity of the judiciary. Criticism: vague words allow arbitrary use
  • denial of government advertising to particular outlets: indirect pressure on press freedom

Digital NEWS AND THE IT Rules, 2021

Why digital news is hard to regulate

  • Internet keeps evolving
  • Fixed classes can force structures
  • convergence: TV channels also online
  • foreign ownership in some platforms
  • borderless: news in the USA is read in India
  • platforms (Google, Facebook) distribute news: publisher vs intermediary vs distributor

Scope

  • IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, under the IT Act, 2000
  • intermediary:
    • Receives, stores or transmits records for another (Facebook, WhatsApp, YouTube, Instagram)
    • Digital news and OTT publishers are not automatically intermediaries
  • reasons:
    • Fake news
    • Hate speech and security threats
    • Abuse
    • Incitement
    • Morphed pictures of women
    • Defamation. Tehseen Poonawalla (2018) (mob lynching guidelines; curb explosive messages) is part of the backdrop

Due diligence by intermediaries

  • inform users of rules, privacy policy, user agreement
  • act on lawful orders to remove or disable unlawful content
  • retain information for prescribed periods (180-day requirements in defined cases, not for every deleted item)
  • grievance redressal mechanism

Significant Social Media Intermediaries (SSMI)

  • threshold originally 50 lakh registered users in India
  • extra duties:
    • Chief Compliance Officer
    • Resident Grievance Officer
    • Nodal Contact Person (24x7 with law enforcement)
    • Monthly compliance report
Memory aid · CRNM

SSMI four: Chief compliance officer · Resident grievance officer · Nodal person · Monthly report. (own mnemonic)

First originator (messaging SSMIs)

  • must enable identification of the first originator on a judicial or authorised government order, for serious offences (sovereignty, security, public order, sexual violence)
  • concerns:
    • Breaks end-to-end encryption
    • Privacy (Art 21) needs legality, necessity, proportionality
    • Security and business interests
TierGrievance mechanism for digital publishers
1. Publisherappoint a Grievance Redressal Officer; resolve within the prescribed period (originally 15 days)
2. Self-regulatory bodyof publishers, if complainant is not satisfied
3. Government oversightCentral Government and an Inter-Departmental Committee; criticised as government-run final oversight

Other provisions

  • emergency blocking directions under the specified process: censorship worries
  • OTT classification:
    • U, U/A 7+, 13+, 16+, A
    • Parental controls and age verification for adult content
  • code of ethics: digital news publishers follow the PCI Norms of Journalistic Conduct and the Programme Code (rules built for old media on new platforms)

Issues WITH THE IT Rules, 2021

Eight issues

  • Safe harbour (Section 79, IT Act):
    • Conditional protection from liability for third-party content
    • Lost if due diligence is not followed
  • Encryption and privacy: traceability may risk mass surveillance
  • Limited consultation across publishers, tech firms, civil society, users
  • Excessive delegated legislation:
    • Rules cannot go beyond the parent Act
    • A fuller statute may be needed
  • Unreasonable restrictions and censorship: test against 19(1)(a) and 19(2)
  • Compliance burden, especially for small digital outlets
  • Legacy media online may face overlapping print, TV and digital rules
Memory aid · SELDUCL

Issues: Safe harbour · Encryption · Limited consultation · Delegated legislation · Unreasonable restriction · Compliance burden · Legacy media overlap. (own mnemonic)

Way forward / key point

Balance: Big Tech shapes public discourse and the State must protect security and order, so regulation within constitutional limits is valid; but it must respect privacy and free speech. The digital space is still evolving.

3 · Right TO Protest#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights handout pp. 37-38. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Protest is derived from Arts 19(1)(a), (b), (c) and linked to Art 21. It is subject to reasonable restrictions. Amit Sahni (Shaheen Bagh) says no indefinite blocking of roads; Himat Lal says no unrestricted right but no blanket ban; Rangarajan needs a proximate link to harm.

Basis AND Limits

Constitutional basis

  • Derived mainly from Art 19(1)(a) (speech) and 19(1)(b) (assembly)
  • Connected to 19(1)(c) (association)
  • protests concern life, livelihood, dignity, so also linked to Art 21
  • Since parent rights are subject to reasonable restrictions, so is protest
  • The task is balancing peaceful protest with public order and others' rights
Memory aid · AAA-21

Protest roots: Art 19(1)(a) speech · 19(1)(b) assembly · 19(1)(c) association, plus Art 21. (own mnemonic)

Landmark Cases

CaseHeld
Himat Lal K. Shah v. Commissioner of Police (1973)assembly right is not an unrestricted right to protest anywhere, any way; State may regulate meetings and processions, but no general or arbitrary ban on peaceful assemblies in all public places
S. Rangarajan (1989)expression cannot be suppressed on illusory, imaginary or remote threats to public order; need a proximate connection with anticipated harm
Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police (2020) (Shaheen Bagh)protest must be balanced with public order and others' rights; no indefinite occupation or blockage of public roads; State may regulate place and manner

Criticism of Amit Sahni

  • Disruption is natural to many protests
  • Society must show reasonable tolerance of inconvenience, else the right becomes hollow
  • But public inconvenience is not an extra ground in Art 19(3)
  • Any restriction must be justified within the Constitution and law
Way forward / key point

Balance: assembly is a Fundamental Right; restrict only within constitutional limits; protect democratic mobilisation and dissent as well as public order.
