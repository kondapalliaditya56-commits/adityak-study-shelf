In simple words#

In simple words
  • Inflation is a general rise in the price level. A rise in one good's price is not inflation by itself.
  • Prices rise when Aggregate Demand (AD) is bigger than Aggregate Supply (AS). Demand-pull pushes AD right; cost-push pushes AS left.
  • We track a fixed basket against a base year (index = 100). Inflation is the % change over the same month of last year.
  • India's headline inflation is CPI-C with a target of 4% ± 2%. The new 2024 = 100 series has 12 divisions, 358 items and a food weight of 36.75%.
  • Core = headline minus food and fuel. Petrol and diesel stay inside core.
  • Borrowers gain, depositors lose, and the poor are hit hardest (Engel's Law). The cure is to make money expensive.

1 · What inflation is, and what it is not#

PYQs for this topic: 1 Prelims · 0 Mains
🧠 Understand it firstPicture a crowd of people. One tall person does not make the crowd tall. Inflation means the whole crowd is growing taller: the overall cost of what people buy keeps rising. A single dearer good (say tomatoes) is not inflation by itself.
✏ ExampleThe handout’s contrast: an agricultural labourer in Odisha and a new joiner in a Bengaluru startup buy very different baskets, so each feels inflation differently. That is why we track one fixed “normative basket” for a group.

Inflation is a general rise in prices or the price level. A rise in the price of one good may cause inflation but is not inflation by itself. People feel inflation when the overall value of the basket they buy goes up. Each person has a different basket, so the experience differs (the handout asks how an agricultural labourer in Odisha feels it versus a new joiner in a Bengaluru startup).

  • Price is the value at which a single good is exchanged, set by that good’s demand and supply.
  • Prices (the price level) are set by Aggregate Demand (AD) and Aggregate Supply (AS).
  • We cannot measure inflation for each person. We track a normative basket reflecting the consumption pattern of a specific group.
📘 From NCERT · XII Macro Ch 1 (Introduction), XII Micro Ch 1 §1.5, XI Ch 2 (price as a signal), XI Glossary
In plain words: The handout says one dearer good is not inflation. NCERT explains why economists talk about “the price level” at all, and how one price differs from it. Read the three small steps; each is two or three lines.
Link to the handout: Handout section 1: “Price = one good; price level = AD and AS”. NCERT’s “price of one good” is the handout’s “price”; NCERT’s “general price level” is the handout’s “price level”.
Step 1 · Prices of different goods tend to move together
  • NCERT (XII Macro Ch 1): “prices of different goods and services generally have a tendency to rise or fall simultaneously”. So macroeconomics can think of one imaginary representative good whose price stands for the general price level.
  • When prices are going up fast “in what is called an inflation”, the direction of movement is usually the same for all the individual commodities as for the economy as a whole.
Step 2 · Micro looks at one price; macro asks “why do prices rise?”
  • XII Micro Ch 1: in microeconomics we study how price and quantity are fixed in one market. In macroeconomics we study aggregate measures: total output, employment and the aggregate price level. One of its questions: “Why do prices rise?”
  • XII Macro Ch 1: for microeconomics, inflation or unemployment were either not mentioned or taken as given. They are not variables that individual buyers or sellers can change.
  • Who can act on them? NCERT: macroeconomic policies are pursued by the State itself or by statutory bodies like the RBI and SEBI, each with public goals set by law, not private profit.
Step 3 · A single price is a signal (XI Ch 2)
  • If a good becomes scarce, its price rises. The rise is a signal that less is available, and gives users an incentive to use it with greater care.
  • NCERT’s example: the price of water goes up because of lower supply, so people stop watering the garden. Likewise a petrol price rise reflects greater scarcity and encourages using less petrol or looking for alternate fuels.
NCERT’s glossary definition (XI Glossary)
  • Inflation: “A sustained rise in the general price level.”
NCERT differs: NCERT’s glossary says a sustained rise in the general price level; the handout says a general rise in prices or the price level. Same idea. Use the handout’s words and add “sustained” if you wish. (handout/teacher wins in class)
⭐ RememberONE price up ≠ inflation. Inflation = the whole basket (price level) up.

2 · AD, AS and the price level#

🧠 Understand it firstAD is everybody’s shopping list added up, in rupees. AS is everything farms and factories can put on the shelves. When the shopping list is longer than the shelves, shops raise prices; when shelves overflow, prices ease. The price level sits where the two meet.
✏ ExampleThink of a school canteen. 100 students want samosas (AD) but the kitchen makes only 60 (AS). The canteen raises the price. Same idea for a whole economy (practice picture, not from the handout).

Prices rise when AD exceeds AS: either AD rises for some reason, or AS falls for some reason.

In plain words: The handout rule is “prices rise when AD exceeds AS”. NCERT says the same thing in its own words (excess demand) and explains why its first model keeps the price level fixed, so you do not get confused when a NCERT diagram shows no price at all.
Link to the handout: Handout: AD > AS ⇒ prices rise. NCERT: excess demand ⇒ rise in prices. “Full employment output” is the output when all factors are fully employed.
Step 1 · Why NCERT first keeps the price level fixed
  • NCERT builds the theory in two steps: (1) find the macroeconomic equilibrium taking the price level as fixed; (2) then let the price level vary.
  • Reason (i): at the first stage the economy has unused resources: machineries, buildings and labours. The law of diminishing returns does not apply, so extra output can be produced without increasing marginal cost, and the price level does not change when the quantity changes. Reason (ii): it is a simplifying assumption that will be changed later.
Step 2 · Equilibrium output against full-employment output
  • NCERT’s caution: equilibrium (Y = AD) by itself does not mean full employment. It only means income will not change if left to itself, even with unemployment.

3 · Causes of inflation#

PYQs for this topic: 5 Prelims · 0 Mains
🧠 Understand it firstThere are only two roads to dearer goods. PULL: buyers have more money or more wish to spend, so the AD curve slides right. PUSH: it becomes costlier or harder to produce, so the AS curve slides left. Cartels, hoarding, black money and shocks from abroad ride on top of these two.
✏ ExamplePull: a salary hike plus cheap loans means more car and phone buying, so prices drift up. Push: war cuts oil supply, fuel is a cost for every producer, so everything gets dearer (the handout stresses oil producers and import dependence).
📐 Formulas of this section
Demand-pull
AD shifts right: AD0 → AD1
price ↑ and output ↑ (Y0 → Y1)
Cost-push
AS shifts left: AS0 → AS1
price ↑ and output ↓

Demand and supply factors of any good split into specific factors (taste for ice cream) and general factors (size of population, income levels, availability of money). Causes of inflation are the general factors that shift AD or AS, not the factors of one good.

ExamplesRising income levels; easy money (lower interest rates, cheaper credit); more government spending; rising population; changing spending habits (less saving, more consumption, usually a secondary effect of higher incomes)Rising cost or non-availability of basic raw materials (crude, metals, coal); higher cost of factors of production (wages, interest on capital); low investment (poor infrastructure, no capital goods, poor R&D); falling productivity or failure to adopt new skills; poor Ease of Doing Business (compliance, contract enforcement, weak entrepreneurship culture); frequent disasters disrupting supply chains

Other factors named in the handout:

  • Legal and market distortions: cartelisation (producers collude to act as a monopoly), hoarding (artificial scarcity), widespread black money (untracked cash).
  • International shocks: geopolitical tension, war, trade sanctions, disasters, especially in countries we import heavily from (oil producers).

4 · Measuring inflation and the idea of an index#

🧠 Understand it firstWe cannot measure inflation for each person, so we fix a typical shopping bag for a group, price it every month, and compare with the same month a year ago. The % change is inflation. Comparing with the same month removes seasonal swings.
✏ ExamplePractice sum (not from the handout): if the same basket cost ₹9,000 in March last year and ₹9,900 now, inflation = (9,900 − 9,000) ÷ 9,000 × 100 = 10%.
📐 Formulas of this section
Inflation (Y-o-Y)
Inflation % = (basket price this month − basket price same month last year) ÷ basket price same month last year × 100
handout: % change in the basket price over the same month of last year

Inflation is the percentage change in prices in the current period over the previous period. It is usually reported year-on-year (Y-o-Y): the current month against the same month of the previous year.

  1. Identify the target group.
  2. Construct a normative basket of goods and services for an average person in that group.
  3. Choose a base year. The basket is built separately for each month to account for seasonality. The importance of each item is its weight (% of total expenditure). Most items stay, some enter and exit, and some weights change.
  4. Collect the prices of the items for each month of the base year to get the basket’s total price.
  5. Collect the prices for the corresponding months of later years to get the new basket price.
  6. Inflation = % change in the basket price in a month of this year over the same month of last year.
  7. Communicate the index value along with the % change over the year.
Seven steps to measure inflation

5 · Indexing: why, and a worked example#

🧠 Understand it firstA raw basket price like ₹11,345 means nothing. So we rescale the base-year basket to a clean 100. Later prices become readable at once: 150 means 50% dearer than the base year, 165 means 65% dearer. The base also gives a fixed yardstick, not a moving one.
✏ ExampleThe handout’s numbers: base March 2015 ₹10,000 (index 100); March 2025 ₹15,000 (index 150); March 2026 ₹16,500 (index 165). Inflation in the last year = (16,500 − 15,000) ÷ 15,000 = 10%. Prices are 65% above 2015.

Hyperinflation, deflation, disinflation

  • Hyperinflation: runaway, exceptionally high inflation (galloping, trotting). Costs: shoe-leather costs (running to the bank before money loses value) and menu costs (constantly updating prices).
  • There is no consensus on the criteria separating high from hyper. Phillip Cagan: more than 50% per month. Examples: Weimar Germany (1923), Zimbabwe (2008). It is commonly understood as the rate beyond people’s normal capacity to bear: the US normal is 1-2%, so 6% looks “hyper” there; India’s normal over two decades is 5-6%, so even 9% is “high”, not “hyper”; Turkey has had 80-100%.
  • Deflation: a fall in prices (negative inflation). Disinflation: a fall in the rate of inflation; prices still rise, but slower, largely a result of base effect. Continued disinflation may become deflation.
  • Japan is the famous modern deflation example: “Lost Decades” since the 1990s; consumers delayed purchases expecting cheaper goods tomorrow.

8 · Spirals, stagflation and the Phillips Curve#

🧠 Understand it firstPrices and wages can chase each other in a loop: dearer goods, higher wage demands, dearer goods again. Expectations matter too: if everyone expects prices to rise, they stock up today and prices do rise. Normally a country picks between inflation and unemployment (the Phillips Curve). Stagflation is the nightmare: both are bad at once.
✏ ExampleWhy stagflation paralyses a policy maker: boosting demand to cut unemployment worsens inflation; cooling demand to cut inflation worsens unemployment. Neither lever helps.
📐 Formulas of this section
Stagflation
Stagflation = stagnation (low growth, high unemployment) + high inflation
both adverse at once
Phillips Curve
inflation ↑ ⇔ unemployment ↓ (inverse)
A.W. Phillips, UK 1861-1957
  • Price-wage spiral: prices rise, workers demand higher wages, firms pass the wage cost to consumers, a continuous positive feedback loop.
  • Inflation expectations: a self-fulfilling prophecy. If people expect prices to rise, panic-buying today creates shortages and prices actually rise.
  • Reflation: the government induces inflation (stimulus cheques, tax cuts, lower interest rates) to boost AD and reduce unemployment after a recession or deflation.
Stagflation

Stagflation = stagnation of output (low growth, high unemployment) + high inflation, together. Merely a contraction is recession; merely high inflation is hyperinflation. Stagflation needs both to be adverse at once.

Normally there is a trade-off: fast growth (low unemployment) comes with high inflation, and slow growth (high unemployment) comes with low inflation. This comes from the Phillips Curve: A.W. Phillips studied the UK from 1861 to 1957 and found an inverse relation between inflation (wage growth) and unemployment. Points where both were low, or both high, were outliers; the both-high points are the periods of stagflation.

  • If inflation is very high, unemployment is probably low, so the policy maker will pay the price of a slight rise in UE to cut inflation. If UE is very high, prices are probably low, so a rise in prices is a bearable cost to cut UE.
  • Stagflation breaks the Phillips Curve: pursuing either goal worsens the other, which creates policy uncertainty and paralysis.
⭐ RememberPhillips Curve = inverse (low UE, high inflation). Stagflation = off the curve: both high.

10 · Is inflation desirable? Engel’s Law and the poor#

🧠 Understand it firstZero or falling prices are not the goal. A little steady inflation keeps firms profitable, so they invest, hire and raise wages. Stability also stops people from putting off buying. But the poor suffer most: they spend most of their income on food, whose prices swing and whose sellers can pass price rises on.
✏ ExampleEngel’s Law: as income rises, the SHARE of income spent on food falls even if the rupee amount rises. A household with ₹10,000 a month that spends ₹7,000 on food feels a food-price jump far more than one that spends a small share on food (practice picture, not from the handout).

As a consumer you might prefer deflation, but a healthy economy needs low and stable inflation. Low inflation within people’s tolerance keeps producers profitable so they invest, expand capacity, pay higher wages and create jobs. Stability brings predictability and stops people delaying decisions.

12 · Controlling inflation: why “make money expensive”#

PYQs for this topic: 1 Prelims · 0 Mains
🧠 Understand it firstWe cannot find the exact cause quickly, and fixing roads, ports and the business climate takes years. So the cure must work whatever the cause: cut AD. AD is spending, and spending needs money. If money becomes expensive (higher interest rate), savers save more and borrowers borrow less, so AD falls and prices cool.
✏ ExampleHandout: a loan of ₹1 lakh at 8%. Raise the rate to 10%: those with money save it for the higher interest, those without cannot afford to borrow. Both cut demand.

  • Two problems: (1) identifying the cause (price is the collective outcome of many intertwined factors); (2) the ability to fix it (you cannot build roads and ports, or attract capital, in a year). Structural fixes (infrastructure, import resilience, EODB) are necessary but come too late. The solution must be effective and agnostic of the cause.

Exam traps#

Each trap ends with a rule for the exam hall.

Prelims trap: Petrol and diesel are inside core

Many assume petrol and diesel sit in 'Fuel and Light'. The handout puts vehicle petrol and diesel under Transport and Communication.

Rule: core = headline minus food and fuel (Fuel and Light) only; petrol and diesel come out only in super core.

Concept trap: Deflation is not disinflation

Deflation is a fall in prices. Disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation; prices still rise, only slower.

Rule: prices fall = deflation; the rate falls = disinflation.

Concept trap: One price is not inflation

A rise in the price of one good may cause inflation but is not inflation by itself.

Rule: pick the option that says 'general rise' and 'price level'.

Prelims trap: Recession: a rule of thumb

Two consecutive quarters of falling output is a rule of thumb. The US NBER uses broader metrics to declare a recession.

Rule: treat 'two quarters' as the thumb rule, not a legal definition.

Handout alert: 18% or 18.8%

The fiscal-drag table gives 18.8% for the rise in post-tax income but the text beneath says 18%.

Rule: quote about 18.8%; the point is that it is less than 20%.

Distinguish between a rise in a price and inflation. What causes inflation?

Intro: Inflation is a general rise in the price level, not a rise in one good’s price.

  • Prices rise when AD exceeds AS.
  • Demand-pull: rising incomes, easy money, government spending, population, spending habits.
  • Cost-push: raw material costs, wages, low investment, poor EODB, disasters.
  • Other: cartels, hoarding, black money, international shocks.

Closing line: The causes to look for are the general factors that shift AD or AS.

How is inflation measured in India? Discuss the recent changes in measurement.

Intro: A normative basket is tracked against a base year and converted to an index.

  • Steps: target group, basket and weights, base year, prices, year-on-year change.
  • CPI (NSO), CPI-AL and CPI-IW (Labour Bureau), WPI (OEA).
  • Headline = CPI-C, target 4% ± 2%, Urjit Patel Committee 2014.
  • 2024 = 100 series: 12 divisions (COICOP 2018), food weight 36.75%, 358 items, e-commerce data, rural house rent.

Closing line: A lower food weight should give a less volatile and clearer signal for monetary policy.

Why does inflation hurt the poor more? Explain with Engel’s Law.

Intro: Engel’s Law says the share of income spent on food falls as income rises.

  • A poor household spends most of its budget on necessities like food.
  • Food prices are volatile and producers can pass them on.
  • No buffer or luxury spending to cut.
  • Deposit returns of the poor are generally negative in real terms.

Closing line: Inflation is a regressive burden.

Explain stagflation and why it creates a policy dilemma.

Intro: Stagflation is low growth, high unemployment and high inflation together.

  • Normally the Phillips Curve gives a trade-off.
  • Policy maker normally pays a small price in one to fix the other.
  • In stagflation both are adverse; fighting one worsens the other.
  • Result is policy uncertainty and paralysis.

Closing line: Stagflation breaks the trade-off that policy relies on.

What is fiscal drag? How does it act as an automatic stabiliser?

Intro: In inflation nominal incomes rise, pushing people into higher tax brackets.

  • Worked example: ₹9 L to ₹10.8 L; tax up 28.2%, post-tax income up 18.8%.
  • Indirect taxes are ad valorem, so collections rise.
  • People feel poorer, cut spending, AD falls.
  • No new tax and no government action needed.

Closing line: The tax system cools inflation automatically.
