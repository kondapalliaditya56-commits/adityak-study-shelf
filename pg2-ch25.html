1 · Opinion Polls · EXIT Polls · Model CODE OF Conduct · Manifestos AND Freebies#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Elections module pp. 23-28. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Opinion polls predict, exit polls estimate after voting; Sections 126, 126A and 126B restrict their display and publication. The Model Code of Conduct is a non-statutory ECI code, in force from the announcement of the schedule, that keeps campaigns peaceful and stops the party in power misusing government machinery. Manifesto promises are not corrupt practices (Balaji 2013), and the freebies debate is about fiscal burden versus welfare.

Opinion Polls AND EXIT Polls

Definitions

  • Opinion Polls: surveys conducted before polling to understand voters' preferences and predict election outcomes.
  • Exit Polls: surveys conducted after voters have cast their votes to estimate election results.
  • Exit polls are generally considered more reliable than opinion polls because they are based on reported voting decisions rather than future voting intentions. However, their accuracy depends on sampling, methodology and voter responses.

Arguments against banning opinion polls

  • 1. Part of Democracy: preparing statistics on public opinion is an important part of democratic politics.
  • 2. Right to Information: people have a right to understand public opinion and the national political mood.
  • 3. Freedom of Expression: a blanket prohibition may raise concerns under Article 19(1)(a).
  • 4. Public Debate: polls can encourage discussion about electoral trends, governance and political issues.

Impact of opinion and exit polls

  • The impact is difficult to determine conclusively. They may influence some voters, particularly those who remain undecided.
  • 1. Bandwagon Effect: some voters may support a party or candidate perceived to be winning because they prefer to be associated with the winning side.
  • 2. Winning Side Effect: supporters of a party predicted to win may become complacent and decide not to vote.
  • 3. Losing Side Effect: supporters of a party predicted to lose may come out in greater numbers to prevent its defeat. Also associated with the Underdog Effect.
  • 4. Impact on Undecided Voters: less likely to change strongly committed voters but may influence undecided voters.
  • 5. Party Cadres: favourable or unfavourable predictions may affect the morale, mobilisation and campaign strategies of party workers.
  • 6. Manipulation of Public Opinion: results may be influenced by the selection of respondents, framing of questions, survey duration and sampling methods.
ProvisionRestriction
Section 126, RPA 1951Prohibits specified election-related activities and the display of election matter through television or similar electronic means during the 48-hour silence period in the concerned polling area.
Section 126A, RPA 1951Restricts conducting exit polls and publishing or disseminating their results during the period notified by the ECI.
Section 126B, RPA 1951Provides liability for offences committed by companies under Section 126A.

Rules in practice

  • Opinion Polls: there is no general statutory prohibition on conducting opinion polls throughout the election campaign. However, displaying opinion poll results through electronic media during the applicable 48-hour silence period is restricted under Section 126.
  • Exit Polls: the restriction ordinarily extends from the commencement of polling in the first phase until 30 minutes after the conclusion of polling in the final phase, as notified by the ECI. It applies to print media, electronic media and other forms of dissemination.
  • Penalty under Section 126A: imprisonment up to two years, a fine or both.

Suggestions for regulation

  • 1. Ensure Fairness: transparent and scientific methods to reduce manipulation.
  • 2. Independent Oversight: bodies such as the Press Council of India (PCI) can strengthen professional standards and accountability.
  • 3. Disclosure of Methodology: sample size, geographical coverage, survey dates, funding sources and margin of error.
  • 4. Restrict Manipulative Surveys: stronger regulation may be considered for misleading or politically sponsored polls.
  • 5. Balance Rights: protect electoral integrity without imposing disproportionate restrictions on Freedom of Speech and Expression.

Model CODE OF Conduct (MCC)

Key point

The MCC is a set of guidelines issued by the ECI to regulate political parties, candidates and governments during elections. Its objective is to ensure free and fair elections, peaceful campaigning and a level playing field.

YearEvolution of MCC
1960The Code originated during the Kerala Legislative Assembly election, with voluntary acceptance by political parties.
1962ECI circulated the Code before the General Election, and it was adopted more widely.
1979ECI expanded the Code, particularly concerning the conduct of the party in power.
1991ECI consolidated the Code and began enforcing it more strictly.
Way forward / key point

Significance: the MCC evolved from a voluntary political understanding into an important mechanism for ensuring fair electoral competition.

Period and area of enforcement

  • The MCC comes into force from the announcement of the election schedule by the ECI and remains applicable until completion of the election process.
  • Lok Sabha General Election: throughout the country. State Assembly General Election: throughout the concerned State. Bye-election: ordinarily the concerned district, with specified exceptions for constituencies in State capitals, metropolitan cities and Municipal Corporations.
  • Important: the MCC begins with the announcement of elections, not the formal election notification issued by the President or Governor.

What does the MCC regulate?

  • 1. Peaceful and Orderly Conduct:
    • Candidates must obtain necessary permissions for rallies, public meetings and processions
    • Campaigning should not unnecessarily obstruct traffic
    • Processions follow prescribed routes and public safety requirements
  • 2. Equal Opportunity:
    • Government guest houses and other public facilities should not be monopolised by the ruling party
    • Access must be fair under applicable rules
    • Government buildings cannot be used as campaign offices
  • 3. Right of Citizens to Peace:
    • No unauthorised wall writing or defacement of public property
    • Campaign vehicles must comply with permission requirements
    • Loudspeakers generally restricted to permitted hours, commonly 6 AM to 10 PM, subject to local rules
    • Noise restrictions around schools, hospitals and other designated areas
  • 4. Decorum in Campaigning:
    • No personal attacks unrelated to public activities
    • No baseless allegations or deliberate distortion of facts
    • No appeals to caste or communal feelings
    • Religious places must not be used for electoral propaganda
  • 5. Prevention of Corrupt Practices and Electoral Offences:
    • The MCC discourages bribery and intimidation of voters
    • Personation and booth capturing
    • Prohibited campaigning near polling stations
    • Transporting voters in violation of election law
    • Other practices prohibited under RPA, 1951

Preventing misuse of government machinery

  • The MCC seeks to prevent the party in power from using its official position to gain electoral advantage.
  • 1. Official Visits: Ministers cannot ordinarily combine official visits with electioneering or use government machinery for campaigns, subject to applicable ECI instructions.
  • 2. New Schemes: governments cannot announce new financial grants, welfare schemes or promises intended to influence voters after the MCC comes into force.
  • 3. Foundation Stones: Ministers and other political executives cannot lay foundation stones for new projects during the applicable period.
  • 4. Ad Hoc Appointments: cannot make ad hoc appointments in public institutions that may influence voters in favour of the ruling party.
  • 5. Official Advertisements: public money cannot be used for partisan advertisements or publicity of government achievements for electoral advantage.
  • 6. Transfer of Election Officials: subject to ECI restrictions and approval requirements.
  • 7. Discretionary Grants: Ministers cannot sanction grants from discretionary funds during the MCC period.
  • Important exception: routine administration, emergency relief and ongoing essential services are not automatically prohibited. They may continue subject to the relevant ECI guidelines and permissions.

Authority and legal status of MCC

  • The MCC is primarily a non-statutory code, developed through political consensus and enforced by the ECI under Article 324.
  • It does not itself prescribe criminal punishment for every violation. Some violations also constitute offences under the RPA 1951, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, or other applicable laws.
  • The ECI can issue notices, advisories, warnings, censures and administrative directions within its legal authority.
  • Important: the ECI cannot impose imprisonment or disqualification merely because a non-statutory MCC provision has been violated, unless an applicable law authorises that consequence.
Key point

Should the MCC be given statutory status?

Arguments in favourArguments against
Stronger deterrence: violations could attract legal penalties.Delayed proceedings: court cases may continue beyond the election.

Committee and institutional views

  • 1. Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990): favoured stronger legal measures against serious MCC violations.
  • 2. Second ARC (2007): recommended statutory backing for specified MCC provisions relating to misuse of official machinery, government positions, discretionary grants and welfare announcements.
  • 3. Parliamentary Standing Committee (2013): supported examining statutory recognition of the MCC.
  • 4. Election Commission: has generally opposed converting the entire MCC into law because of concerns relating to flexibility, quick enforcement and prolonged litigation.
  • Balanced approach: instead of making the entire Code statutory, serious violations involving public funds, government machinery and deliberate electoral manipulation may be given clearer legal consequences.

Election Manifestos AND Freebies

Definitions

  • Election Manifesto: a document presenting the policies, programmes and promises of a political party before elections.
  • Freebies: commonly refers to goods, services, subsidies or cash benefits promised or distributed to influence voters. However, Indian electoral law does not provide a single comprehensive definition distinguishing freebies from welfare measures.

S. Subramaniam Balaji v. Government of Tamil Nadu (2013)

  • The Supreme Court observed that promises of freebies may affect the fairness of elections. However, it held that promises in election manifestos do not, by themselves, constitute corrupt practices under Section 123 of RPA, 1951.
  • The Court directed the ECI to frame guidelines on election manifestos.

ECI guidelines on manifestos

  • 1. Constitutional Principles: consistent with the ideals and principles of the Constitution.
  • 2. Welfare Measures: genuine welfare promises cannot be prohibited merely because they involve public expenditure.
  • 3. Electoral Fairness: parties should avoid promises likely to undermine the purity of elections or unduly influence voters.
  • 4. Financial Feasibility: manifestos should broadly explain the rationale, funding sources and financial requirements of major promises.
  • Important: manifesto promises are generally not legally enforceable merely because they appear in the manifesto.

Arguments in favour of freebies and welfare promises

  • 1. Social Justice: food, health, education and housing may reduce deprivation.
  • 2. Gender Empowerment: financial assistance, transport subsidies and education-related benefits may improve women's opportunities.
  • 3. Inclusion: subsidies may improve access to essential services for disadvantaged communities.
  • 4. Immediate Relief: transfers and subsidies can protect vulnerable households during economic difficulties.
  • 5. Democratic Choice: political parties should be allowed to propose competing welfare models, subject to constitutional and fiscal requirements.

Arguments against excessive freebies

  • 1. Fiscal Burden: unfunded promises may increase State expenditure and debt.
  • 2. Crowding Out Capital Expenditure: excessive recurring expenditure may reduce resources available for infrastructure, education and healthcare.
  • 3. Competitive Populism: parties may compete through increasingly expensive promises rather than long-term development programmes.
  • 4. Electoral Inducement: benefits announced close to elections may blur the distinction between public welfare and vote-buying.
  • 5. Environmental Consequences: free electricity and water subsidies may encourage overuse of scarce resources.
  • 6. Intergenerational Equity: excessive borrowing to fund recurring benefits may shift costs to future generations.
  • Important: not every subsidy or welfare measure is a freebie. The distinction depends on public purpose, targeting, fiscal sustainability and developmental outcomes.

Suggestions on freebies and manifestos

  • 1. Define Freebies: clearer criteria to distinguish legitimate welfare from electoral inducements.
  • 2. Fiscal Transparency: parties should disclose the estimated costs and funding sources of major promises.
  • 3. Independent Scrutiny: strengthen fiscal assessments through existing institutions such as the CAG and legislative committees.
  • 4. Targeted Welfare: prioritise benefits based on need, eligibility and measurable outcomes.
  • 5. Accountability: parties and governments should report the implementation and fiscal impact of major promises.
  • 6. Voter Awareness: citizens should be informed about both the benefits and long-term financial consequences of electoral promises.

2 · History OF Electoral Reforms · Tarkunde · Dinesh Goswami · 1996 Reforms · LAW Commission 170TH AND 255TH Reports#

Exam lens

In simple words
  • Electoral reform moved up the national agenda after the Fourth General Election (1967). The Tarkunde Committee (1974) gave us the voting age of 18 and the multi-member ECI, the Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990) proposed photo ID cards and stronger MCC, the 1996 amendments tightened nominations and polling rules, and the Law Commission reports (170th in 1999, 255th in 2015) pushed party reform, paid news as an offence and time-bound petitions.

History OF Electoral Reforms IN India

Background

  • Electoral reforms acquired an important place on the national agenda after the Fourth General Election (1967).
  • The emergence of regional parties and growing electoral competition strengthened India's multiparty democracy.
  • At the same time, money power, muscle power, electoral malpractices and political fragmentation raised concerns.
  • Several committees and Law Commission reports examined reforms to improve electoral integrity, representation and institutional independence.

Tarkunde Committee (1974)

Associated with Jayaprakash Narayan's electoral reform initiative

  • 1. Three-Member ECI: strengthen the Election Commission by making it a multi-member body.
  • 2. Voting Age: reduce the minimum voting age from 21 to 18 years.
  • 3. Voters' Councils: establish voters' councils at the polling-booth level to ensure fairness.
  • 4. Appointment Committee: appointment of Election Commissioners through a committee including the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition and Chief Justice of India.
  • 5. Electoral Independence: reduce executive influence over the Election Commission.
  • Implementation: the voting age was reduced to 18 through the 61st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988. The ECI subsequently became a three-member body.

Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)

Recommendations on electoral procedures and independence of election administration

  • 1. Voter Identification: introduce Photo Identity Cards to prevent impersonation.
  • 2. Minimum Age for Candidates:
    • Lok Sabha and Legislative Assemblies from 25 to 21 years
    • Rajya Sabha and Legislative Councils from 30 to 25 years
  • 3. Transfer of Officials:
    • Restrict transfers of officials connected with election duties
    • Stronger protection before elections
  • 4. Security Deposits: increase to discourage non-serious candidates.
  • 5. Parliamentary Oversight: establish a Standing Committee of Parliament on Electoral Reforms.
  • 6. Model Code of Conduct: strengthen enforcement and provide statutory consequences for serious violations.
  • 7. Election Administration: improve the institutional independence and professional functioning of election officials.
Value addition

Important: many recommendations remain proposals. In particular, the minimum ages for contesting Parliamentary and Assembly elections have not been reduced.

Major Electoral Reforms OF 1996

Several amendments to RPA, 1951 to improve electoral administration

  • 1. Security Deposits: increased to discourage frivolous candidates.
  • 2. Proposers: candidates not sponsored by recognised political parties were required to obtain 10 proposers.
  • 3. Multiple Constituencies: candidates were prohibited from contesting more than two constituencies in the same general election.
  • 4. Death of a Candidate: the earlier rule requiring countermanding following a candidate's death was modified. Special provisions were retained for the death of a candidate set up by a recognised political party.
  • 5. Armed Persons: going armed to or near a polling station became an electoral offence.
  • 6. Liquor Restrictions: restricting the sale and distribution of liquor during the prescribed polling period.
  • 7. Paid Holiday: employees entitled to a paid holiday on polling day, subject to statutory provisions.
  • 8. Bye-Elections: a general requirement to fill casual vacancies within six months, subject to exceptions.

LAW Commission: 170TH Report (1999)

Electoral reform cannot be effective without reforming political parties

  • Internal Party Democracy: regular internal elections and transparent organisational functioning.
  • Three-Tier Organisation: Executive Committees at the national, State and local levels.
  • Candidate Selection: party leadership should give due consideration to recommendations of local party units.
  • Political Party Regulation: introduce a separate statutory framework for party organisation.
  • Hybrid Electoral System: retain FPTP while adding seats through Proportional Representation.
  • Simultaneous Elections: work towards restoring a common election cycle for Lok Sabha and State Assemblies.

LAW Commission: 255TH Report (2015)

Scope

  • The report examined political funding, party regulation, election expenditure, ECI independence and election petitions.

Political party reforms

  • 1. Internal Democracy: parties must function democratically within their organisations rather than remaining excessively centralised.
  • 2. Transparency: strengthen reporting of party finances, contributions and expenditure.
  • 3. Deregistration: clearer legal mechanisms to deal with inactive, fraudulent and non-compliant political parties.
  • 4. Candidate Accountability: improve disclosures and strengthen consequences for false affidavits.

Electoral reforms

  • 1. Paid News: make paying for and receiving payment for paid news an electoral offence and recognise its effect on election fairness.
  • 2. Opinion Polls: strengthen transparency and regulation.
  • 3. Election Petitions: early disposal through day-to-day hearings and limited adjournments.
  • 4. Independent Candidates: recommended restrictions to address the proliferation of non-serious contestants.
  • 5. Right to Reject and Recall: did not recommend introducing these mechanisms for Parliamentary and Assembly elections.
  • 6. Election Expenditure: strengthen the regulation of political party expenditure and disclosure of campaign financing.

3 · Electronic Voting Machines · Vvpat · Safeguards · Supreme Court Judgment 2024#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Elections module pp. 30-32. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • An EVM has a Ballot Unit, a Control Unit and a VVPAT. It makes counting faster and cuts invalid votes. Trust concerns are about malfunction, transparency and chain of custody. In ADR v. ECI (April 2024) the Supreme Court refused a return to paper ballots or 100% VVPAT counting but added verification rights for the second and third placed candidates.

Components AND Advantages

Components of EVM

  • 1. Ballot Unit (BU): allows the voter to select a candidate.
  • 2. Control Unit (CU): records and stores votes under the control of the polling officer.
  • 3. VVPAT: prints a paper slip showing the candidate's name, serial number and symbol, allowing the voter to verify the choice before the slip is deposited in a sealed compartment.

Advantages of EVMs

  • 1. Faster Counting: reduces the time required to count votes.
  • 2. Fewer Invalid Votes: eliminates invalid votes arising from incorrect marking of paper ballots.
  • 3. Reduced Paper Use: saves paper and facilitates election logistics.
  • 4. Administrative Efficiency: simplifies storage, transportation and counting compared with large quantities of paper ballots.
  • 5. Restrictions on Mass Fraudulent Voting: EVM design limits the speed at which votes can be recorded, making rapid ballot stuffing more difficult.

Trustworthiness OF EVMs

The debate

  • The Election Commission maintains that EVMs are stand-alone, non-networked devices, with technical and administrative safeguards against manipulation.
  • However, concerns about malfunctioning, transparency and verification remain important for public confidence.

Major issues

  • 1. Technical Malfunctioning: EVMs or VVPAT units may develop faults during polling.
  • 2. Transparency of Verification: critics question whether the existing sample of VVPAT verification is sufficient.
  • 3. Chain of Custody: secure storage, transportation, sealing and monitoring are essential to prevent unauthorised access.
  • 4. Public Understanding: limited knowledge about EVM functioning may increase mistrust and misinformation.
  • 5. Handling of Complaints: strict procedures for reporting suspected VVPAT discrepancies may discourage genuine complaints if voters fear penalties.
  • Important: a malfunction is not, by itself, evidence of deliberate manipulation.

Safeguards for EVMs

  • 1. First-Level Checking: machines are checked before deployment, with participation by representatives of political parties.
  • 2. Randomisation: EVMs are allocated through prescribed randomisation procedures to reduce the possibility of targeted manipulation.
  • 3. Mock Polls: trial voting before actual polling to verify functioning.
  • 4. Sealing and Strong Rooms: sealed and stored under prescribed security and surveillance arrangements.
  • 5. Reserve EVMs: additional machines kept available for replacement in case of malfunctioning.
  • 6. Candidate and Party Participation: representatives may observe specified stages of checking, sealing, polling and counting.
  • 7. VVPAT Verification: paper slips provide an additional physical record for verification.

Supreme Court Judgment ON Vvpat (april 2024)

Association for Democratic Reforms v. Election Commission of India (2024)

  • The Supreme Court rejected demands for:
    • A return to paper ballot voting
    • Mandatory 100% VVPAT slip counting
    • Giving VVPAT slips to voters for depositing separately in ballot boxes
  • However, the Court introduced additional safeguards:
  • 1. Symbol Loading Units: must be sealed and stored securely for 45 days after declaration of results.
  • 2. EVM Verification: candidates securing the second or third highest votes may request verification of the burnt memory or microcontroller in 5% of EVMs per Assembly constituency or Assembly segment.
  • 3. Application Period: the request must be made within seven days of declaration of results.
  • 4. Cost: verification expenses are initially borne by the requesting candidate, subject to refund where tampering is detected.
  • Significance: the judgment retained EVM-based voting while providing additional opportunities for technical verification and electoral transparency.

Number card

  • 45 days (store symbol loading units)
  • 5% of EVMs per Assembly segment
  • 7 days to apply
  • VVPAT sample 5 polling stations per segment
  • candidates #2 and #3.

Suggestions TO Improve Trustworthiness OF Elections

Six suggestions

  • 1. Electoral Roll Verification: improve voter-list accuracy through lawful verification, adequate notice and opportunities for correction.
  • 2. VVPAT Transparency: effective implementation of verification safeguards and publish accessible procedural information.
  • 3. EVM Awareness: outreach programmes explaining how EVMs work and how votes are verified.
  • 4. Electoral Funding: reduce the influence of money power, which distorts the level playing field.
  • 5. Media Accountability: strengthen safeguards against paid news and deliberate misinformation.
  • 6. Independent ECI: public confidence depends not only on voting technology but also on the credibility and impartiality of election administration.

4 · Independence OF THE Election Commission OF India · Safeguards · Issues · Recommendations#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Elections module pp. 32-33. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The ECI is a constitutional body (Article 324) with safeguards of status, tenure, service conditions and staff. Its independence is weakened by the executive majority in the 2023 selection committee, unequal removal protection, no charged expenditure, borrowed staff and no power to deregister parties. Reforms include a broad-based selection committee, removal parity, financial autonomy, a permanent secretariat and a cooling-off period. Integrity of individuals still matters most.

Independence OF ECI

Key point

The ECI is a constitutional body under Article 324. Its independence is essential because it conducts elections in which the Union and State Governments themselves may be contestants.

Institutional safeguards

  • 1. Constitutional Status: ECI derives its powers directly from the Constitution.
  • 2. Security of Tenure: the CEC enjoys removal protection similar to that of a Supreme Court Judge.
  • 3. Protection of Service Conditions: the CEC's conditions of service cannot be varied to the CEC's disadvantage after appointment.
  • 4. Election Machinery: under Article 324(6), the President or Governor must provide necessary staff when requested by the Commission.
  • 5. Electoral Authority: Article 324 gives the ECI powers of superintendence, direction and control over elections.

Issues Affecting Independence OF ECI

Eight issues

  • 1. Appointment Process: the 2023 Act gives the executive a majority in the Selection Committee, raising concerns about political influence.
  • 2. Unequal Removal Protection: other Election Commissioners do not enjoy the same removal procedure as the CEC.
  • 3. Lack of Financial Autonomy: ECI's expenditure is not generally treated as charged expenditure on the Consolidated Fund of India.
  • 4. Dependence on Government Staff: relies substantially on officers deputed from the Union and State Governments.
  • 5. Limited Power to Deregister Parties: cannot generally deregister a political party merely for inactivity or non-compliance without statutory authority.
  • 6. Selective Enforcement Allegations: parties have sometimes questioned the consistency and speed of ECI action against electoral violations.
  • 7. Election Scheduling: the timing and phasing of elections have occasionally attracted allegations of political bias. Such allegations require evidence and should not be treated as established misconduct merely because they are raised.
  • 8. Trust Deficit: public confidence may decline when the ECI appears reluctant to take strong action against powerful political actors.
Value addition

Important: the ECI must not only act impartially, but must also be seen to act impartially.

Recommendations TO Strengthen ECI Independence

AreaRecommendation
AppointmentsEstablish a broad-based and independent Selection Committee, reducing executive dominance.
Removal ProtectionExtend to Election Commissioners the same constitutional removal protection as the CEC.
Financial AutonomyMake ECI expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
Independent SecretariatEstablish a permanent secretariat with greater control over staffing.
Post-Retirement RestrictionsConsider a cooling-off period before Election Commissioners accept political appointments.
Party RegulationGive the ECI appropriate statutory powers to deregister fraudulent, inactive or persistently non-compliant parties.
EnforcementEnsure timely and consistent action against serious electoral violations.
TransparencyImprove disclosure of decisions and reasons relating to MCC enforcement and election administration.

Important recommendations of the Law Commission

  • 1. Removal Parity: other Election Commissioners should enjoy the same removal protection as the CEC.
  • 2. Independent Secretariat: reduce the Commission's reliance on executive-controlled staffing.
  • 3. Appointment Process: strengthen the impartiality and transparency of appointments.
  • 4. Party Regulation: improve statutory authority to regulate non-compliant political parties.

ECI's recommendation on financial autonomy

  • The Election Commission has recommended that its expenditure be treated as charged expenditure, rather than being subject to the ordinary annual voting process.
  • Important distinction: the ECI's recommendation concerning charged expenditure should not be incorrectly attributed solely to the Law Commission.

ROLE OF Individual Integrity

Institutional reforms alone may not guarantee impartiality

  • The independence of the Election Commission also depends on the Honesty, Integrity, Boldness and Courage of its members.
  • Election Commissioners must be willing to act against powerful political actors.
  • They must exercise their powers without fear or favour.
  • Their decisions should create public confidence in the fairness and credibility of elections.
Way forward / key point

Central issue: the independence of ECI is not merely a function of legal safeguards. It also depends on the integrity, impartiality and institutional courage of those who exercise constitutional authority.

5 · Electoral Finance · Corporate Donations · Electoral Bonds · State Funding · Electoral Trusts · LC 255TH#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Elections module electoral finance section. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Elections are expensive, so parties depend on big donors. Corporate money can capture policy, shell companies can hide the source, and anonymous electoral bonds kept voters in the dark. In ADR v. Union of India (15 Feb 2024) a five-judge bench struck the Electoral Bonds Scheme down unanimously. The fixes on the table are state funding (in kind first), regulated Electoral Trusts, audited party accounts and digital disclosure.

Corporate Donations AND Electoral Bonds

Problems with corporate donations

  • 1. Economic Concentration: as elections become expensive, parties may become increasingly dependent on large donations from wealthy individuals and corporations.
  • 2. Policy Capture: corporate contributions may create expectations of favourable policies, contracts or regulatory treatment.
  • 3. Conflict of Interest: political parties may favour donors rather than the wider electorate.
  • 4. Shell Companies: companies with limited business activity may be used to route political contributions and obscure the actual source of funds.
  • 5. Shareholder Rights: political donations involve company resources, but shareholders may not have adequate information about the beneficiaries.
  • 6. Unequal Political Voice: large contributions may allow corporate donors to exercise political influence disproportionate to that of ordinary voters.
Value addition

Important: a correlation between corporate contributions and favourable economic policies does not by itself establish an illegal quid pro quo. Such claims require evidence.

Issues WITH Electoral Bonds

Seven issues

  • 1. Lack of Transparency: voters could not determine which corporate house donated to which political party. This prevented scrutiny of possible relationships between political contributions and government policies.
  • 2. Unequal Information: SBI possessed transaction-related information, creating concerns that the Government might gain access to information unavailable to other political parties and citizens.
  • 3. Ruling Party Advantage: parties in power could enjoy advantages from their position in government and access to political resources.
  • 4. Shareholder Accountability: corporate shareholders lacked adequate party-wise disclosure of political donations.
  • 5. Shell Companies: removal of the corporate contribution ceiling increased concerns about the use of companies primarily created to route political donations.
  • 6. Voters in the Dark: citizens could not meaningfully assess the financial interests associated with political parties.
  • 7. Quid Pro Quo: anonymous large contributions increased the risk of political donations being exchanged for favourable policy decisions.

Supreme Court Judgment ON Electoral Bonds (2024)

Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (15 February 2024)

  • A five-judge Constitution Bench unanimously struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme.
  • 1. Right to Information: information concerning political funding is essential for voters to make informed political choices under Article 19(1)(a).
  • 2. Political Equality: large anonymous contributions can give wealthy donors disproportionate influence over political decision-making.
  • 3. Quid Pro Quo: non-disclosure prevents voters from examining possible exchanges of political contributions for government benefits.
  • 4. Corporate Donations: companies and individual citizens cannot be treated identically because corporate contributions may have different implications for political equality.
  • 5. Unlimited Corporate Funding: the removal of the 7.5% ceiling under Section 182 of the Companies Act was arbitrary and violated Article 14.
  • 6. Donor Privacy: the privacy of political affiliation is important, particularly for smaller contributions. However, complete anonymity for large donations failed to balance donor privacy with voters' right to information.

Foreign Contributions TO Political Parties

FCRA, 2010

  • The Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010 restricts political parties from accepting contributions from foreign sources.

Important developments

  • 1. Delhi High Court Judgment (2014): found violations of foreign contribution restrictions involving political donations received by major parties from companies treated as foreign sources under the then-applicable law.
  • 2. Finance Act, 2016: amended the definition of foreign source, providing an exception for certain Indian companies with foreign investment within the permitted limits.
  • 3. Finance Act, 2018: extended the relevant amendment retrospectively to 5 August 1976.

Issues

  • 1. Foreign Influence: parties receiving funds linked to foreign-controlled entities may become vulnerable to external influence.
  • 2. Transparency: complex corporate structures can make it difficult to identify the ultimate source of political contributions.
  • 3. Retrospective Legislation: retrospective amendments raise concerns when they alter the legal consequences of past political donations.
Value addition

Important: the amendments did not create a general permission for political parties to accept foreign donations. Contributions remain subject to the applicable FCRA definition of foreign source and legal restrictions.

State Funding OF Elections

Key point

State funding: instead of depending primarily on private contributions, political parties or candidates receive financial or material assistance from the State for election-related activities.

In favour

  • 1. Level Playing Field: reduces the financial gap between wealthy and resource-poor parties.
  • 2. Checks Corporate Influence: reduces dependence on large corporate donors.
  • 3. Reduces Corruption: may weaken incentives for quid pro quo contributions.
  • 4. Supports Smaller Parties: helps parties with limited private funding participate.
  • 5. Political Equality: encourages fairer political competition.
  • 6. Transparency: public funding can be linked to mandatory disclosure, auditing and compliance.

Against

  • 1. Financial Burden:
    • Elections are already expensive
    • Public funding adds costs for taxpayers
  • 2. Continued Private Funding: unless private contributions are effectively regulated, parties may keep raising money through other sources.
  • 3. Criminalisation: without safeguards, public funds may benefit candidates with serious criminal backgrounds.
  • 4. Weak Intra-Party Democracy: party leaders may control public funds without accountability to members.
  • 5. Reduced Public Mobilisation: may discourage parties from maintaining a social base and raising funds through participation.
  • 6. Disadvantage to New Parties: if restricted to recognised parties, emerging groups remain excluded.
  • 7. Difficulty in Estimating Costs: hard to determine the actual expenditure requirements of parties.
Way forward / key point

Central issue: state funding is useful only if it reduces dependence on private money and illegal financing, rather than merely adding another source of funds.

Electoral Trusts Scheme, 2013

Key point

Electoral Trust: a not-for-profit entity established to collect political contributions and distribute them to eligible political parties. The Scheme provides a regulated channel for donations.

Major features

  • 1. Formation: established under the prescribed legal framework.
  • 2. Contributions: eligible individuals and companies may contribute funds.
  • 3. No Cash Donations: Trusts cannot accept contributions in cash.
  • 4. Distribution: at least 95% of contributions must be distributed to eligible political parties during the prescribed financial year.
  • 5. Transparency: Trusts must maintain records of donors and recipient parties.
  • 6. Reporting: Trusts submit prescribed contribution and distribution reports to the authorities.

Advantages

  • 1. Transparency: more information than anonymous electoral bonds.
  • 2. Regulated Funding: donations routed through a formal institution with accounting obligations.
  • 3. Reduced Direct Transactions: Trusts act as intermediaries between corporations and parties.

Limitations

  • 1. Concentration of Funding: large corporate contributors may still exercise disproportionate influence.
  • 2. Unequal Distribution: Trusts may allocate most contributions to a few major parties.
  • 3. Incomplete Information: a Trust's donor and recipient lists do not necessarily establish which individual donor's money reached a particular party.
In the news (Saarthi IAS CA; verify dates)

ADR data (2024-25): Electoral Trusts distributed ₹3,826.35 crore to political parties; 82.52% of the distributed amount went to the BJP. Electoral Trusts offer a more transparent channel but do not automatically eliminate unequal financial influence. (verify)

Democratic Equality Vouchers

Key point

A proposed mechanism for financing political parties through citizens.

Mechanism

  • 1. Voucher Allocation: every eligible voter receives a voucher of a prescribed monetary value.
  • 2. Voter Choice: citizens can assign their vouchers to the party of their choice.
  • 3. Public Financing: parties receive funds according to the vouchers allocated by citizens.

Advantages

  • Strengthens political equality by giving each voter a comparable opportunity to support a party financially.
  • Reduces dependence on wealthy donors and corporate contributions.
  • Encourages parties to build wider public support.

Challenges

  • Increases the financial burden on the State.
  • Requires a secure administrative system.
  • May be vulnerable to coercion, manipulation or organised voucher collection.
Way forward / key point

Assessment: Democratic Equality Vouchers seek to make political financing more citizen-centred.

LAW Commission: 255TH Report (2015) ON Finance

Major recommendations

  • 1. Expenditure Period: election expenditure should be counted from the date of election notification, rather than nomination.
  • 2. Corporate Donations: stronger shareholder accountability and disclosure.
  • 3. Auditing of Parties: annual accounts audited by qualified Chartered Accountants selected through a framework involving a CAG-maintained panel.
  • 4. Anonymous Contributions: limited to ₹20 crore or 20% of a party's total contributions, whichever is lower. Beyond this limit, details of donors and donations should be disclosed, irrespective of the amount.
  • 5. Public Disclosure: party contribution reports made available through the ECI website.
  • 6. Statutory Reporting: existing ECI transparency requirements should receive clearer legal backing under RPA, 1951.
  • 7. Stronger Penalties: increase the disqualification period under Section 10A from three to five years.
  • 8. Loss of Tax Benefits: parties violating disclosure norms should face penalties beyond the withdrawal of tax exemptions.

WAY Forward FOR Electoral Finance Reform

Eight steps

  • 1. Transparent Political Donations: strengthen disclosure of major donations so voters can identify financial relationships between donors and parties.
  • 2. Corporate Accountability: enforce appropriate limits and require effective shareholder oversight of political contributions.
  • 3. Regulate Party Expenditure:
    • Enforceable reporting requirements
    • Examine whether reasonable limits on overall party campaign expenditure are feasible
  • 4. Electoral Trust Reforms: improve transparency of allocation and reduce opportunities for concealed donor influence.
  • 5. Partial State Funding: carefully designed assistance in kind, subject to audited accounts and internal party democracy.
  • 6. Independent Auditing: strengthen scrutiny of party finances through qualified auditors and effective regulatory oversight.
  • 7. Digital Disclosure: publish contribution reports and expenditure statements in accessible, searchable formats.
  • 8. Curb Black Money: improve coordination between ECI, tax authorities and enforcement agencies to detect unaccounted electoral expenditure.

Aadhaar-voter ID Linkage

Key point

The objective of linking Aadhaar information with electoral records is to improve the accuracy and integrity of electoral rolls.

In favour

  • 1. Removal of Duplicate Entries: identifies persons registered in multiple constituencies.
  • 2. Detection of Impersonation: may strengthen identity verification and discourage fraudulent registration.
  • 3. Electoral Roll Purification: identifies duplicate or incorrect records.
  • 4. Administrative Efficiency: digital verification may reduce manual work.

Against

  • 1. Existing Verification Mechanism:
    • Electoral Registration Officers and Booth Level Officers already verify
    • Strengthen rather than replace
  • 2. Accuracy of Aadhaar:
    • Aadhaar does not establish citizenship or electoral eligibility
    • An Aadhaar number alone cannot establish the right to vote
  • 3. Exclusion Errors: differences in names, ages and addresses may lead to wrongful rejection or deletion of genuine voters.
  • 4. Right to Privacy: linking databases may facilitate profiling by demographic, social or political characteristics.
  • 5. Targeted Political Advertising: linked personal information may enable political profiling and targeted messaging.
  • 6. Surveillance and Data Misuse: unauthorised monitoring or commercial exploitation.

Constitutional test: K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017)

  • The Supreme Court recognised the Right to Privacy under Article 21. Interference must satisfy:
  • Legality: a valid legal basis.
  • Legitimate State Aim: a genuine public purpose.
  • Proportionality: necessary and proportionate to its objective.
  • Procedural Safeguards: adequate protection against arbitrary interference.
Value addition

Present position: submission of Aadhaar is voluntary. A person's name cannot be deleted from the electoral roll merely because Aadhaar information has not been submitted.

Way forward / key point

Way forward: technology cannot replace responsible electoral administration. Regular field verification, accessible correction mechanisms and strong data protection safeguards remain essential.

Special Intensive Revision (SIR) OF Electoral Rolls

Key point

SIR is an electoral roll revision exercise undertaken by the ECI under Article 324 and Section 21(3) of RPA, 1950.

Objectives

  • 1. Electoral Roll Accuracy: identify deceased, duplicate, shifted and otherwise ineligible entries.
  • 2. Inclusion: ensure eligible citizens missing from rolls can register.
  • 3. Electoral Integrity: prevent impersonation and other malpractice arising from inaccurate rolls.

Major concerns

  • 1. Documentation: strict documentary requirements may disproportionately affect migrant workers, poor households, women and marginalised communities.
  • 2. Citizenship Verification: the boundary between determining voter eligibility and broader citizenship inquiries requires careful legal safeguards.
  • 3. Wrongful Deletion: errors may deprive eligible citizens of voting rights.
  • 4. Limited Time: large-scale verification shortly before elections creates administrative pressure.
  • 5. Transparency: citizens need adequate notice, published reasons for exclusion and accessible appeals.

Supreme Court: ADR v. ECI (27 May 2026) (dated, verify)

  • The Supreme Court upheld the legality of Bihar's 2025 SIR exercise.
  • Constitutional and Statutory Authority: ECI can undertake special revision under Article 324 read with Section 21(3) of RPA, 1950.
  • Legitimate Purpose: roll revision serves free and fair elections.
  • Citizenship Inquiry: ECI may undertake a limited inquiry into citizenship for determining registration eligibility.
  • Procedural Safeguards: revision must comply with statutory procedures and principles of fairness.
  • Aadhaar: may serve as proof of identity under the applicable framework, but is not conclusive proof of citizenship.
Way forward / key point

Significance: the judgment recognised the ECI's authority to conduct SIR while retaining the need for due process, transparency and protection against arbitrary exclusion.

Social Media AND Democracy

Overview

  • Parties increasingly use social media for electioneering, voter engagement, mobilisation and narrative building.
  • However, the electoral impact of social media is difficult to measure conclusively because voter choices are influenced by several social, economic and political factors.

Advantages

  • 1. Political Inclusion: new entrants and smaller parties reach voters.
  • 2. Diversity of Viewpoints: alternative opinions beyond traditional media.
  • 3. Direct Engagement: politicians communicate directly with voters.
  • 4. Political Mobilisation: rapid organisation of campaigns, protests and movements.
  • 5. Public Accountability: citizens question leaders and discuss government decisions.

Challenges

  • 1. Decline in Ethical Discourse: combative, personalised attacks.
  • 2. Hate Speech: unregulated spaces, particularly regional-language platforms and private messaging groups, may facilitate communal hatred.
  • 3. Normalisation of Extremism: repeated exposure to inflammatory content deepens divisions and polarisation.
  • 4. Fake News: false narratives, manipulated images and misleading claims.
  • 5. Echo Chambers: algorithms repeatedly show similar viewpoints.
  • 6. Micro-Targeting: personalised messages based on demographic and behavioural data.
  • 7. National Security: online rumours and politically motivated narratives may contribute to communal tensions and instability.

PAID NEWS AND Electoral Integrity

Key point

Paid News: news or analysis appearing in media in return for payment or other consideration, without clearly identifying the content as paid promotional material.

Major problems

  • 1. Misleading Voters: paid content is presented as independent journalism.
  • 2. Unequal Competition: wealthy candidates can purchase favourable coverage.
  • 3. Hidden Expenditure: payments may be omitted from candidate expenditure accounts.
  • 4. Media Credibility: weakens the independence and credibility of the press.
  • 5. Corporate Influence: media ownership and commercial interests may affect political coverage.

Existing measures

  • 1. Media Certification and Monitoring Committees (MCMCs): examine suspected paid news and monitor prescribed election advertisements.
  • 2. Expenditure Monitoring: expenditure on established paid news may be included in the candidate's election expenditure account according to applicable procedures.
  • 3. Press Council of India: has adopted norms concerning journalistic ethics and paid news.

Reform recommendations

  • 1. Electoral Offence: the Law Commission's 255th Report (2015) recommended making paid news a distinct electoral offence.
  • 2. Clear Disclosure: political advertisements should be clearly identified as sponsored content.
  • 3. Digital Transparency: online platforms should disclose sponsors and expenditure associated with political advertisements.
  • 4. Media Independence: strengthen ethical standards and safeguards against excessive political and corporate influence.

WAY Forward FOR Digital Electoral Integrity

Seven steps

  • 1. Multi-Stakeholder Approach: parties, ECI, platforms, media and civil society should develop common standards for ethical campaigning.
  • 2. Political Party Responsibility: internal guidelines against hate speech, disinformation and deceptive digital practices.
  • 3. AI Transparency: clear labelling and disclosure of synthetic political content.
  • 4. Platform Accountability: timely action against unlawful content through transparent procedures.
  • 5. Voter Literacy: strengthen SVEEP (Systematic Voters' Education and Electoral Participation) to help citizens recognise manipulated content.
  • 6. Data Protection: protect personal information from unauthorised political profiling and micro-targeting.

Voting Behaviour IN India

Key point

Voting behaviour: the manner in which individuals exercise their electoral choices. Individual voting is a function of individual preferences and group influences, including religion, caste, social background, economic interests and political affiliations. Determinants are classified as Short-Term and Long-Term influences.

Short-term influences (immediate effect on a particular election; may not determine long-term behaviour)

  • 1. State of the Economy: inflation, unemployment, income and price rise.
  • 2. Local Conditions of Life: water supply, electricity, roads, public transport, sanitation.
  • 3. Personality of Leaders: public standing, credibility and charisma.
  • 4. Specific Political Events: major events preceding elections may alter preferences. Example: the National Emergency (1975-77) significantly influenced the 1977 Lok Sabha election.
  • 5. Media Coverage: coverage, political advertisements and public debates influence which issues voters consider important.
  • 6. Government Performance: recent policy decisions, welfare delivery and public controversies may produce support for or opposition to the ruling party.
Memory aid · LEPSEM

Local life, Economy, Personality of leaders, Specific events, Execution (government performance), Media: the six short-term influences (order is free). (own mnemonic)

Long-term influences (social, economic and political characteristics of voters)

  • SOCIAL FACTORS
  • 1. Age: young and older voters may differ in priorities, participation and party preferences.
  • 2. Gender:
    • Affects political awareness, participation and preferences. Historically women's participation was lower than men's
    • The gender gap in turnout has narrowed considerably
  • 3. Inhabitation: urban voters were traditionally considered more aware and active than rural voters. However, rural voters have become increasingly politically conscious, while urban voter apathy has emerged as an important concern.
  • 4. Caste: caste loyalties remain influential where social networks and local mobilisation are organised around caste. The rise of OBC and Dalit mobilisation, especially after the Mandal period, increased the political importance of caste.
  • 5. Religion:
    • Religious identity may influence preferences through appeals to majority and minority interests, community security and cultural identity. These apprehensions may be real or perceived
    • Their political use can strengthen identity-based mobilisation and, at times, communal polarisation

Economic factors

  • 1. Higher and Middle-Income Groups: influenced by taxation, economic policy, employment opportunities and long-term financial interests.
  • 2. Poorer Sections: immediate concerns of livelihoods, wages, subsidies, welfare delivery and access to public services.
  • 3. Economic Dependence: in some settings, dependence on employers, landlords or local political intermediaries may create pressure on voter preferences.
Value addition

Important: economic status does not mechanically determine voting behaviour. Poorer voters may also make independent and issue-based choices.

Political factors

  • 1. Political Ideology: support for parties whose positions match the voter's preferences.
  • 2. Political Socialisation: family background, education, peer groups and community networks influence attitudes.
  • 3. Party Identification: the psychological attachment of an individual to a political party.
  • 4. Political Leadership: the image and credibility of leaders can influence voting independently of ideology.
  • 5. Government Performance: governance, corruption, welfare delivery and public service outcomes.

Emerging Trends IN Voting Behaviour

Six trends

  • 1. Leader-Centric Voting: electors often vote for parties or leaders rather than individual candidates. Parties become increasingly dependent on the image of their leadership rather than programmes or ideology.
  • 2. Poor Performance of Independents: independents generally have limited success. Example: in the 2024 Lok Sabha election 3,921 independents contested, but only seven were elected.
  • 3. Vote Against Rather than Vote For: voters sometimes support an alternative party primarily to remove the incumbent Government, not because of strong support for the alternative. Associated with Anti-Incumbency.
  • 4. National and State Choices: voters increasingly distinguish national from State-level issues, and may support different parties at different levels.
  • 5. Welfare-Based Voting: direct benefit transfers, public service delivery and welfare programmes shape preferences.
  • 6. Personality and Identity Politics: leadership charisma, caste, religion and regional identity continue to influence outcomes.
Way forward / key point

Central argument: Indian voting behaviour reflects a combination of identity, ideology, economic interests, leadership and government performance. No single factor explains all electoral outcomes.

Urban Voter Apathy

Key point

Urban voter apathy: relatively low electoral participation in many urban constituencies despite higher education, income and access to information.

Major causes

  • 1. Weak Local Identification: urban voters may feel less connected to representatives and constituency-level party organisations.
  • 2. Political Distrust: perceptions of corruption and lack of meaningful alternatives.
  • 3. Migration: many migrants remain registered in native constituencies and cannot easily travel to vote.
  • 4. Occupational Constraints: work schedules, mobility difficulties and long queues.
  • 5. Weak Party Mobilisation: less extensive grassroots networks among some urban groups.
  • 6. Individualism: urban lifestyles and weaker community-based mobilisation reduce collective participation.

Way forward

  • 1. SVEEP: targeted voter awareness campaigns in urban areas.
  • 2. Migrant Voting: examine secure and accessible voting arrangements for domestic migrants.
  • 3. Electoral Registration: simplify registration and correction procedures for mobile populations.
  • 4. Workplace Awareness: implement the paid polling holiday under Section 135B of RPA, 1951.
  • 5. Civic Education: electoral literacy in schools, colleges and workplaces.

Paradoxes IN Indian Democracy

Key point

Indian democracy has achieved significant success in universal adult suffrage, regular elections and peaceful transfers of power. However, several contradictions remain between democratic institutions and democratic practices.

1. Electoral participation vs money and muscle power

  • Participation has increased substantially since Independence, with repeated peaceful transfers of power.
  • On the other hand, money power, muscle power and criminal networks continue to influence elections.
  • Example: ADR reported that 46% of MPs elected to the 18th Lok Sabha declared criminal cases. (verify)

2. Democratic bureaucracy vs repressive State

  • The bureaucracy functions under democratic institutions and political control.
  • On the other hand, excessive use of coercive laws, administrative arbitrariness and restrictions on civil liberties raise concerns about accountability of State institutions.
  • Central issue: democratic control over administration does not automatically guarantee citizen-centric and rights-based governance.

3. Issue-based politics vs identity politics

  • Democratic politics should ideally focus on development, employment, education, healthcare and governance.
  • However, competition frequently revolves around caste, religion, language, region and community identities.
  • Problem: excessive emphasis on identities may make them rigid and reduce opportunities for negotiation, reconciliation and issue-based cooperation.
  • However: identity politics can also enable marginalised groups to demand recognition, dignity and political representation.

4. Democracy vs abject poverty

  • Poor citizens have the right to remove governments that fail to address their problems, yet poverty, inequality and deprivation have persisted alongside regular elections.
  • 1. Political Equality: universal adult suffrage guarantees one vote to every eligible citizen.
  • 2. Economic Inequality: unequal access to wealth, education and opportunities limits practical capacity to influence decisions.
  • 3. Social Justice: reservation and other affirmative action seek to correct historical inequalities.
  • 4. Limited Material Transformation: greater political representation does not automatically eliminate poverty or produce equal economic opportunities.
  • Central argument: Indian democracy has been deeply concerned with transforming the pre-existing imbalance of social power, but gains in political representation have not always resulted in corresponding material improvements for poorer communities.

5. Multiparty system vs absence of real choice

  • India has a large number of parties representing different social and regional interests. However, several parties may adopt broadly similar positions on major economic policies.
  • Paradox: there may be many parties but limited differences between the policy choices offered to voters.

6. Democratic participation vs weak liberal ethic

  • Indian democracy formally recognises tolerance, equality and constitutional rights. However, practices such as mob lynching, vigilantism, hate speech and intolerance of dissent may continue.
  • Central issue: tolerance cannot be sustained merely through constitutional declarations without a strong liberal ethic, fraternity and respect for individual rights.

7. Multiparty democracy vs personality cult

  • India has a competitive multiparty system, but parties may become excessively dependent on individual leaders or political families.
  • Consequences:
    • Weakens intra-party democracy
    • Encourages centralisation of power
    • Reduces ideological and policy debate
    • Makes parties dependent on the popularity of individual leaders

8. Electoral democracy vs psephocracy

  • Psephocracy: a situation in which democracy becomes excessively centred on elections and electoral victories rather than continuous democratic accountability.
  • Democratic practices are observed during elections and transfers of power, but transparency, consultation and accountability may weaken between two elections. This may create conditions for what critics describe as electoral authoritarianism.
  • Central argument: democracy must function continuously through institutional accountability, civil liberties and citizen participation, not merely through periodic elections.

Contradictions IN THE Indian Party System

Eight contradictions

  • 1. Personality Cult: leader-centric politics has re-emerged strongly at national and regional levels.
  • 2. High Command Culture: candidate selection and major policy decisions remain concentrated in central leadership.
  • 3. Rise of Regional Parties: have increased representation of local identities and State-level concerns.
  • 4. Multiparty System but Limited Choice: parties may differ in identity and leadership while offering similar economic policies.
  • 5. Independent Candidates: a large number contest, although few win.
  • 6. Fluid Political Ideology: leaders may change party affiliations or ideological positions for electoral advantage.
  • 7. Representation vs Consensus: the multiparty system has increased representation of diverse interests, but consensus-building may become more difficult.
  • 8. Weak Internal Accountability: parties demand democratic accountability from governments but may not follow equally democratic procedures internally.

Intra-party Democracy

Key point

Intra-party democracy: democratic participation, transparency and accountability within political parties. It includes regular organisational elections, decentralised decision-making, transparent candidate selection and opportunities for internal debate.

Major issues

  • 1. Dynastic Politics: leadership may remain concentrated within influential political families.
  • 2. High Command Culture: leadership controls nominations, policies and organisational decisions.
  • 3. Weak Internal Elections: irregular or lacking genuine competition.
  • 4. Limited Role of Local Units: local workers may have little influence over candidate selection.
  • 5. Personality Cult: programmes become subordinate to individual leaders.
  • 6. Weak Legal Regulation: Section 29A provides for registration but does not establish a comprehensive enforceable framework for internal party democracy.

Way forward

  • 1. Statutory Framework: implement the broad direction of the Law Commission's 170th Report (1999) on organisation and functioning of parties.
  • 2. Regular Elections: genuine and transparent elections for internal party offices.
  • 3. Candidate Selection: increase the role of local party units and ordinary members.
  • 4. Financial Transparency: audited accounts and disclosure of major donations.
  • 5. ECI Powers: appropriate statutory authority to act against fraudulent or persistently non-compliant parties.
  • 6. Internal Debate: democratic consultation rather than excessive dependence on central leadership.
Way forward / key point

Central issue: parties cannot fully strengthen democratic governance unless they also practise democracy within their own organisations.

Political Parties AND Right TO Information

Key point

In 2013 the Central Information Commission (CIC) held that six major National Parties were public authorities under the RTI Act, 2005, relying on factors such as their public functions and indirect government support.

For bringing parties under RTI

  • 1. Public Function: parties play a central role in representative democracy.
  • 2. Transparency: citizens can scrutinise political financing and party decisions.
  • 3. Public Support: parties benefit from facilities such as public broadcasting and prescribed government concessions.
  • 4. Political Accountability: disclosure may reduce corruption and opaque funding.

Against

  • 1. Organisational Autonomy: parties are voluntary political associations, not ordinary government departments.
  • 2. Strategic Confidentiality: disclosure of internal deliberations may affect legitimate organisational activities.
  • 3. Administrative Burden: full compliance may create difficulties, especially for small parties.
  • 4. Political Misuse: RTI requests could be used to disrupt rival organisations.
Value addition

Present position: parties have not generally complied with the CIC's 2013 ruling, and the question of their effective coverage under RTI remains unresolved in practice.
