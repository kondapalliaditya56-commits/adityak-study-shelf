FYQ 1

The Montagu Statement of August 1917 declared that British policy in India would henceforth aim at which of the following?

Answer: (A)

(A)

(A) ✓ — The August Declaration of 1917, issued by Edwin Montagu as Secretary of State for India, set the goal as the "gradual development of self-governing institutions" with a view to the progressive realisation of responsible government in India within the British Empire. The key words 'gradual' and 'progressive' kept the timeline in British hands; GOI 1919 was conceived as the 1st step toward this goal.

Why others wrong: (b) Dominion Status was not part of the 1917 Statement; the promise was only "progressive realisation" within the Empire, not Dominion Status. (c) The Statement set no time-bound commitment for responsible government at the Centre; the timeline was firmly British-controlled, and GOI 1919 itself did NOT make the Centre responsible. (d) Universal adult franchise was never promised in 1917; the Lucknow Pact 1916 demand for broad franchise was not conceded even in the 1919 Act.

Memory hook: Montagu's two magic words — 'gradual' and 'progressive' — meant: yes Swaraj, but on British clock.

FYQ 2

The Montagu-Chelmsford Report (1918) was authored jointly by Edwin Montagu and:

Answer: (B)

(B)

(B) ✓ — Secretary of State Edwin Montagu personally visited India in 1917-18 — an unusual step for someone in his position — and toured various provinces. The joint report he co-authored with Viceroy Chelmsford16-21 became the foundational document for the Government of India Act, 1919, which is why the Act is also called the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms or Montford Reforms.

Why others wrong: (a) Hardinge's viceroyalty ended before the Report; he was not its co-author. (c) Reading succeeded Chelmsford and his tenure began in 1921 — after the Report (1918) and the Act (1919). (d) Irwin came still later in the late 1920s; he had no role in drafting the 1918 Report.

Memory hook: Mont-ford = Mont-agu + Chelms-ford — Report 1918, Act 1919.

FYQ 3

Which of the following was introduced for the first time at the Centre by the Government of India Act, 1919?

Answer: (C)

(C)

(C) ✓ — The GOI Act 1919 introduced a Bicameral Legislature at the Centre for British India for the first time, consisting of the Central Legislative Assembly (CLA, ~145 members, lower house) and the Council of States (CoS, ~60 members, upper house). It also introduced an elected majority and direct elections in CLA, CoS, and Provincial Legislative Assemblies for the 1st time.

Why others wrong: (a) Non-official majority at the Centre was NOT new in 1919 — under ICA1909 a non-official majority already existed in Provincial Legislative Councils (though not at the Centre); the genuine first in 1919 was an ELECTED majority at the Centre, which is not what option (a) states. (b) GOI 1919 introduced DIRECT, not indirect, elections in CLA, CoS, and PLAs for the 1st time. (d) Right to vote remained linked to strict income/property criteria as under ICA1909; no universal adult franchise was granted, and certainly not for the CoS, which actually carried HIGHER income criteria than the lower house.

Memory hook: 1919 = first time British India sees two Houses at the Centre — CLA (lower) + CoS (upper).

FYQ 4

Under the Government of India Act, 1919, the Narendra Mandal (Chamber of Princes) was:

Answer: (D)

(D)

(D) ✓ — The Narendra Mandal — Chamber of Princes — was a key recommendation of the Montagu-Chelmsford Report (1918) and was formally constituted by Royal Proclamation in 1921, not by the GOI Act 1919 itself. The Act provided the framework; the Proclamation brought it to life. It was created as an advisory body for the Princely States and carried NO legislative power — purely advisory. It was a reward to the Princes for their support during WWI.

Why others wrong: (a) The Mandal had no legislative power — it was purely advisory; the British only promised to consult the Princes on policy matters affecting the Princely States.

(b) Membership was nominated/representational, not elected, and was certainly not tied to State revenue. (c) The Act provided the framework but did NOT itself constitute the body in 1919; the formal constitution came by Royal Proclamation in 1921.

Memory hook: MC Report recommended → Royal Proclamation 1921 inaugurated → Mountbatten 1947 finally addressed it.

FYQ 5

With reference to the Government of India Act, 1919, what was the position regarding women's right to vote?

Answer: (A)

(A)

(A) ✓ — The Act did not directly grant women the right to vote. Instead, it empowered Provincial Legislative Assemblies to pass resolutions removing the sex disqualification. When women were admitted to vote, they had to meet the exact same strict property qualifications as men, meaning less than 1% of women actually qualified.

Why others wrong: (b) Universal adult suffrage was never granted by GOI 1919; the Lucknow Pact 1916 demand for broad franchise was explicitly not conceded. (c) The husband's-property + lower-income criterion for women came only with the Government of India Act, 1935 — NOT 1919; this is a UPSC-tested 1919 vs 1935 trap. (d) The Act did not bar women — it left the matter to PLAs, which gradually removed the sex disqualification.

Memory hook: 1919 = PLAs may lift the bar, same male yardstick. 1935 = husband's property + lower threshold.

FYQ 6

Under the Government of India Act, 1919, separate electorates were extended to which of the following communities for the first time (over and above the existing Muslim separate electorate)?

Answer: (B)

(B)

(B) ✓ — Separate electorates for Muslims continued from ICA1909 but were made watertight under GOI 1919, and were now extended to Sikhs, Anglo-Indians, and Christians as well — all with watertight rolls. Members of these communities could vote only in their respective reserved constituencies, not in unreserved seats.

Why others wrong: (a) Depressed Classes did NOT get a separate electorate under GOI 1919; they got reservation of nominated seats — not elected seats — in all legislatures. (c) Parsis and Buddhists were not added to the separate electorate list under the 1919 Act. (d) Depressed Classes received nominated seats, not elected reserved seats, and nothing more; this option excludes Sikhs, Anglo-Indians and Christians who were the actual additions.

Memory hook: SAC = Sikhs, Anglo-Indians, Christians — added to the 1919 watertight rolls.

FYQ 1 Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

"The Dyarchy introduced by the Government of India Act, 1919 was designed not to share power but to discredit Indian self-government." Examine.

Demand of the question: The prompt is a strong critical claim — that Dyarchy's purpose was sabotage, not power-sharing. The directive verb "Examine" asks for evidence-based scrutiny: take the claim seriously, test it against the Act's structural features, & decide whether the structural features support the claim. The phrase "designed not to share but to discredit" is the load-bearing assertion — you must show how the design did the discrediting.

Suggested intro (~25 words): Dyarchy split provincial subjects into Reserved and Transferred — but the split was so loaded against Indian Ministers that failure was structurally guaranteed before any minister took office. Body guidance — 4 themes:

1. The Reserved/Transferred split itself (see 'Dyarchy at the Provincial Level — The Most Debated Feature'). Reserved subjects (Law and Order, Police, Administration of Justice, Finance, Land Revenue, Control over Officials) stayed with the Governor and his officials — no accountability to PLA. Transferred subjects (health, education, local government, agriculture) went to Indian Ministers responsible to PLA. Verdict: Indians received the politically soft subjects with no control over the levers of governance.

2. The money problem (see 'Dyarchy at the Provincial Level — The Most Debated Feature'). Finance was Reserved. Indian Ministers running Transferred subjects had no budget of their own; they had to negotiate funds from a Governor's purse over which they had no claim. Without money, education/health/agriculture could not be staffed, run, or reformed.

3. The bureaucracy problem (see 'Dyarchy at the Provincial Level — The Most Debated Feature'). Control over Officials was Reserved. Indian Ministers could not appoint, transfer, discipline, or override their own staff. A Minister of Education could not move a District Inspector. The Governor could overrule Ministers on any matter (vi).

4. The fallback clause (see 'Dyarchy at the Provincial Level — The Most Debated Feature'). "In case of failure of constitutional machinery", the Governor could take over executive and legislative powers over Transferred subjects too — the safety net for the British, not for the Indian Minister. Verdict: the architecture predicted failure and built in the response in advance.

Closing verdict: Dyarchy's specific design — soft subjects without money, soft subjects without staff, soft subjects with an overruling Governor, & a safety clause for British takeover — was not the awkward result of a transitional compromise. It was a deliberate construction in which Indian Ministers were set up to fail, & the failure was meant to demonstrate that Indians could not govern. The chapter's anchor sentence captures it: "The title was real. The power was not."

Revise: 'Reserved Subjects' + 'Transferred Subjects' + 'Why Dyarchy Was a Problem in Practice'.

FYQ 2 Mains-FYQ · 10 marks · 150 words The Government of India Act, 1919 went further than ICA 1909 but fell short of Swaraj. Discuss the principal sources of INM dissatisfaction with the Act.

Demand of the question: "Discuss" + "principal sources" = enumerate and explain the specific gaps the INM saw. The prompt frames the Act as a transitional step — better than ICA 1909 (acknowledged) but short of Swaraj (the actual goal). The body must list and briefly explain each gap.

Suggested intro (~25 words): GOI 1919 introduced elected majorities at Centre and Provinces — a real advance over ICA 1909 — but the INM judged it against the Lucknow Pact (1916) & the post-1906 Swaraj benchmark, & found it wanting on six counts. Body guidance — 6 sources of dissatisfaction (see 'Why Were INM Leaders Dissatisfied?'):

1. No true federalism (5.1). Subject division between Centre and Provinces was not federal — Centre kept overriding power; no constitutional list-system like the future 7th Schedule (COI 1950).

2. Dyarchy mockery (5.2). At Provincial level, Governor as Centre's agent kept control over Reserved subjects — federalism subverted from within.

3. Limited franchise (5.3). Income-linked right to vote, far short of the Lucknow Pact 1916 demand for franchise "as broad as possible". No universal adult franchise.

4. No responsible government at Centre (5.4). VEC was not accountable to legislature. Provinces had only partial responsibility (for Transferred subjects).

5. Seat allocation by importance, not population (5.5). Punjab weighted for military importance, Bombay for commercial importance — population principle subordinated.

6. No effective self-government (5.6). An improvement over ICA 1909's "limited self-government" but still far from Swaraj — the INC's stated goal since the 1906 Calcutta Session.

Closing verdict: Six specific gaps — federalism, provincial dyarchy, franchise, responsibility, seat allocation, & the self-government ceiling — made the Act a milestone the INM walked past, not one it celebrated.
