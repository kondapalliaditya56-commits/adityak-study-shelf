In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • The RTI Act, 2005 gives every citizen the right to information held by a public authority. The bill came from the National Advisory Council, not the bureaucrats. Earlier steps were the H.D. Shourie working group (1997), the Freedom of Information Bill, 2002 (never notified) and Tamil Nadu's 1997 law.
  • 'Information' is wide: records, memos, e-mails, advice, contracts, samples and electronic data. The right covers inspecting work and records, notes and certified copies, samples and electronic copies.
  • A public authority must catalogue its records, publish 16 types of information within 120 days, appoint PIOs within 100 days and transfer misdirected requests within 5 days.
  • Section 8 lists exemptions such as sovereignty and security, court-forbidden matters, privilege of the legislature, trade secrets, Cabinet papers and private information. The handout calls copyright the only absolute exemption. Exempt information opens after 20 years, with exceptions.
  • Citizens complain to the CIC or SIC. Penalty on a PIO is Rs 250 a day up to Rs 25,000. Fees are waived for BPL applicants and when a deadline is missed.
  • The 2019 Amendment lets the Centre fix the tenure and salary of Information Commissioners, ending the fixed five-year term and the link to the CEC and ECs. Critics fear a loss of independence.

Key terms

Public authority
A body set up by or under the Constitution, a Parliament or state law, or a government notification, or one owned, controlled or substantially financed by government
Substantially financed
Not defined in the Act; the ARC gave some recommendations
PIO
Public Information Officer; designated in each administrative unit
Suo-moto disclosure
The authority gives out information on its own so that fewer RTI requests are needed
Section 8
The list of information exempt from disclosure
Third party
Their information is released only after notice and a hearing; the notice period is 10 days
Retrospective
The Act covers all information held, even if created before the Act began

1 · RTI in India: how the law came#

  • In 1997, for the first time in India, the government set up a working group under H.D. Shourie that brought a draft bill, the Freedom of Information Bill, 2002. It was enacted but not implemented or notified. Most critics called it feeble legislation.
  • Several states then enacted their own RTI laws, with Tamil Nadu the first state in 1997.
  • The RTI Act, 2005: this bill was not prepared by government bureaucrats but by the National Advisory Council.

3 · Main features: information, right and public authority#

  • All citizens have the right to information.
  • Information is defined very broadly: any material in any form including records, documents, memos, e-mail, opinions, advice, press releases, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, papers, samples, models, data material held in any electronic form, and information relating to any private body which is held by a public authority under any other law for the time being in force.

Right to information

  • It is the right to information accessible under the Act, held by or under the control of any public authority, and includes the right to:
  • Inspect work, documents or records.
  • Take notes, extracts or certified copies of documents or records.
  • Take certified samples of material.
  • Obtain information in the form of diskettes, floppies, tapes, video cassettes or any other electronic mode or printouts where the information is stored in a computer or other device.

Public authority means any authority, body or institution of self-government established or constituted

BySource
aBy or under the Constitution
bBy any other law made by Parliament
cBy any other law made by the State legislature
dBy notification issued or order made by the appropriate Government
eIncludes any body owned, controlled or substantially financed (including NGOs substantially financed directly or indirectly by funds provided by the appropriate government)
  • Substantially: The term "substantially" is not defined in the Act. Some recommendations are given in ARC recommendations (see Ch 57: 50 percent of operating costs for 3 years or at least 1 crore).
  • Shortcut: Four sources + owned. Constitution, Parliament law, State law, Government notification, plus bodies owned, controlled or substantially financed.

The judiciary

  • Subhash Agrawal filed a petition in 2009 on whether judges were adhering to a 1997 resolution declaring their assets. The Apex Court stated that the apex court was not covered under the RTI.
  • The CIC later ruled against the Supreme Court. The SC (its administrative side) appealed to the Delhi HC, which sided with the CIC verdict.
  • Eventually, in 2019, the Apex Court held that the judiciary by and large is covered under the RTI Act. Yet some SC observations are problematic:
  • – All information with the RBI about Scheduled Commercial Banks under the RBI Act is subject to disclosure, but in 2019 the courts said the RBI need not disclose banks' risk evaluation reports and financial inspection reports, though many banks are mired in financial mess, scams and scandals.
  • – The SC said extensive RTI use has created fear psychosis and paralysis in the government, and that personal status of civil servants (name, address, physical and mental status) is beyond RTI, privacy to be maintained.
  • – Professional records, assets and liabilities are also beyond RTI
  • Names of interviewers responsible for recruitment need not be disclosed
Handout alert

Handout alert: The judiciary paragraph gives 2009 (petition) and 2019 (the CIC ruling, the Delhi HC verdict and the Apex Court judgement) loosely, in a single run of sentences. Learn it as a sequence: SC said not covered, CIC ruled against SC, Delhi HC sided with CIC, SC in 2019 said judiciary by and large is covered.

Political parties

  • Money raised through electoral bonds is not subject to disclosure
  • Money raised over and above, through proper channels, goes to the Election Commission and is in the public domain
  • Questions on selection of candidates get the usual answers (winnability)
  • Criminal and educational background information is already subject to disclosure
  • In 2013 the CIC said recognized political parties are subject to RTI because they meet the substantial-financing criteria: IT exemptions, free airtime on state media, land at subsidized prices.

Parties' objections to RTI

  • Arguments against:
    • Parties are free associations of people
    • They do not use tax money directly
    • They lack infrastructure to implement RTI
    • It will be used by parties against each other
  • Position now:
    • CIC decision not stayed or nullified by courts
    • Parties still do not implement it, citing that the matter is sub judice, though courts have not nullified the decision
  • Memory aid (Public authority disputes): President/Governors, State federations, BCCI, PPP projects, Judiciary, Political parties: "People See Big Public Justice Places."

6 · Exemptions under Section 8#

  • Section 8 specifies the information exempt from disclosure. The handout lists the points a to q.

Section 8 as the handout lists it

PointExemption
aInformation that would prejudicially affect sovereignty and integrity of India, security, strategic, scientific or economic interest of the state, relations with a foreign state, or lead to incitement of an offence
bInformation expressly forbidden to be published by any court or tribunal, or that constitutes contempt of court
cDisclosure that leads to a breach of privilege of Parliament and state legislatures
dCommercial confidence, trade secrets, intellectual property harming a third party's competitive position, unless a competent authority is satisfied that larger public interest is served
eInformation held in a fiduciary relationship, unless larger public interest warrants disclosure
fInformation received in confidence from a foreign government
gDisclosure that would endanger the physical safety of any person or identify a confidential source for law enforcement or security
hInformation that would impede investigation, apprehension or prosecution of offenders
iCabinet papers including records of deliberations of CoM, Secretaries and other officers
jPersonal information with no relationship to public activity or interest, or causing unwarranted invasion of privacy, unless larger public interest justifies
kPublic interest override: despite the Official Secrets Act 1923 and the Section 8(1) exemptions, a public authority may allow access if disclosure outweighs harm to protected interests
lInfringement of copyright subsisting in a person other than the state: the only absolute exemption in the Act
mInformation not to be denied to Parliament cannot be denied to citizens (as the handout words it)
nIntelligence and security agencies in the Second Schedule are outside the Act, but corruption and human rights violation information must be given
oThird-party information released after notice to and hearing of the third party; notice period 10 days
pAct prevails over the Official Secrets Act 1923 and any inconsistent law; no court may entertain a suit about an order under the Act except by appeal under the Act
qWholly retrospective: applies to information held before the Act came into force, regardless of date

Detail on the key points

  • Cabinet papers (i): decisions of the Council of Ministers, the reasons and the material on which decisions were made shall be made public after the decision has been taken and the matter is complete or over. Matters under the other exemptions are not to be disclosed.
  • Intelligence agencies (n): human rights violation information is provided only after approval of the concerned Information Commission. The Act did not mention investigating agencies, but CBI and NIA were added to the Second Schedule list through an executive order.
  • Parliament (m): the handout says that when denying information to a citizen it should not be disclosed to Parliament, and that what cannot be denied to Parliament cannot be denied to citizens.
  • Retrospective (q):
    • A unique aspect
    • It is wholly retrospective

The 20-year rule

  • Information in exempted categories must be released 20 years after creation, except Cabinet decisions, information relating to breach of legislature privilege, and information prejudicial to sovereignty and integrity, security, strategic and scientific, economic interest of the state, relations with a foreign state, or leading to incitement of an offence.

Third-party information

  • Request involves third-party information: Public authority gives notice
  • Hearing of the third party: Third party heard
  • Notice period: 10 days
  • Release: After notice and hearing

8 · RTI Amendment Act, 2019#

  • Impact on transparency and autonomy.

Key amendments

AreaChange
Tenure and appointmentCentral Government decides tenure of CIC and ICs; five-year term removed
Salary and service conditionsGovernment empowered to fix salaries, allowances and service terms; delinked from CEC and ECs
Autonomy concernsExcessive central control perceived to weaken independence of Information Commissions
StatusCIC is a statutory body, not a constitutional one

Impact on autonomy

  • Negative:
    • Executive overreach (tenure and salary control reduces independence)
    • Public distrust (citizens may hesitate to file RTIs due to perceived bias)
    • Federal dilution (State Information Commissions lose autonomy under central oversight)
  • Positive:
    • Administrative flexibility (governance-specific adjustments to tenure and salaries)
    • Fiscal discipline (cost-effective salaries, independent of EC benchmarks)
    • Policy alignment (central oversight may streamline implementation nationwide)
  • Critical reflection: The amendment balances efficiency with institutional independence, but risks executive overreach. The true impact hinges on how responsibly the government exercises these powers within a framework of democratic accountability.
  • Memory aid (2019 changes): Tenure, Salary, Status: "Time, Salary, Status are with the Centre now."

From Lakshmikanth · Indian Polity, 8th ed. Ch 62-63, Central and State Information Commissions
  • CIC (2005) is not a constitutional body. It has a Chief Information Commissioner and not more than ten Information Commissioners, appointed by the President on the advice of the PM (chair), the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the PM. If there is no recognised Leader of Opposition, the leader of the largest opposition group counts.
  • Members must be persons of eminence in public life with wide knowledge in law, science and technology, social service, management, journalism, mass media or administration. They cannot be MPs or MLAs, hold an office of profit, belong to a political party, or run a business or profession.
  • Term:
    • As the Central Government prescribes or until age 65, whichever is earlier
    • No reappointment. An Information Commissioner can become Chief but the total service cannot exceed five years. Pay and conditions cannot be changed to the member's disadvantage during service
  • Removal by the President: insolvency, conviction for an offence involving moral turpitude, paid employment outside office, unfitness through infirmity of mind or body, or an interest likely to harm official functions. For proved misbehaviour or incapacity the matter first goes to the Supreme Court. A member with an interest in a Central Government contract is deemed guilty of misbehaviour.
  • Complaints the Commission hears: no PIO appointed, information refused, no reply in time, unreasonable fee, incomplete, misleading or false information, and any other matter. It can order an inquiry on its own (suo-moto) and has civil court powers: summoning, discovery of documents, evidence on affidavit, requisitioning public records, issuing summons for witnesses.
  • It can examine any record, and none may be withheld. It can order access in a particular form, direct appointment of a PIO, order publication, change record practices, give more training, seek an annual compliance report, order compensation, impose penalties or reject the application.
  • It sends an annual report to the Central Government, which lays it before each House of Parliament. It can also recommend steps to a public authority that does not follow the Act.
  • State Information Commission: same pattern. Appointed by the Governor on advice of the Chief Minister (chair), the Leader of Opposition in the Assembly and a state Cabinet Minister nominated by the Chief Minister. The number of State Information Commissioners varies by state, and its annual report goes to the State Legislature.
  • 2019 Amendment, 'before' position: terms were fixed at 5 years. The CIC's pay matched the Chief Election Commissioner and an Information Commissioner's matched an Election Commissioner. State Chief's pay matched an Election Commissioner and a State IC's matched the Chief Secretary. The Amendment also removed the deduction of pension from their salary.

Traps#

Trap 1 · Political parties and RTI In 2013 the CIC said recognised political parties are public authorities because of indirect government support. Parties still refuse, saying the matter is sub judice, though courts have not nullified the order. Rule: CIC ruling stands; compliance is the problem.
Trap 2 · The only absolute exemption The handout names copyright infringement (copyright held by someone other than the State) as the only absolute exemption. Other Section 8 grounds can give way to a larger public interest. Rule: copyright is absolute; others are weighed against public interest.
Trap 3 · Intelligence agencies are not fully shut out Agencies in the Second Schedule are exempt, yet information on corruption and human rights violations must be provided (human rights only with the Commission's approval). CBI and NIA were added by executive order. Rule: exempt agencies still answer corruption and human rights allegations.
Trap 4 · After the 2019 Amendment The Centre now decides the tenure, salary and service terms; the five-year term was removed and pay was delinked from the CEC and ECs. The CIC is a statutory body, not a constitutional one. Rule: 2019 = Centre-controlled tenure and pay; CIC is statutory.
