2013PYQ 1 "Lord Dalhousie was the founder of modern India"Mains 2013 · 10 marks · 200 words

"In many ways, Lord Dalhousie was the founder of modern India." Elaborate.

Demand of the question: Elaborate on the claim that Lord Dalhousie was the founder of modern India — which calls for substantiation with specific evidence of how Dalhousie48-56's tenure built the modern Indian infrastructure that survived into the post-1947 nation. The "in many ways" framing tells you the prompt accepts a qualified affirmative — you must enumerate the ways and explain why they qualify as foundational. The directive verb Elaborate asks for examples + analytical reasoning, not a yes/no debate.

Suggested intro (~25 words): Lord Dalhousie48-56 (1848–56) was, in many ways, the founder of modern India — the physical and institutional infrastructure he built for colonial purposes outlasted them, and India inherited it.

Guiding themes (pick 4–5 for a 200-word answer):

1. Railways (1853) — first line Bombay–Thane; by 1856 extended to Calcutta–Raniganj + Madras–Arakkonam

2. Telegraph Department (1853) — 4,000 miles connecting major Indian cities

3. Post Office Act (1854) — uniform half-anna rate from Peshawar to Calcutta

4. Public Works Department (PWD) — separated from military; Grand Trunk Road revival

5. Wood's Despatch (1854) — "Magna Carta of Indian Education"; vernacular schools + universities + structured education administration

6. Three universities (Bombay, Madras, Calcutta) — established 1857 under Lord Canning, but the groundwork was Dalhousie48-56's

Suggested conclusion (~25 words): In many ways, Dalhousie48-56's colonial infrastructure became the skeleton of modern India — the irony being that what he built for empire became the bones of an independent nation. Reconciliation note for Mains-grade rigour: Dalhousie48-56 built this infrastructure for colonial purposes — easier troop movement, cheaper administration, raw material extraction, market creation. The Mains 2013 quote is true and the colonial-purpose framing matters. The reconciliation: the infrastructure outlasted its colonial purpose. India inherited a colonial skeleton and made it the bones of a postcolonial state. The strongest single anchor for this argument is Wood's Despatch (1854) — its education-administration blueprint is what most directly shaped modern India's civil service + university + literacy infrastructure.

FYQ 1 Discuss the role of the Doctrine of Lapse in fuelling the 1857 revolt.Mains-FYQ · 10 marks · 150 words

Demand: Link DoL annexations to specific 1857 grievances; show causal chain from policy to revolt.

Guiding themes:

1. DoL refused Hindu adoption (see 'Doctrine of Lapse: Legal Principle vs Pretext') → 7 States annexed 1848-54 with Jhansi and Nagpur as flashpoints (see 'Annexations via Lapse: Chronology (1848–54)').

2. Pension refusal to Nana Sahib (see 'Doctrine of Lapse Applied to Pensions: Nana Sahib + 1857 Link') and threat to Bahadur Shah Zafar37-57's titular dignity (see 'Critique + Post-Dalhousie').

3. Talukdars and inam-holders dispossessed (see 'The Inam Commission (1852)' and 'Annexation of Awadh (1856)') — created landed grievance coalition.

4. Conclusion: annexation-aggrieved leaders formed the spine of the 1857 revolt — Lakshmibai (Lapse 1854), Nana Sahib (pension 1851), Bahadur Shah Zafar (Mughal dignity).

2. DoL formulated by Court of Directors 1847 but implemented systematically by Dalhousie48-56 (see 'Doctrine of Lapse: Legal Principle vs Pretext').

3. Annexation in Lieu of Subsidy: Berar 1853 from Hyderabad (see 'Annexation in Lieu of

Subsidy: Berar (1853)').

4. Sikh Wars + Punjab annexation 1849 (see 'Sikh State Annexation: 1st + 2nd Anglo-Sikh Wars (1845–49)').

6. Awadh 1856 on misgovernance pretext — NOT under DoL (see 'Annexation of Awadh (1856)').
