Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • Four population groups (Bantus 70%, Europeans 18%, Mixed 9%, Asians 3%); teacher's SAf = CNOT; Memon Muslim traders versus indentured labourers
  • Pietermaritzburg 1893; Franchise Amendment Bill 1894 and Immigration Law Amendment Bill 1895 (leave, re-indenture, or £3 poll tax)
  • Natal Indian Congress 1894 versus INC 1885; Indian Opinion; Phase 1 petitions, prayers, press
  • Phoenix 1904 (Ruskin, Unto This Last) and Tolstoy Farm 1910; Black Act 1906 (RC); Transvaal Immigration Restriction Act 1907
  • Gokhale 1912 (Poll Tax and RC); 1913 Christian-marriage judgement, march of 2,000, Kasturba arrested, Hardinge 10-16; the three 1914 concessions; Prelims 2019: Gandhi and indentured labour

Mains hooks

  • Satyagraha was born in South Africa; Champaran 1917 was its first use on Indian soil
  • Two phases: Constitutional (1896-1906) like the INC Moderates, Extra-Constitutional (1906-1915)
  • The Satyagrahi's five-clause code (teacher: iDeal indian Bf/Husband; goal = irritate the villain)
  • Poll Tax Campaign and the 1913 turn to a mass movement; Phoenix to Tolstoy to the Indian ashrams
  • South Africa as the laboratory for Champaran, Kheda and Dandi

Natal and Transvaal were the laboratory: the code, the training farms and the mass method were all tested there before 1915.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Injustice, then a method, then a mass movement, then victory: an ejection and two Natal laws (injustice) lead to petitions (Phase 1), then to Satyagraha (the method) built on a code and two training farms, then to the 1913 turn (mass), and finally to the three-demand settlement of 1914 (victory).

  1. A barrister, a ticket and a platform (1893). Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi arrives in South Africa as a young barrister hired for a commercial case of a Memon Muslim merchant. At Pietermaritzburg station he is ejected from a first-class compartment solely for being Indian, despite a valid ticket. The humiliation on the cold platform becomes his political catalyst.
    → So he resolves to fight, not to endure.
  2. The community asks him to stay. He planned a short stay, but the Indian community was leaderless and had no educated advocate who understood both law and their grievances. He was the only person who could fight within the system, so he stays: a short visit becomes twenty-two years (1893 to 1915).
    → Soon two Natal laws give him his first fight.
  3. Two Natal laws (1894-95). The Franchise Amendment Bill 1894 would strip Indians of the vote on racial grounds: taxation without representation, like the American colonists of 1776. The Immigration Law Amendment Bill 1895 forces an indentured labourer, after his contract, to leave, re-indenture for two more years, or pay a poll tax of £3 a year.
    → Gandhiji answers within the law.
  4. Petitions, prayers and the press (1896-1906). He founds the Natal Indian Congress, modelled on the INC, and the newspaper Indian Opinion. His methods are petitions, prayers and public meetings: a Moderate in South Africa, like the INC Moderates at home.
    → But the government is unmoved by petitions.
  5. Satyagraha is born (1906). By 1906 he concludes that constitutional methods are not enough, and that the answer must be direct but non-violent. A true Satyagrahi is truthful, non-violent, fearless, ready to accept suffering and loving to the wrongdoer. In 1904 he had already founded Phoenix Settlement near Durban, inspired by Ruskin's Unto This Last.
    → Now a law arrives that tests the method.
  6. The Black Act and the broken promise (1906-14). The Asiatic Law Amendment Act 1906 forces every Indian in Transvaal to carry a Registration Certificate, on pain of fine, jail and deportation. The government offers to withdraw it if Indians register; Gandhiji registers first, and the promise is broken. He leads a bonfire of RCs and Jail Bharo through the Passive Resistance Association.
    → The government now closes the borders.
  7. Caged within provinces (1907-08). The Transvaal Immigration Restriction Act 1907 bars movement from Cape, Natal and Orange Free State into Transvaal. Indians cross illegally, refuse bail and hawk without licence to fill the jails; Gandhiji is jailed in 1908.
    → A long struggle needs a base.
  8. Tolstoy Farm and Gokhale (1910-12). In 1910 Tolstoy Farm near Johannesburg becomes the residential base for Satyagrahis and their families, part ashram and part training ground: Phoenix, then Tolstoy, then the Indian ashrams. In 1912 Gokhale visits, the government promises repeal and breaks the promise, and Gokhale advises focus on two demands: abolish the Poll Tax and the RC.
    → Then a court ruling lights the fuse.
  9. 1913: the movement goes mass. The Poll Tax Campaign brings indentured labourers in. A Supreme Court judgement recognises only Christian marriages, so Hindu and Muslim wives are legally not wives. Indians cross into Transvaal illegally, Kasturba Gandhi is arrested, 2,000 workers march, and all workers strike.
    → Pressure now comes from India as well.
  10. Gokhale tours India; Hardinge 10-16 speaks. Gokhale travels across India building public opinion, and Viceroy Hardinge 10-16 publicly condemns the South African government: unprecedented pressure from within the British Empire. The South African government comes to the table.
    → So the talks end in a settlement.
  11. Victory and return (1914-15). The government concedes all three demands: the Poll Tax is abolished, the RC requirement is dropped and Indian marriages are validated. Gandhiji returns to India in January 1915 as a leader who had won, having learned that mass civil disobedience works. The laboratories of Natal and Transvaal would be deployed at Champaran, Kheda and Dandi.

In simple words#

  • Gandhiji came to South Africa in 1893 for a case and stayed 22 years after he was thrown out of a first-class compartment at Pietermaritzburg.
  • Two Natal laws, the Franchise Bill 1894 and the Immigration Bill 1895 (a £3 poll tax), led him to found the Natal Indian Congress and start petitions, prayers and the press (Phase 1).
  • When petitions failed, Satyagraha was born (Phase 2, 1906 on): a bonfire of Registration Certificates against the Black Act, Jail Bharo, illegal border crossings and hawking, with Phoenix and Tolstoy Farm as training bases.
  • In 1913 the poll tax, the Christian-marriage judgement, Kasturba's arrest, a march of 2,000 and a strike made it a mass movement, and Hardinge 10-16 condemned South Africa.
  • The 1914 settlement conceded all three demands and Gandhiji sailed home in January 1915 with the method that he would use at Champaran, Kheda and Dandi.

2 · How did Gandhiji end up staying?#

Story link

Because an ejected barrister decided to fight, and because a leaderless community asked him to stay, a short visit became twenty-two years. Ask: what made him stay?

  • Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi arrived in South Africa in 1893 as a young barrister, hired to handle a commercial legal case for a Memon Muslim merchant.
  • Shortly after landing, at Pietermaritzburg railway station, he was ejected from a first-class compartment solely for being Indian, despite holding a valid ticket.
  • The humiliation of that night on the cold platform became his political catalyst: the moment he resolved to fight racial discrimination rather than quietly endure it.
Teacher's board

Stressed: "ejected from a first-class compartment solely for being Indian" and "resolved to fight racial discrimination rather than quietly endure it".

  • He had planned a brief stay. The Indian community, leaderless and without any educated advocate who understood both law and their grievances, persuaded him to stay on.
  • He was the only person who could fight for them within the system.
  • A short visit became twenty-two years (1893 to 1915), years that forged the methods and the man who would transform India's independence movement.

3 · What started the struggle? The two trigger laws#

Story link

Because Natal wanted to cut the Indians out of politics and out of the colony, two laws provoked his first response. Ask: what did each law take, the vote or the livelihood?

  • Two laws of the 1890s directly provoked Gandhiji's first political response in South Africa.

3.1 Franchise Amendment Bill, 1894 (Natal)

  • The Bill sought to disenfranchise Indians, to strip them of the Right to Vote.
  • Some Indians had earlier qualified by a wealth criterion (property above a certain value).
  • The 1894 Bill aimed to close that door entirely, purely on racial grounds.
  • Later scholars called this "Taxation without Representation" (TWR) in a different form: Indians paid taxes and contributed to the economy but were denied any say in who governed them.
  • Compare the American colonists' grievance against Britain in 1776: taxed by a Parliament in which they had no voice.
Teacher's board

Stressed: "This Bill sought to disenfranchise Indians".

3.2 Immigration Law Amendment Bill, 1895 (Natal)

  • The law gave indentured labourers a brutal choice on completing their contract:
OptionWhat it meant
1Leave South Africa
2Re-indenture for two more years
3If neither, pay a poll tax of £3 per year, a large sum for a labourer on bare subsistence wages

Teacher's board
  • Option 2 = "again sign oppressive contracts".
  • Stressed: "poll tax of £3 per year".
  • The tax was not about revenue. It was about making Indian settlement economically unsustainable: go back, sign another bond, or pay an annual penalty "just for existing".

4 · Phase 1: the Constitutional struggle (1896-1906)#

Story link

Because he first trusted the law, he behaved like an INC Moderate. Ask: how is the NIC different from the INC?

  • Gandhiji's early response was entirely within the law: petitions, prayers and public meetings (the handout: "petitions, prayers, and press").
  • He founded the Natal Indian Congress (NIC), modelled on the Indian National Congress at home, to give the community a formal, organised political voice.
  • He launched the newspaper Indian Opinion to document and publicise Indian grievances.
  • Approach: moderate, respectful, constitutional: appeal to the conscience of the rulers, expose injustice by argument, trust that reason would prevail.
Teacher's board

Board shorthand: NIC beside Natal Indian Congress; Np (newspaper) beside Indian Opinion.

  • This phase echoes the Moderates of the INC in India at the same time: petitions, prayers and press.
  • Gandhiji was, in spirit, a Moderate in South Africa before he became something far more in India.
Handout alert
  • Exam trap. The Natal Indian Congress (1894) and the Indian National Congress (1885) are separate bodies founded in different countries.
  • Gandhiji founded the NIC
  • He did not found the INC.

5 · Phase 2: the Extra-Constitutional struggle (1906-1915)#

Two phases of the struggle
Story link

Because petitions failed, a new method was needed: Satyagraha. Ask: what code and which training grounds did it need?

  • By 1906 Gandhiji concluded that constitutional methods alone were not enough: the government was unmoved by petitions.
  • Something more direct was needed, but it had to remain non-violent.
  • This was the crucible in which Satyagraha was born.
Handout alert
  • Exam trap. Satyagraha was first developed and deployed in South Africa, not India.
  • Gandhiji brought it to India only after 1915
  • the Champaran Satyagraha (1917) was its first use on Indian soil

5.1 What is a Satyagrahi?

  • Satyagraha was not passive submission: it was active, disciplined resistance grounded in a specific personal code. A true Satyagrahi must be:
ClauseThe code
iTruthful in word, action and intention
iiNon-violent absolutely, even under provocation
iiiFearless: willing to face arrest, imprisonment and physical suffering
ivReady to accept suffering, not to inflict it
vLoving toward the wrongdoer: hate has no place
Teacher's board
  • Truthful: gives courage and credibility of the demand. Non-violent: longer movement, greater participation. Fearless: willing to bear violence.
  • Loving: Gandhiji had faith in the humanity of the evil-doer, who will realise his mistakes sooner or later if we follow Satyagraha.
  • "Goal of Satyagrahi = IRRITATE THE VILLAIN".
  • "Mnemonic for exam": "iDeal indian Bf/Husband".
  • Standard:
    • Not "protest when convenient"
    • It demanded a complete transformation of the individual.
  • An undisciplined Satyagrahi was a contradiction in terms.

5.2 Phoenix Settlement (1904)

  • 1904: inspired by John Ruskin's Unto This Last, Gandhiji founded the Phoenix Settlement near Durban.
  • Residents lived and worked together, practising self-reliance and simple living.
  • Indian Opinion was also printed here.
  • It was his 1st communal living experiment, the philosophical precursor to Tolstoy Farm (1910) and later his ashrams in India (link Chapter 36: Ruskin's influence on Gandhian ideology).
Teacher's board

Stressed: "John Ruskin's Unto This Last" and "Tolstoy Farm (1910)".

5.3 Satyagraha against Registration Certificates (1906-1914)

  • The Asiatic Law Amendment Act 1906, bitterly called the Black Act, was passed by the Transvaal government.
  • It required every Indian to carry a Registration Certificate (RC) at all times.
  • Failure to produce it on demand meant fine, imprisonment and deportation.
  • The RC was a physical identity card to be produced to any authority: "an instrument of racial surveillance".
Teacher's board

Stressed: "Registration Certificate (RC)". Margin: "COMPULSORY VIDAI" [second word uncertain: check your notebook].

  • The government offered to withdraw the law if Indians registered voluntarily.
  • Gandhiji, in good faith, was the first to register, leading by example.
  • The government broke its promise.
  • Response: a bonfire of Registration Certificates, then courting arrest ("Jail Bharo").
  • The struggle ran through the Passive Resistance Association.

5.4 Satyagraha against immigration laws (1907)

  • The Transvaal Immigration Restriction Act 1907 barred Indians from moving freely between provinces (from Cape Province, Natal or Orange Free State into Transvaal): Indians were "physically caged" within provincial boundaries.
  • Response: Indians crossed provincial boundaries illegally, courted arrest and refused bail.
  • Indians in Transvaal hawked without a licence, a minor illegal act committed deliberately to fill the jails.
  • Gandhiji was jailed in 1908
  • the prisons overflowed
  • Strategy: make enforcement so costly that the government must negotiate.

5.5 Tolstoy Farm (1910)

  • 1910: extended struggle costs morale, families and livelihoods, so Gandhiji founded Tolstoy Farm, a communal settlement near Johannesburg, as a residential base for Satyagrahis and their families during the passive phases.
  • Named after the Russian writer Leo Tolstoy, whose ideas on non-violent resistance influenced him.
  • Part ashram, part training ground: residents learned self-reliance (farming, crafts, basic trades).
  • If you are fearless and self-sufficient, the government has fewer levers against you.
  • This is the seed of the ashram model in India.
  • Progression: Phoenix Settlement (1904) → Tolstoy Farm (1910) → Sabarmati/Sevagram in India, each building on the last.

5.6 The visit of Gokhale (1912)

  • Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Gandhiji's political mentor and a leading Moderate of the INC, visited South Africa in 1912.
  • The government, impressed by his stature, promised that discriminatory laws would be repealed.
  • After he left, the promise was broken.
  • His advice: focus on two emotionally resonant demands: abolition of the Poll Tax (under the Immigration Law) and abolition of the Registration Certificate (under the Black Act).
  • These two hit every Indian household and could mobilise the masses.

6 · The Poll Tax Campaign and the final countdown (1913)#

Story link

Because Gokhale had narrowed the demands to those every household felt, 1913 made the struggle mass. Ask: which two classes joined this year?

  • By 1913 the movement was at a turning point: the Poll Tax Campaign gave it a mass character.
  • Until then it was led by educated Indians (lawyers, merchants, community leaders).
  • The poll tax hit the indentured labourer directly, so the working class joined.
  • 1913 Supreme Court judgement: only Christian marriages were legally valid.
  • Hindu, Muslim and other rites were not recognised: Indian wives were legally not wives, their children potentially illegitimate.
  • The community was outraged: the struggle was now about family, dignity and religious identity, not just taxes and documents.

  • The sequence was the closest thing to a mass uprising the community had seen:

7 · Outcome: what did Gandhiji win?#

Story link

Because the pressure now came from workers, wives, India and the Viceroy, the government gave in on all three. Ask: which demand did not return?

  • The negotiations ended with the government conceding all three key demands:
  • i) Poll Tax abolished: the £3 annual penalty on ex-indentured labourers who chose to stay was removed.
  • ii) Registration Certificates: the Black Act requirement was dropped.
  • iii) Indian marriages validated: Hindu, Muslim and other rites were given legal recognition.
Number card · The three 1914 concessions
2 + 1 = 3
Gokhale's two demands plus the marriage ruling.
Poll Taxabolished (Gokhale's demand 1)RCdropped (Gokhale's demand 2)MarriagesIndian marriages validated (the 1913 grievance)
  • Each win had a direct, concrete impact on thousands of families.
  • Gandhiji returned to India in January 1915, "not as a defeated petitioner but as a leader who had won".
  • Lessons:
    • Mass civil disobedience works
    • ordinary people, not just elites, can be the soldiers of a non-violent movement
    • The State's most powerful weapon, the law, is neutralised when people accept its punishment without fear.
  • The laboratories of Natal and Transvaal produced the experiments deployed at Champaran, Kheda, Dandi and beyond.

Recall sheet#

Read the question, answer in your head, then tap to check.

Why did Gandhiji go to South Africa and why did he stay?1893: as a young barrister for a Memon Muslim merchant's commercial case. Ejected at Pietermaritzburg despite a first-class ticket, he resolved to fight racial discrimination; the leaderless Indian community persuaded him to stay (22 years).
Population groups of South Africa?Bantus 70%, European settlers 18%, Mixed race 9%, Asians 3%.
Teacher's mnemonic for the four provinces (1911)?SAf = CNOT: Cape Colony, Natal (maximum Indians), Orange Free State, Transvaal.
Two kinds of Indians in South Africa?Memon Muslim traders and merchants (voluntary); indentured labourers (bond contracts, sugar plantations and mines, after 1890).
What was indentured labour?A contract-bound, tied labour system, a step above slavery in name only: a fixed term under conditions hard to escape.
Franchise Amendment Bill 1894?Natal Bill to disenfranchise Indians on racial grounds; some had qualified by property. Called Taxation without Representation, like 1776.
Immigration Law Amendment Bill 1895?After the contract: leave, re-indenture for two years, or pay a £3 poll tax a year (to make settlement unsustainable).
Phase 1 (1896-1906) methods?Petitions, prayers, public meetings; Natal Indian Congress (modelled on the INC); Indian Opinion newspaper; a Moderate approach.
NIC versus INC?NIC 1894, founded by Gandhiji in South Africa; INC 1885, not his.
Why did Gandhiji turn to Satyagraha in 1906?Constitutional methods failed; the government was unmoved by petitions; a direct but non-violent method was needed.
The five clauses of the Satyagrahi's code?Truthful; non-violent; fearless; ready to accept suffering, not inflict it; loving toward the wrongdoer.
Teacher's mnemonic and goal for the code?"iDeal indian Bf/Husband"; goal of a Satyagrahi = irritate the villain; Gandhiji had faith the evil-doer will realise his mistakes.
Phoenix Settlement?1904, near Durban, inspired by Ruskin's Unto This Last; self-reliance and simple living; Indian Opinion printed there; first communal experiment.
Black Act?Asiatic Law Amendment Act 1906 (Transvaal): every Indian must carry a Registration Certificate at all times; penalty fine, imprisonment, deportation.
What broken promise led to the bonfire?The government offered to withdraw the law if Indians registered voluntarily; Gandhiji registered first; the promise was broken; bonfire of RCs, Jail Bharo, via the Passive Resistance Association.
Transvaal Immigration Restriction Act 1907?Barred free movement from Cape, Natal and Orange Free State into Transvaal; resisted by illegal crossings, refused bail, hawking without licence; Gandhiji jailed 1908.
Tolstoy Farm 1910?Near Johannesburg; residential base for Satyagrahis and families; part ashram, part training ground; named after Leo Tolstoy.
Progression of Gandhi's settlements?Phoenix Settlement (1904) to Tolstoy Farm (1910) to Sabarmati/Sevagram in India.
Gokhale in 1912?Visited South Africa; the government promised repeal and then broke it; advised focus on Poll Tax and RC abolition.
Why did the 1913 campaign go mass?The Poll Tax hit indentured labourers directly, so the working class joined; the Christian-marriage judgement made every family a stakeholder.
The 1913 marriage judgement?The Supreme Court held only Christian marriages valid: Hindu and Muslim wives not legally wives, children potentially illegitimate.
Six steps of the final struggle?Illegal crossing into Transvaal; Kasturba arrested; march of 2,000 workers; province-wide strike; Gokhale's India tour; Hardinge 10-16 condemns. Result: talks.
The three concessions?Poll Tax abolished; RC dropped; Indian marriages validated. Return to India January 1915.
What did South Africa teach Gandhiji?Mass civil disobedience works; ordinary people can fight non-violently; the law's weapon is neutralised by accepting punishment without fear; Natal and Transvaal as laboratories for Champaran, Kheda and Dandi.
Where was Satyagraha first used?Developed in South Africa; first use on Indian soil was Champaran 1917.
Prelims 2019 statement on indenture?Gandhi was instrumental in its abolition (true). He supported the 1918 War Conference recruitment (so statement 2 is false). Answer (b) 1 and 3.
What does the teacher say about this chapter?"We won't do in class", "Upon student voluntary", "No PYQ", "Bipan Chandra chapter therefore included". Prelims 2019 still tests its indenture anchor.
Practice Mains: "Gandhiji did not bring Satyagraha to India; he brought it back from South Africa." Examine.Intro: 22 years (1893-1915) of experiment. Body: code (five clauses); training grounds (Phoenix 1904, Tolstoy 1910); repertoire (bonfire of RCs, Jail Bharo, illegal crossings, hawking, strikes, march); mass turn of 1913. Closing: Natal and Transvaal were the laboratories for Champaran, Kheda and Dandi.
Practice Mains: What converged in 1913 to bring the South African struggle to its peak?Intro: until 1913 an educated-led struggle. Body: Poll Tax Campaign drew in labourers; the Christian-marriage judgement hit every family; Kasturba's arrest, the 2,000-worker march, strike and Hardinge 10-16's condemnation. Closing: talks and the 1914 settlement (Poll Tax abolished, RC dropped, marriages validated).
Who was Hardinge 10-16 in this story?The Viceroy who publicly condemned the South African government's treatment of Indians: pressure from within the British Empire.
How was the Poll Tax linked to indentured labour?It was the penalty on ex-indentured labourers who chose neither to leave nor to re-indenture: £3 a year (1895); abolished 1914.
