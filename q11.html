FYQ 2

With reference to Pitt's India Act 1784, which one of the following is correct?

Answer: (B)

(b): Pitt's India Act 1784 banned offensive war by the Governor-General without express authorisation from the Court of Directors of the EIC or the Board of Control of the British State; defensive war was permitted. The reason was ruinous war expenditure from the Three Carnatic Wars (1746–63), Battle of Buxar (1764), Anglo-Mysore Wars (1767–69 and 1780–84), and 1st Anglo-Maratha War (1775–82).

(a) ✗ — opposite of what the Act did. (c) ✗ — Act did not grant sovereignty over princely States. (d) ✗ — Act mandated balance of power + Indirect Rule, NOT direct conquest.

FYQ 4

With reference to the two roots of the Subsidiary Alliance, which one of the following is correct?

Answer: (B)

(b). +. Why (b) is correct: The Subsidiary Alliance had two prior roots that Wellesley98-05 only formalised, not invented. The French root was Dupleix42-54's installation of a French force at Hyderabad in 1751 — Salabat Jung was made Nizam, Marquis de Bussy was stationed at his court, and the host State paid for the French troops. The British root was Robert Clive57-67's mini Subsidiary Alliance with Awadh in the Treaty of Allahabad (1765) — an EIC army permanently stationed in Awadh, paid by Awadh via annual subsidy. Wellesley98-05 in 1798 took this two-strand template and turned it into a full contractual treaty with a standard Model. (a) ✗ — SA was not Wellesley's invention; he formalised existing Dupleix + Clive precedents. (c) ✗ — Cornwallis86-93's tenure focused on the Permanent Settlement (1793) and judicial reform, not SA. (d) ✗ — Mysore 1799 was a signatory, not the first precedent; the precedents go back to Hyderabad 1751 and Awadh 1765. *Memory hook: Dupleix 1751 + Clive 1765 → Wellesley 1798 (formalisation, not invention).*

FYQ 6

Why did the EIC specifically demand Foreign Policy as part of the Subsidiary Alliance?

Answer: (B)

(b).

Why (b) is correct: The EIC maintained the army using the host State's own subsidy money. Without Foreign Policy in the EIC's hands, the legal right to deploy that army could still belong to the Indian ruler. By taking Foreign Policy, the EIC ensured it alone could declare war or peace using the troops it commanded — converting the Indian State's army into an EIC instrument with the ruler footing the bill.

(a) ✗ — controlling European trade was a separate concern; Foreign Policy in the SA was about military deployment. (c) ✗ — there was no Mughal Emperor restriction the EIC needed to evade in 1798. (d) ✗ — the EIC was not seeking to internationalise the conflict; it was localising military control. *Memory hook: EIC paid the army with the ruler's money; Foreign Policy let it ALSO command the army legally.*

FYQ 1 How did Wellesley98-05 evade the conquest ban of Pitt’s India Act 1784 to build British paramountcy through the Subsidiary Alliance? 150 words · 10 marks "Pitt’s India Act 1784 banned the EIC from territorial conquest. Yet under Wellesley98-05 (Governor-General 1798–1805) the EIC absorbed half the subcontinent. Examine the Subsidiary Alliance as the legal-political device that made this possible."

Demand of the question: Show how Wellesley used the Subsidiary Alliance to expand WITHOUT formally conquering — the legal trick that turned ban into expansion.

Suggested intro (~25 words): Pitt’s India Act 1784 banned conquest. Wellesley98-05 used the Subsidiary Alliance — a "voluntary" treaty that transferred foreign policy to the EIC — to expand without conquering.

Guiding themes (pick 4-5 for a 150-word answer):

1. Two roots of the SA — Dupleix42-54’s 1751 Hyderabad model (French root) + Clive’s 1765 Treaty of Allahabad (British root); Wellesley98-05 codified both into the Model Treaty (see 'Two Roots of the SA: Dupleix42-54 + ToA1765 [ ▲ Index ]' and 'Model Treaty of Subsidiary Alliance + Legal Logic [ ▲ Index ]').

2. Legal trick — the Indian ruler kept his throne but surrendered foreign policy and external defence to the EIC; the EIC stationed its own troops paid for by the ruler — protectorate without annexation (see 'Model Treaty of Subsidiary Alliance + Legal Logic [ ▲ Index ]').

3. Foreign-policy logic — by taking control of foreign policy, the EIC could fight wars in the Indian ruler’s name; conquest formally remained absent because no annexation document was signed (see 'Model Treaty of Subsidiary Alliance + Legal Logic [ ▲ Index ]').

4. Vicious-chain consequences — SA crippled Indian States: their armies disbanded, finances drained to pay the subsidy, court factions left to scheme; many States collapsed within a generation (see 'Vicious-Chain Consequences of Subsidiary Alliance for Indian States [ ▲ Index ]').

5. Nine signatories (1798–1818) — Hyderabad, Mysore, Awadh, Tanjore, Surat, Carnatic, Peshwa, Sindhia, Bhonsle of Berar; covered most of southern + eastern India by Wellesley’s recall in 1805 (see 'Which States Signed and Under What Circumstances [ ▲ Index ]' and 'Direct Annexations + Wellesley98-05’s Recall [ ▲ Index ]').

Suggested conclusion (~20 words): The Subsidiary Alliance turned Pitt’s conquest ban into expansion law — the EIC ruled India without formally conquering it.

FYQ 2 Examine the vicious-chain consequences of the Subsidiary Alliance for the Indian States that signed it. 150 words · 10 marks "The Subsidiary Alliance was sold to Indian rulers as protection. In practice it triggered a vicious chain of consequences that hollowed out the States that signed. Examine."

Demand of the question: Show the chain — step by step — of how SA crippled the State that signed it. Each link feeds the next; the result is internal collapse without external invasion.

Suggested intro (~25 words): The Subsidiary Alliance was a slow vivisection — army disbanded, fisc drained, court paralysed, succession compromised. Most signatories collapsed within a generation.

Guiding themes (pick 4-5 for a 150-word answer):

1. Disbanded army — the ruler’s own army was disbanded; only EIC troops remained — the ruler lost the ability to defend himself or even police his own State (see 'Vicious-Chain Consequences of Subsidiary Alliance for Indian States [ ▲ Index ]').

2. Fiscal drain — the ruler paid for the EIC troops stationed in his State; the subsidy was huge and ate revenue surplus; this destroyed the ability to fund education, irrigation, infrastructure (see 'Vicious-Chain Consequences of Subsidiary Alliance for Indian States [ ▲ Index ]').

3. Court paralysis — with foreign policy removed, the ruler’s court lost its strategic function; factions schemed over palace politics only; talented administrators left (see 'Vicious-Chain Consequences of Subsidiary Alliance for Indian States [ ▲ Index ]').

4. EIC Resident as real ruler — the EIC posted a Resident at the court; the Resident decided what the ruler could and could not do; the ruler became a figurehead (see 'Model Treaty of Subsidiary Alliance + Legal Logic [ ▲ Index ]' and 'Vicious-Chain Consequences of Subsidiary Alliance for Indian States [ ▲ Index ]').

5. Eventual annexation — when the State was bankrupt + the ruler weak, the EIC could annex with little resistance (Awadh 1856, Doctrine of Lapse cases). SA was the slow fuse; annexation was the explosion (see 'Vicious-Chain Consequences of Subsidiary Alliance for Indian States [ ▲ Index ]' and 'Direct Annexations + Wellesley98-05’s Recall [ ▲ Index ]').

Suggested conclusion (~20 words): The Subsidiary Alliance was the EIC’s slow poison: army gone, money gone, court gone, ruler gone. Annexation finished the process.
