1 · Local government and the original Constitution#

Panchayati Raj is India's framework of rural local self-government: elected bodies at village (Gram Panchayat), intermediate/block (Panchayat Samiti) and district (Zila Parishad) levels plan, implement and monitor rural development. It brings decisions closer to people and keeps governance accountable and inclusive.

Gandhi's idea

Gram Swaraj: self-reliant, self-governing villages as the backbone of democracy. Gandhi believed "India lives in her villages", so empowerment must begin at the village.

What local government means

  • Administration of a small locality by a body representing local inhabitants.
  • A fairly large level of autonomy.
  • Raises at least part of its revenue locally.
  • Spends it on local services: basic amenities, sewage, sanitation, water supply, street lighting, roads, bridges, upkeep of the area.

The original Constitution

Only two provisions at the start
ProvisionContent
Article 40 (DPSP, Part IV)State shall organise village panchayats and give them the powers and authority needed to function as units of self-government
State List, entry 5Local self-government is a State subject

The original Constitution gave local bodies constitutional importance but not constitutional status; status came in 1992. Art. 40 was only directive, so implementation was left to each State.

Importance versus status

Importance = Art. 40 + State List entry 5 (1950). Status = Part IX through the 73rd Amendment (1992).

3 · Committees on Panchayati Raj#

Each committee added a piece of the final design.

Order of committees
BAGLTG

Balwant Rai Mehta (1957), Ashok Mehta (1977), G.V.K. Rao (1985), L.M. Singhvi (1986), Thungon (1988), Gadgil (1988): "Big Apples Grow Like Tiny Grapes".

Six committees at a glance
CommitteeObjectiveKey recommendationsImpact
Balwant Rai Mehta (1957)Examine Community Development Programme (1952) and National Extension Service (1953); recommend democratic decentralisationThree tiers (GP, Panchayat Samiti, Zila Parishad) linked by indirect elections; GP directly elected; Samiti = executive body; Zila Parishad = advisory and supervisory body; District Collector chairs Zila Parishad; genuine, progressive and continuous transfer of power and resourcesFoundation of the system; Rajasthan first (Nagaur, 2 Oct 1959), then Andhra Pradesh
Ashok Mehta (1977)Reasons for the decline of PRIs; revitaliseTwo tiers: Zila Parishad (primary unit of planning) and Mandal Panchayat (group of villages, 15,000-20,000 population); SC/ST seats in proportion to population; political parties to take part; regular social audits and a State Panchayati Raj Commission; District Planning Committee; constitutional recognition strongly recommendedNot implemented because the Janata Government fell (1979); redefined PR as an instrument of decentralised planning
G.V.K. Rao (1985)Review administrative arrangements for rural developmentZila Parishad as principal planning and coordinating body; DRDA merged with Zila Parishad; strengthen block-level administration; popular participation and bureaucratic accountabilityDistrict made focal point; influenced the 73rd Amendment
L.M. Singhvi (1986)Review status of PRIs; constitutional and legal measuresConstitutional recognition; a new Part exclusively for PR; regular, free and fair elections through State Election Commissions; Nyaya Panchayats; Gram Sabha as the foundationMost recommendations went into the 73rd Amendment
P.K. Thungon (1988)Groundwork for constitutional recognition and frameworkThree tiers; elections every five years; reservation for SCs, STs and women; SFCs and SECsImmediate basis of the 64th Amendment Bill (1989), later the 73rd Act after re-drafting by the Narasimha Rao government

Ashok Mehta contrast

Mehta 1957 = three tiers, Samiti is the executive block. Ashok Mehta 1977 = two tiers, district is primary and Mandal Panchayat (15,000-20,000) replaces the block.

Handout alert

The Ashok Mehta Committee is dated 1977 in the Panchayati Raj sections but 1978 in the urban committees list. Use 1977 (appointed by the Janata party; handout says its recommendations lapsed when the Janata Government fell in 1979).

4 · The road to the 73rd Amendment#

Before 1992, PRIs were weak and irregular because they depended on State discretion and had no constitutional backing.

Three governments, three attempts

Rajiv Gandhi Government (1989)
64th Amendment Bill: three tiers, regular elections, reservation for SCs, STs and women, State Finance and Election Commissions, protection from arbitrary dissolution. Lok Sabha passed it in August 1989; Rajya Sabha rejected it (critics feared centralisation)
V.P. Singh Government (1990)
June 1990 two-day conference of Chief Ministers; fresh Bill in the Lok Sabha in September 1990; government fell so the Bill lapsed. Built consensus and cooperative federalism
P.V. Narasimha Rao Government (1991-92)
Redrafted, removed centralising clauses; Bill in the Lok Sabha September 1991; passed December 1992
73rd Amendment Act, 1992
Presidential assent 20 April 1993; in force 24 April 1993; added Part IX (Arts. 243-243O) and the Eleventh Schedule (29 subjects)

64th Bill (1989)

  • Rajiv Gandhi
  • passed Lok Sabha
  • defeated in Rajya Sabha
  • seen as over-centralising

73rd Act (1992)

  • Narasimha Rao
  • consultation and redrafting
  • stressed State autonomy
  • became law
Leaders
RVN

Rajiv (64th, 1989), V.P. Singh (1990 Bill, lapsed), Narasimha Rao (73rd, 1992): "Road Via New-delhi".

5 · 73rd Amendment: Part IX, Articles 243 to 243E#

The Act made PRIs part of the justiciable Constitution. It operationalised Art. 40, obliged States to set up a uniform three-tier system, hold regular elections and devolve powers, and marks India's shift from representative to participatory democracy.

Articles 243 to 243E
ArticleRule
243 DefinitionsDistrict; Gram Sabha (persons registered in the electoral rolls of a village within the Panchayat area); intermediate level (between village and district, specified by the Governor); Panchayat (self-government institution for rural areas); Panchayat area; population (last preceding census with published figures); village (specified by the Governor, may be a group of villages)
243A Gram SabhaBase of the democratic pyramid: all registered voters in the Panchayat area; village assembly to deliberate and oversee local development
243B ConstitutionUniform three tiers: village, intermediate, district. States with population below 20 lakh may skip the intermediate tier
243C CompositionAll members at every level directly elected. Village chairpersons as per State law; intermediate and district chairpersons indirectly elected by and from among elected members
243D ReservationSC/ST in proportion to population; women one-third of seats and of chairperson posts (including SC/ST women); Backward Classes at the State's discretion; exception: Arunachal Pradesh (83rd Amendment, 2000)
243E Duration5 years; fresh election within 6 months of dissolution; a reconstituted body serves only the remainder of the term
Articles A to O
GCCRDDPTFAEUECB

Gram Sabha (A), Constitution (B), Composition (C), Reservation (D), Duration (E), Disqualification (F), Powers (G), Taxes (H), Finance Commission (I), Audit (J), Elections (K), UTs (L), Exemptions (M), Continuance (N), Bar on courts (O): "Gram Councils Choose Representatives; Duration Dictates Powers; Taxes Fund Accounts; Elections Unite; Exempt areas Continue Barring courts."

Numbers to hold

20 lakh (skip middle tier), one-third women, 5 years, 6 months, 21 years, one year (continuance), 24 April 1993.

Gram Sabha in more detail

  • Direct-democracy platform of rural governance.
  • Powers: approves the Gram Panchayat's annual plans and budgets; reviews development and welfare schemes; conducts social audits (e.g., MGNREGA Sec. 17); identifies beneficiaries for housing, pensions, subsidies.
  • Backing: State laws (e.g., MP Gram Swaraj Adhiniyam, Kerala People's Plan Campaign) and flagship programmes like MGNREGA and the PESA Act 1996.
  • Eg: Kerala and Rajasthan empower Gram Sabhas to approve annual plans and social audits.
Gram Sabha powers
ARSI

Approves plans and budgets, Reviews schemes, Social audits, Identifies beneficiaries: "All Residents See Improvements".

PESA Act 1996

Extends Gram Sabha powers to Scheduled Areas (Fifth Schedule); lets it approve projects, manage minor water bodies, and safeguard tribal culture and identity.

Examples in the handout

Himachal Pradesh and Sikkim: functional three-tier. Goa, being smaller, runs a two-tier system. Bihar and Rajasthan have expanded women's reservation to 50%.

6 · Articles 243F to 243O#

Articles 243F to 243O
ArticleRule
243F DisqualificationIneligible for State Legislature elections, or under State laws; minimum age 21; disputes go to an authority designated by the State Legislature
243G PowersStates may devolve powers so Panchayats work as self-government institutions: plan and implement economic development and social justice schemes, including the 29 subjects of the Eleventh Schedule. Eg: Kerala's People's Plan Campaign gives local bodies up to 40% of State plan funds
243H Taxes and fundsState legislature may authorise Panchayats to levy, collect and appropriate taxes and fees; assign State taxes; give grants-in-aid; create Panchayat funds. Eg: Karnataka and Maharashtra: property tax and water charges at GP level
243I Finance CommissionGovernor constitutes an SFC every 5 years to review Panchayat finances: principles for tax sharing and grants, measures for financial autonomy, other fiscal reforms. Report laid before the State Legislature with an Action Taken Report. Eg: 15th Central FC: Rs 2.36 lakh crore for local bodies (2021-26)
243J AuditStates legislate for maintenance and audit of accounts. Eg: Kerala online auditing
243K ElectionsSuperintendence, direction and control of elections: State Election Commission, headed by a State Election Commissioner appointed by the Governor, removed only as a High Court judge is. SECs conduct elections for both Panchayats and Municipalities
243L Union TerritoriesPart IX applies to UTs with modifications by the President
243M Exempt areasDoes not apply to Nagaland, Meghalaya, Mizoram; Scheduled and tribal areas; hill areas of Manipur; Darjeeling district of West Bengal. Parliament passed PESA 1996 for Scheduled Areas
243N ContinuanceExisting State laws stayed valid for one year after commencement (24 April 1993); States had to enact conforming laws; most did by 1994
243O Bar on courtsCourts cannot interfere in Panchayat electoral matters; only election petitions per State law
Exempt areas
NMMSHD

Nagaland, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Scheduled and tribal areas, Hill areas of Manipur, Darjeeling: "No More Mischief Since Hills Differ".

7 · Compulsory and voluntary provisions#

Mandatory (binding on all States)

  • Constitutional status of PRIs (Arts. 243-243O)
  • regular elections every five years (Art. 243E)
  • reservation for SC/ST and women (Art. 243D(3), 243T)
  • Gram Sabha in every village (Art. 243A)
  • State Election Commission (Art. 243K)
  • State Finance Commission (Art. 243I)

Voluntary (State discretion)

  • Devolution of powers and responsibilities (Art. 243G) largely at State discretion
  • Gram Panchayat structure, number of members and the intermediate tier
  • merging smaller villages or creating new Panchayats
  • additional reservations for backward classes or minorities beyond SC/ST/women
  • assigning State schemes to PRIs
Six mandatory
CRRGSS

Constitutional status, Regular elections, Reservation, Gram Sabha, SEC, SFC: "Cats Run Round Grassy Slopes Silently".

Devolution is voluntary

Even Art. 243G (devolution of functions) is left largely to States; this is why devolution varies and why the "4Fs" are not realised.

10 · Challenges and way forward for PRIs#

Despite the 73rd Amendment, PRIs face deep structural problems.

Eight challenges
DBTORWPI

Devolution inadequate, Bureaucratic control, Tied funding, Over-dependence on grants, Reluctance to tax, Weak Gram Sabha, Parallel bodies, Infrastructure and digital divide: "Dogs Bark Till Owners Return With Pet Iguanas".

Challenges explained
ChallengeWhat happens
Lack of adequate devolution (the 4Fs)The 3Fs: Functions, Functionaries, Funds, now the 4th F: Functionality. Many States have not transferred them in practice; Panchayats get duties without staff, resources or stable finances; SFC recommendations remain unimplemented
Excessive bureaucratic controlSarpanches and members spend disproportionate time seeking approvals and fund releases from block/district officials; autonomy erodes
Tied and scheme-bound fundingFunds earmarked for centrally or State-sponsored schemes with rigid conditions; one-size-fits-all; misallocation or under-use
Over-dependence on grantsWeakens fiscal accountability and citizen engagement (e.g., lower demand for social audits)
Reluctance to use fiscal powersRepresentatives fear political backlash or social friction, so own-revenue is chronically low
Weak Gram SabhaState laws often do not define powers, procedures or enforcement; Gram Sabhas inactive or ritualistic; marginalised groups lose a forum
Parallel bodiesMission cells, development societies, autonomous agencies bypass PRIs, duplicate functions, concentrate resources, foster elite capture
Poor infrastructure, capacity, digital divideLack of secretaries, buildings, databases, IT; limited training and literacy; only a small fraction of GPs have digital access

11 · Urban local government: evolution#

Urban local government is administration of urban areas by elected representatives; the jurisdiction of a body is an urban area demarcated by the State Government.

Colonial evolution

1688
First municipal corporation at Madras
1726
Extended to Bombay and Calcutta
1870
Lord Mayo's Resolution: local revenue mobilisation and fiscal autonomy
1882
Lord Ripon's Resolution: "Magna Carta of Local Self-Government"; people's participation, representation of Indians, financial decentralisation
1907-1909
Royal Commission on Decentralisation (chair Hobhouse), report 1909: strengthen urban bodies administratively and financially
1919
GoI Act 1919: dyarchy; local self-government a transferred subject under Indian ministers
1924
Cantonments Act: framework for military urban areas
1935
GoI Act 1935: local self-government a provincial subject
Landmarks
MRHDCP

Mayo 1870, Ripon 1882, Hobhouse 1907-09, Dyarchy 1919, Cantonments Act 1924, Provincial subject 1935: "Many Rulers Heard Dull Council Proposals".

Two Magna Cartas

Ripon's 1882 Resolution is the "Magna Carta of Local Self-Government" (colonial). The handout also calls the 1992 constitutional phase the New Magna Carta phase.

12 · The 74th Amendment: Part IX-A#

Culmination of decades of effort; it made municipalities third-tier governments.

Road to the 74th Amendment

1989
Rajiv Gandhi government: 65th Amendment Bill (Nagarpalika Bill); passed Lok Sabha, defeated in Rajya Sabha, lapsed
1990
V.P. Singh (National Front): revised Nagarpalika Bill; lapsed with dissolution of Lok Sabha
1991
P.V. Narasimha Rao: modified Municipalities Bill
74th Amendment Act, 1992
Came into effect 1 June 1993
  • Added Part IX-A, "The Municipalities", Arts. 243P-243ZG.
  • Inserted the Twelfth Schedule with 18 items (Art. 243W).
  • Regular elections, reservation for SCs, STs and women, and devolution of powers and funds; municipalities are justiciable and States must implement.

Twelfth Schedule (18 items)

It states the total as 18.

Handout alert

Only 16 items are named in the paragraph. The count to remember is 18 (the text says "including"). Do not try to force the named list to 18.

Types of urban local government

Eight types
TypeDescription and governance
Municipal Corporation (Mahanagar Palika)Large metropolitan cities; State laws (Parliamentary Acts in UTs). Council (directly elected councillors plus nominated experts) headed by a Mayor (largely ceremonial); Standing Committees (public works, health, education, taxation, finance); Municipal Commissioner (usually IAS) is chief executive
Municipality (Nagar Palika / Municipal Council / Board)Smaller towns; structure like corporations but the Chairperson/President holds real executive power; Standing Committees; Chief Executive Officer manages administration
Notified Area CommitteeFast-developing areas not meeting full municipality criteria; entirely nominated (Chairman and members appointed by the State); works under the State Municipal Act as notified in the Gazette; powers similar to municipalities
Town Area CommitteeSmaller, transitional areas between rural and urban; limited services (roads, drainage, lighting, conservancy); fully elected, fully nominated or mixed as per State law
Cantonment BoardCantonment areas under the Ministry of Defence, regulated by the Cantonments Act, 2006; elected plus nominated/ex-officio members; Station Commander is ex-officio President; obligatory and discretionary civic functions
TownshipCreated by public or industrial enterprises for employees' housing colonies; appointed Town Administrator, no elected members; part of the enterprise's machinery
Port TrustStatutory bodies under Parliamentary Acts in port cities; civic amenities and port operations; elected plus nominated members; Chairman is an official
Special Purpose AgencyState legislation or executive order; single functions (planning, housing, water, sewerage, electricity, transport, pollution control); function-based, independent of territorial ULBs
Eight types
CMNTCTPS

Corporation, Municipality, Notified Area, Town Area, Cantonment, Township, Port Trust, Special purpose: "Cats Make Noisy Tea; Cows Taste Plain Sugar".

Who is elected

Fully nominated: Notified Area Committee. No elected members: Township. Elected plus nominated: Cantonment Board, Port Trust, Corporation council (with nominated experts).

Patterns of municipal government

Bombay pattern

  • Separation of powers: Mayor only ceremonial
  • Municipal Commissioner (bureaucrat) holds executive power
  • his appointment and transfer lie with the State Government

Howrah/Calcutta pattern

  • Mayor holds executive powers, Commissioner works under him
  • stronger democratic accountability, also recommended by the 2nd ARC

17 · The 15th Finance Commission and local bodies#

Local government is the closest tier; it cannot deliver water, sanitation, street lighting or waste management without predictable money. FC-XV made the highest-ever grant to local bodies, partly linked to performance and reforms.

Total grants: Rs 4.36 lakh crore (2021-26)
ComponentAmount
Rural local bodies (Panchayats)Rs 2.4 lakh crore
Urban local bodies (Municipalities)Rs 1.2 lakh crore
Health grants through local governmentsRs 70,051 crore (first-ever dedicated health grants through local bodies)
Handout alert

2.4 + 1.2 + 0.70051 comes to about Rs 4.30 lakh crore, not 4.36. Quote the headline Rs 4.36 lakh crore and the component figures as the handout gives them; the handout also says this is nearly 4.2% of the divisible pool.

Tied versus untied (rural bodies)

Untied (40%)

  • Local priorities and Gram Sabha resolutions: streetlights, small roads, culverts
  • repair of GP buildings, drainage, sanitation
  • local innovations (rainwater pits, village markets, CCTV)
  • community facilities and sports grounds
  • minor emergency responses

Tied (60%)

  • Two sectors: drinking water (under Jal Jeevan Mission: tap connections, groundwater recharge, rainwater harvesting, quality testing, maintenance) and sanitation (to reach ODF+, ODF++: solid waste, liquid/greywater, faecal sludge, village drainage, sanitation workers' support)

Health grants (Rs 70,051 crore)

  • Convert Sub-Centres and PHCs into Health and Wellness Centres (HWCs).
  • Strengthen diagnostic infrastructure for primary healthcare.
  • Block-level public health units.
  • Urban health systems: UPHCs, sub-centres, HWCs.

Entry conditions and release

Entry conditions
APS

Accounts online (provisional and audited, in eGramSwaraj and AuditOnline), Property tax reforms (urban: minimum floor rates, better collection, fewer arrears), SFC constituted on time, report tabled, acted upon: "All Panchayats Submit".

Release mechanism

Entry conditions met
Needed for everything
1st instalment
Released automatically if entry conditions are met
2nd instalment
Depends on utilisation and reporting: at least 50% of the previous instalment used, Grant Transfer Certificates (GTCs) submitted, online accounting through PFMS
Penalty
If a State does not constitute and act on SFCs, no grants to any local body in that State after March 2024

Planning framework

Bottom-up planning

Village Action Plan (VAP)
5-year plan: water source mapping, greywater treatment, infrastructure needs, O&M strategy
Gram Panchayat Development Plan (GPDP)
VAP feeds the GPDP, integrating JJM, SBM-G, MGNREGS, DMF and State schemes

Why it is transformative

  • Largest-ever fund flow to local governments.
  • WASH emphasis: 60% of rural grants tied to drinking water and sanitation.
  • Modernisation: eGramSwaraj, PFMS and AuditOnline make accounts digital and real-time.
  • Autonomy with accountability through tied and untied grants.
  • Health governance decentralised through local bodies.
  • SFCs enforced: grants linked to timely SFC constitution and action; strengthens fiscal federalism.
Conclusion

A decisive shift in decentralisation: record allocation, strict transparency norms, sector priorities and innovative health grants meant to make local governments stronger, more autonomous and more accountable.
