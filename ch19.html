Reform met a religious society: reason plus scripture won laws but not the masses, and the educated few turned reason on the Raj itself.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Reason met a religious society: reformers who joined reason to scripture (Roy, Vidyasagar) won laws but not the masses, because reform was elite, top-down and gradual. The colonial school and press that carried the Doctrine of Reason made the educated 8% who finally turned it on British rule itself.

  1. Europe's rebirth, Bengal's mirror. The Renaissance was Western Europe's 14th-century rebirth, driven by reason and aimed at human welfare, and it set off the Reformation, the end of feudalism, capitalism, the Enlightenment and the Industrial Revolution. 19th-century Bengal looked similar: an oppressive priestly establishment, no modern education, birth-based privilege and poverty. The response was the same too: reach back to the scriptures for what was great and use reason to remove what was not.
    → But why did reform begin in the 19th century, and in Bengal?
  2. The Raj opens the door. After 1813 the British moved from Orientalism to Liberals, Utilitarians and Evangelists, who backed Western education and reform, while Evangelist conversion frightened Indians into reforming from within. The British called ancient India a myth, and cultural nationalism was the reply. Western education (1835) gave the Doctrine of Reason, and Bengal led because the Governor General sat there.
    → Now, who would use that reason, and how?
  3. Logic alone, or logic plus scripture? In India religion determined social practice, and the masses were deeply religious, so a reformer had to reform or reinterpret religion. Derozio's Young Bengal used only logic, ate beef and drank wine, and won a great controversy but poor mass politics. The socio-religious reformers used the Ctrl+F method: find the logic in the scripture and show it to the people.
    → One man did this best: Roy.
  4. Roy, the bridge. Roy, the Father of Modern India, held universalism and found monotheism in the Upanishads; he used the Doctrine of Reason against other-worldliness, Karma and Incarnation. He opposed the Sanskrit College of 1824, wanted Western sciences, and worked with Bentinck 28-35, Macaulay and David Hare. His political demands, shared with Young Bengal, were Separation of Powers, a free Press, Indianisation and Trial by jury.
    → His first great test was Sati.
  5. Sati: the first law. By the 19th century Sati was driven by property, burden, honour and glory, and lower castes in Bengal had begun to copy it through Sanskritisation. Bentinck 28-35 demanded scriptural proof, so in 1828 the Brahmo Samaj beat Radhakanta Deb in debate, and in 1829 Regulation XVII made Sati culpable homicide. Radhakanta Deb's Dharma Sabha appealed to the Privy Council in 1830 and lost.
    → Roy now had a law, and needed a body to carry the work on.
  6. The Brahmo Samaj splits. The Brahmo Samaj (1828) took the low-hanging fruits first and stayed an upper-caste, urban body; after Roy's death in 1833 Debendranath Tagore, the organisation man, held it together. Keshab Chandra Sen wanted a mass, all-India movement and a new religion, so 1866 gave the Brahmo Samaj of India and the Adi Brahmo Samaj; he also drove the Brahmo Marriages Act 1872. In 1878 he married off his minor daughter and the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj split away.
    → Meanwhile Maharashtra took up the work, from the elite and from below.
  7. Maharashtra and the voice from below. The Paramhansa Mandali (1849) copied Young Bengal and failed the same way; the Prarthana Samaj (1867) of Atmaram Pandurang, Ranade, Bhandarkar and Telang was Brahmoism for Maharashtra, with Keshab's help. Ranade also built the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha and the National Social Conference (1887). Phule opened a girls' school in Pune (1848), wrote Gulamgiri and founded the Satyashodhak Samaj (1873): he spoke for the oppressed and organised them.
    → But the hardest customs lay inside the home.
  8. The home front: marriage, age, infants. Child marriage had religious sanction in the Garbhadhan ceremony, so the IPC 1860 set only age 10 and the Rakhmabai case (1884) split opinion. After the death of 11-year-old Phulmoni, Lansdowne 88-94 raised the age to 12 in 1891; Tilak said reform must come from society, Bhandarkar showed the Dharmashastras allow marriage after puberty, and census data showed the practice went on. The Female Infanticide Act 1870 could not reach dowry and hypergamy, nor the nutritional neglect of girls.
    → Vidyasagar took on the widow.
  9. Vidyasagar's war on widowhood. Vidyasagar changed Sanskrit College from within, opened 25 girls' schools in 1857-58, and sent a petition with 1,000 signatures; the Hindu Widow Remarriage Act came on 26 July 1856 and he married his own son to a widow. But a widow lost her husband's property on remarriage, orthodox Hindus won the scripture debate after 1857, and Maharashtra saw only 38 widow remarriages by 1900. Early reform failed because it was elite, top-down, gradual and silent on caste.
    → So a second wave tried the masses, and the faiths answered each in their own way.
  10. Pride, revival and other answers. Dayanand's Arya Samaj (1875) said the Vedas were infallible, rejected idols and rose in Punjab, while Ramakrishna and Vivekananda offered spiritual pride and Vivekananda accepted idols and won the masses. Revivalism had a positive face and a negative one (aggressive Shuddhi, cow protection and communal tension). Sir Syed built Aligarh (1875) on modernisation without surrender of faith, and Narayana Guru consecrated an Ezhava's Shiva at Aruvippuram in 1888.
    → All of this stood on a schoolroom built by British policy.
  11. Schools: from Orientalism to Sargent. Orientalists (Warren Hastings 73-85, Jones, Wellesley 98-05) governed the Indian way until Macaulay's Minute settled the debate in 1835 for a Western syllabus in English, making Brown Sahibs and a downward filtration that never reached the masses. Wood's Despatch 1854 gave the vernacular-to-English ladder, grants-in-aid and three universities (1857), then came Hunter 1882, Curzon's Act 1904, Sadler, Hartog, Wardha 1937 and the unfunded Sargent Plan 1944. By 1921 only about 8% were Western educated and 92% were illiterate.
    → Those few read, and wrote, in the press.
  12. The press and the educated eight per cent. The press began in 1780 with Hicky's Bengal Gazette, shut down in 1782 by Warren Hastings 73-85, and then ran a cycle of repression (1799, 1823, 1878, 1908-10) and concession (Metcalfe 1835, Ripon 80-84's repeal in 1882). Indian-run papers carried reform and then nationalism, and the WEMC's method from 1857 to 1905 was pen and press. The Doctrine of Reason that was meant to make Brown Sahibs ended by making the leaders of the Indian National Movement.

In simple words#

  • The British shift after 1813 (Liberals, Utilitarians, Evangelists), the sting of their criticism (cultural nationalism) and Western education gave Indians the Doctrine of Reason.
  • Because religion determined social practice, the winners were socio-religious reformers who used logic plus scripture (Roy, Vidyasagar)
  • Young Bengal used only logic and failed
  • Laws worked where an act could be seen and proved (Sati 1829, a disabling law) and stalled where society had to change (widow remarriage 1856, child marriage, infanticide).
  • Early reform was elite, upper-caste, top-down and gradual
  • After 1857 came radicals and revivalists (Dayanand, Vivekananda), plus Phule, Sir Syed and Narayana Guru
  • Education moved from Orientalism to Macaulay 1835 (Brown Sahibs), Wood 1854, Hunter, Curzon, Sadler, Hartog, Wardha and Sargent
  • The educated 8% (WEMC) led the INM
  • The press grew from Hicky 1780 through a cycle of repression and concession (Gagging Act 1878, repeal 1882) and carried reform and nationalism.

1 · The "Renaissance" framing#

Story link

Because Europe's rebirth by reason changed religion, society, economy and polity, the Bengal reformers borrowed the same method. Ask: what did the reformers take from the past, and what did they use to fix it?

  • The word Renaissance first named Western Europe's 14th-century intellectual rebirth: a conscious recovery of ancient learning, driven by reason and aimed at human welfare.

19th-century Bengal looked strikingly similar:

  • the same problems:
    • An oppressive priestly establishment misusing social power, no modern education, birth-based privilege and deep poverty
  • the same response: reformers reached back into ancient Indian scriptures to restore what was great, and used reason and rationality to remove what was not.
Handout note
  • The chain of consequences. The European Renaissance triggered a cascade: Reformation in religion, end of Feudalism in society, Capitalism in economy, Enlightenment in polity, and the Scientific and Industrial Revolutions.
  • The Bengal Renaissance had a similar cascading effect: it fed a modern Western Educated Middle Class (WEMC), and from that class came the leaders of the Indian National Movement.
Teacher's board
  • Renaissance = re-birth of society, and it is the mother of Capitalism, Enlightenment and the Humanities.
  • Before it (Lecture 21, slides 35 and 36) he drew Europe in the Dark Ages: education was theology
  • the Church taught "we are born to suffer", no happiness on earth
  • wealth was concentrated and no scope of being rich
  • no democracy
  • Then Venice and Genoa earned cash from trade, new towns in port areas grew, and cash became a source of power (against land).
  • The theme turned to "Happiness of the Human", so each field changed: art (portrait painting), economy (capitalism: security of property, equality of opportunity, "right to be rich"), society (inter-personal relations, rights and obligations in marriage, and so social reforms), polity (fundamental rights, democracy, Enlightenment 17th-18th c), technology (medicine, machines, the Industrial Revolution 1750-1850, voyages of discovery 16th c) and psychology/philosophy.
  • The method of the Renaissance is the "method of R": reason.
Teacher's board
  • The Renaissance method has two steps, "like a UPSC aspirant".
  • Step 1: go back to the golden past (ancient Greek and Roman scriptures), copy-paste it to today's times, and realise it will not work.
  • Step 2: do reason, logic, rationality.
  • So: learn from the past, then find new solutions through the Doctrine of Logic/Reason/Rationality (DoL/R/R).
  • He links this to the Constitution 1950: Scientific Temperament, which is a Fundamental Duty.
  • Why "socio-religious reform" and not simply "social reform"?
  • In India religion did not merely coexist with social practice, it determined it.
  • Sati, child marriage, caste hierarchy, widow remarriage and polygamy were all justified by selective interpretations of religious texts.
  • You could not touch the social practice without confronting the religious sanction behind it.
Teacher's board
  • Socio-religious reformers use Logic AND Religion
  • Pure social reformers use only Logic. Because the masses are deeply religious, a practice rooted in religion can only be changed by reforming or re-interpreting the religion.
  • The Derozians are social reformers, not socio-religious reformers: they used only logic to convince people and the government.
  • That is exactly why they failed (Section 7).

2 · The social issues#

Story link

Because reformers had a method, they listed the evils it must tackle. Ask: which of these were never evil in themselves, only distorted?

  • Reformers identified these major social ills: education, idolatry, priestcraft, child marriage, widow remarriage, female infanticide, polygamy, polytheism and casteism.
Handout alert

A small mismatch. The handout's list has nine ills; the teacher's board (slide 34) draws ten, adding child sacrifice (Sagar Island 1803, Section 6). Learn ten.

  • Not every item is evil in itself.
  • Idol worship helps a worshipper concentrate spiritual energy
  • Not all priests are negative: Ramakrishna Paramhansa himself was a temple priest.
  • Polytheism is inherently pluralistic and so secular in character.
  • Social ills arise when there is no genuine spirituality, when social power is misused, and when society is fragmented by divisions.
  • The problem was never the practice itself but its distortion.

3 · Why social reform began in the 19th century, and why in Bengal#

Story link

Because the evils were old, something new must have started the reform. Ask: what did the British (Liberals, Evangelists, cultural nationalism) and Western education add?

3.1 The role of the British

  • After 1813 the British moved away from Orientalism (which treated Indian culture and learning as worthy of respect and study) towards a new coalition of Liberals, Utilitarians and Evangelists, who actively supported Western education and social reform.
  • The Evangelists also promoted conversion to Christianity, which created fear among Indians for the survival of their religion and pressure to reform from within.
Teacher's board
  • Why the government mattered:
    • The government has L (legislative) and E (executive) power, and financial and military resources (MR)
    • so if the Government wants, social reform becomes possible
  • After 1813 the British criticised Indian civilisation (Chapter 15), and the answer to that criticism was cultural nationalism.
  • After 1857 the British ideology became "Dogs and Indians not allowed".

3.2 Cultural nationalism as a response

  • As the British moved away from Orientalism they argued that the greatness of ancient India was a myth.
  • Indian reformers pushed back with cultural pride: "we will prove you wrong by cleaning up what has gone wrong and recovering what was great." This cultural nationalism ran through all 19th-century reform: mild before 1857, far stronger after.
Teacher's board
  • Colonialism has three faces (Lecture 22, slide 7), political, economic and cultural, and each calls up a nationalism.
  • The cultural nationalism goal:
    • "by studying scripture we will prove that our ancient civilisation [Vedas, Shastras] does not support social ills and supports reason"
    • The ancient past was great
    • In the present, social reform makes Indian society great.
Teacher's board
  • The "Ctrl+F" method. Step 1: identify the logical, ideal practice.
  • Step 2: open the scriptures (like a PDF), press Ctrl+F, search for LOGIC and get "50 results".
  • Step 3: go to the people with those 50 results.
  • The target population is deeply religious, so this has better chances than a pure social reformer's bare argument.
  • (He uses the same picture for Roy on Sati: "choose logic, then Ctrl+F logic in scripture".)

3.3 Western education (1835 onwards)

  • Reformers educated in Western thought met the Doctrine of Reason: no practice, however old or sacred-seeming, is beyond rational scrutiny.
  • It became their primary tool against irrational and inhumane practices.
Teacher's board

The teacher's logic chain (Lecture 21, slide 39; the board also reads "UPSC syllabus minus anything which glorifies India"): shift from Orientalism to Liberalism (1813+), so the government was interested in social reform for the White Man's Burden (WMB); Western education (1835) produced a WEMC motivated by Reason (R) in India, and social reform was the first step; the WEMC became the leaders of social reform.

3.4 Why in Bengal

Teacher's board
  • Why Bengal? (Lecture 21, slide 40) It was the political headquarters (the Governor General sat here), so Western education progressed most here.

5 · Classifying the reformers#

Story link

Because the reformers differed, the teacher sorts them by method, belief and goal. Ask: where does each man sit on the gradual or radical line?

  • Three axes: method, beliefs, goals. (Add the first teacher cut: social versus socio-religious reformers, Section 1.)

5.1 By method

  • No mass movement attempted: Ram Mohan Roy (the teacher: he spoke complex Sanskritised Bangla prose, so he did not even try).
  • Built mass movements: Keshab Chandra Sen (1860s), Swami Dayanand Saraswati (1870s), Swami Vivekananda (1880s).
  • Pure rationality: Derozio and Young Bengal. They failed to create a mass movement because their target people were religious and pure reason without faith did not move them.
  • Rationality plus scripture was more successful: Roy, Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, Dayanand, Vivekananda.
Teacher's board
  • Mass-movement builders use the press AND go to the people (they change hearts, values and attitudes), and are usually radical.

9 · Sati#

Story link

Because Roy had a method, Sati became its great test. Ask: why did this law succeed when others stalled?

9.1 Sati defined, and its origin

  • Sati was the practice by which a widow self-immolated on her husband's funeral pyre.
  • The earliest firm epigraphic evidence is the Eran Pillar Inscription of Bhanugupta (510 CE) at Eran, in Sagar district, Madhya Pradesh: it records the wife of a chieftain named Goparaja performing the act after his death in battle (the handout says out of love and devotion).
  • In Vedic times there was no Sati.
  • The Rig Veda has a passage where a wife goes to the pyre but is called back by her family to the world of the living, and returns.
  • The post-Vedic Smritis supported it as a voluntary spiritual act, a sign of the wife's oneness of soul with her husband.
Teacher's board
  • Ancient times:
    • The Rig Veda opposes Sati
    • The act was completely voluntary, no force, and (the teacher's gloss) it "gradually" split in two: Murder (relatives pressing for property, which he dates to the 1800s) and Suicide (done for self-respect)
  • The teacher also underlined "epigraphic evidence" and Sagar district, Madhya Pradesh (the Eran site).

9.2 Why Sati persisted into the 19th century

  • By the 19th century the original spiritual rationale had largely collapsed.
  • What drove it was social pressure, greed and patriarchy:
  • Relatives did not want to share property with a childless widow
  • A widow was seen as an economic and social burden
  • Sati removed any perceived risk to caste "purity" or family honour from a widow's future conduct;
  • society glorified the act as spiritual salvation, a powerful social pressure.
  • So by the 19th century a "voluntary spiritual act" had become, in most cases, coerced suicide or outright murder, driven by inheritance greed and patriarchal control.

9.3 The scale of the problem

  • Sati was never a pan-India phenomenon. It existed mainly in Bengal among upper castes, and historically in Vijayanagara (1336-1646) and Rajputana, where the related Jauhar (mass self-immolation by women to avoid capture and dishonour by victorious armies) had different origins in warrior-clan honour.
  • What made the 19th century alarming: lower castes in Bengal began practising Sati as part of Sanskritisation.
Teacher's board
  • Sanskritisation (Lecture 22, slide 25): when the lower caste (LC) copies the behaviour of the upper caste (UC). Why?
  • When people perceive that a sub-group has true knowledge of religion and its rituals and practises them, that sub-group gets higher status.
  • So imitating the upper caste is a tool for upward social mobility.
  • On slide 26 his triangle puts UC at the top and LC below: the lower castes were climbing up by copying Sati.

9.4 How Sati was abolished (1829)

Two kinds of law: who decides?
  • Christian missionaries opposed Sati from 1813 but achieved nothing. The decisive campaign was Roy's.
  • Bentinck 28-35 set a firm pre-condition: it had to be proven from Indian scriptures that Sati was not religiously sanctioned.
  • (His Orientalist instinct: he would not override Indian tradition without Indian scriptural authority.)
  • A formal scriptural debate followed: the Brahmo Samaj versus Radhakanta Deb, a prominent orthodox Hindu leader and, otherwise, an education reformer.
  • The teacher dates the debate 1828.
  • Roy won: Sati is murder as per the Shastras.
  • 1829: the Bengal Sati Regulation (Regulation XVII), the 17th law of the year, made Sati punishable as culpable homicide.
  • Radhakanta Deb founded the Dharma Sabha (1830) to oppose abolition and petitioned the Privy Council in London (the Crown's advisory body, here acting as the highest court of appeal for India). The petition was rejected.

13 · Child marriage#

Story link

Because religion itself backed child marriage, it was harder than Sati. Ask: why did the age ladder (10, then 12) change so little on the ground?

13.1 Why child marriage was so hard to uproot

  • Child marriage had explicit religious backing. The Garbhadhan ceremony prescribed marriage before puberty and consummation at puberty.
  • So here reformers were arguing against a practice scripture itself sanctioned, not a distortion.
  • Orthodox opposition was stronger, more principled and harder to beat than on Sati.

"Harder than Sati, therefore religious sanction."

  • Vidyasagar's efforts contributed to the Indian Penal Code 1860 under Canning 56-62: the minimum age of consummation = 10 years (Section 375 IPC 1860, the definition of statutory rape): a modest beginning.
  • Akshay Kumar Dutt added the medical dimension.

14 · Female infanticide, and the Lex Loci Act 1850#

Story link

Because law could stop killing but not hunger, infanticide split into two problems. Ask: where does the State end and society begin?

  • The Lex Loci Act 1850 (also the Caste Disabilities Removal Act 1850, and the handout's timeline also lists a Religious Disabilities Act 1850) protected the property and inheritance rights of converts.

  • Both have evidence: the Act did weaken the orthodox hold on individual choice and made conversion materially safer.

15 · Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820-1891)#

Story link

Because the home was the hardest ground, Vidyasagar fought on every front there. Ask: which of his battles succeeded and which did not?

  • Born Ishwar Chandra Bandyopadhyay on 26 September 1820 in Bengal, he was a prodigy.
  • He studied Sanskrit literature for years, moved to Calcutta young (a widow became his caregiver, which may have shaped his lifelong commitment to widows), passed the Hindu Law Committee examination and earned the title Vidyasagar ("ocean of knowledge") in 1839.
  • He mastered English, Sanskrit and Bengali and used academic prize money to set up a school and print rare Sanskrit texts.
  • His ideology: reason and rationalism are supreme.
  • He said "I have no time to think about God": his energy went to social reform. He rejected other-worldliness.

15.1 The educator

  • As Additional Secretary and then Principal of Sanskrit College (Calcutta, 1824), he threatened resignation if lower-caste students were not admitted and introduced Western sciences alongside Sanskrit.
  • (The college Roy had opposed; Vidyasagar changed it from inside.) He wanted Western education and culture delivered in the vernacular, not only English, so that knowledge went beyond the elite.
  • As Inspector of Schools in Bengal he set up 20 Model Schools and ended physical punishment.
  • (Inspector = district level; the provincial head was the Director of Education, like SP and DGP in the police.)
  • Female education is "the only true route to uplifting society".
  • Between November 1857 and May 1858, in the middle of the Revolt, he opened 25 schools for girls with 1,300 girls enrolled at a time
  • attendance, books and transport free
  • He improved Sanskrit literature and made it accessible through Bengali.
  • Tagore said his greatest contribution was to the Bengali language: the Father of Modern Bengali Prose.

16 · Widow remarriage after the Act: why it failed#

Story link

Because an enabling law leaves the choice to society, the 1856 Act stalled. Ask: what did the widow lose, and who won the scripture debate?

  • The Hindu Widow Remarriage Act 1856 was a landmark that did not solve the problem.
  • The catch: on remarriage a widow lost her right to her deceased husband's property, enough to keep the law largely ineffective.
  • Reform was scattered and always met organised orthodox resistance:
  • Maharashtra:
    • Vishnu Shastri Pandit formed a widow-remarriage society in 1866
    • orthodox rivals at once set up a counter-organisation.
  • Phule worked among lower castes in Maharashtra for widow remarriage but failed.
  • Madras: Veeresalingam Pantulu set up a society.
  • Pandita Ramabai was a rare exception:
    • A Brahmin woman who stayed unmarried long, finally married a man of a lower caste
    • after his death she went to England to study medicine, converted to Christianity (criticised even by reformers), and in the USA raised money for a widow home in Bombay.
  • She opposed the new role model of the "educated but obedient wife".
  • The result was failure. Post-1857 orthodox Hindus had stronger social support and won the scripture-based debate, proving "to most people's satisfaction" that Hinduism did not allow widow remarriage.
  • The damage spread downward: even lower castes, who had traditionally allowed widow remarriage, stopped it to align with upper-caste norms (the handout: "Sanskritisation working in reverse", adopting upper-caste restrictions, not only customs).
  • By 1900, only 38 widow remarriages had taken place in Maharashtra.
  • The reform worked only in Haryana, where it was already accepted.

18 · The Arya Samaj (1875)#

Story link

Because the early reformers did not reach the masses, a second wave came after 1857. Ask: how is the Arya Samaj different from the Brahmo Samaj?

18.1 Swami Dayanand Saraswati

  • His original name was Mool Shankar, from Gujarat. He never married.
  • He left home and spent 20 years travelling in search of spiritual truth.
  • His journey ended in Mathura, where Swami Virajananda directed him to learn directly from the Vedas.
  • Virajananda gave him a task, to spread Vedic knowledge, and a new name: Rishi Dayanand (later Maharishi).

18.2 His beliefs and Satyarth Prakash

  • His foundation:
    • The Vedas are the ultimate, infallible source of all true knowledge
    • everything after (rituals, idol worship, caste hierarchy, priestly domination) was corruption
  • The answer was Back to the Vedas, not Western modernity.
  • He wrote it out in Satyarth Prakash (The Light of Meaning of Truth, 1875), deliberately in Hindi, not Sanskrit, to reach the masses.
  • It is the foundational text of the Arya Samaj.
  • It attacks idol worship, polytheism, animal sacrifice, untouchability, child marriage and birth-based caste
  • it also critically examines Christianity, Islam, Jainism and Buddhism, arguing that Vedic Hinduism is superior to all.
  • He stressed the Doctrine of Reason, with the claim that the Vedas alone contain scientific truths, so Hinduism is not backward, it is ahead.
Teacher's board
  • Beside the title: "ARYA Samaj 1875". Beside "Vedic Hinduism is superior to all": "[BUT on ground, no communalism)" (on the ground the Arya Samaj, he says, did not begin as communal).
  • On the margin of the Vivekananda slides he contrasts "vs SDS" (Dayanand): Hinduism = superior, and idols and rituals rejected.

18.3 Founded 10 April 1875, Bombay

  • The Arya Samaj ("Society of the Nobles") was founded on 10 April 1875 in Bombay (the teacher underlined Bombay).
  • Motto: "Krinvanto Vishvam Aryam", "Make this world noble." Its ten tenets cover God, the Vedas, dharma, truth, righteous conduct, physical and social good, love and justice, knowledge over ignorance, collective welfare and individual freedom within social rules.
Teacher's board

"Not imp" (Lecture 23, slide 22) beside the ten tenets.

18.4 What the Arya Samaj did

  • Attacked ills from within the Vedic framework:
    • Idolatry, polytheism, priestcraft, rituals, birth-based caste, child marriage
    • Supported widow remarriage, inter-caste marriage and female education.
  • Caste:
    • Discarded birth-based caste and denounced untouchability
    • upheld the Varnashram system by profession, not birth: Brahmans scholars, Kshatriyas protectors, Vaishyas traders, Shudras in service, changeable with profession, not hereditary, not hierarchical.
  • (Dayanand proposed a meritocratic alternative, not a defence of caste.)
  • Women:
    • "an educated man needs an educated wife"
    • Widow-remarriage programmes in the 1880s.
  • Shuddhi: bringing back those converted (voluntarily or not) to Islam or Christianity.
  • Under Dayanand it was for those who came forward, not a forced reconversion.
  • Gurukuls to teach Vedic knowledge and train spreaders.
  • Projected Hinduism as a "religion of the book" like Islam and Christianity: the Vedas as Shruti (that which is heard) and Apaurusheya (not of human origin, eternal cosmic truths).
  • Swarajya: a country free of foreign influence, an aggressive, self-assertive answer to British criticism.
Teacher's board

On the Swarajya slide he wrote "CULTURE" above the table with a curved line to "free of foreign influence": Dayanand's swarajya is cultural (freedom from foreign cultural influence), not yet political.

18.5 Successes and failures

  • The Arya Samaj failed in east and west India (the teacher: competition from the Brahmo Samaj of India 1866 in Bengal and the Prarthana Samaj 1867 in Maharashtra) but succeeded most in Punjab and the North-West Provinces, especially after Dayanand's death in 1883, when it became both more popular and more aggressive.
  • Why Punjab: late-19th-century Punjab was a region of intense religious competition (Hinduism, Sikhism, Islam, Christian missionaries), and the Samaj's assertive, organised, scriptural approach suited it.
  • After his death the disciples set up the Dayanand Anglo-Vedic (DAV) College Trust and Management Society: the first DAV High School at Lahore on 1 June 1886, Lala Hans Raj headmaster (Shaheed Bhagat Singh studied at DAV School, Lahore).
  • The teacher: the DAV is Western education plus the Vedas.
  • Though never directly in the independence struggle, Dayanand's teachings influenced Lala Lajpat Rai, V.D.
  • Savarkar, Madam Cama, Ram Prasad Bismil, M.G.
  • Ranade, Madan Lal Dhingra and Subhas Chandra Bose (the handout's list).

20 · Ramakrishna, Vivekananda and Sister Nivedita#

Story link

Because the educated found their jobs hollow, Ramakrishna offered undiluted tradition. Ask: why did Vivekananda win more mass acceptance than Dayanand?

20.1 Ramakrishna Paramhansa

  • A temple priest, not a Western-educated reformer, writer or debater.
  • He believed in meditation, renunciation and monotheism
  • Western ideas and education left him untouched.
  • He did not criticise colonial rule, but his pure focus on Hinduism was an implicit rejection of Western values and culture.
  • His appeal to educated Bengalis is revealing.
  • They held clerical jobs under British administration, which he called Chakri (the grinding routine of office work), and found them spiritually hollow.
  • Reformism by the 1870s felt like an admission that Indians were uncivilised.
  • Ramakrishna offered not reform, not self-criticism, but the spiritual richness of their own tradition, undiluted.
  • He argued that Chakri left no time for spiritualism, and that this absence was the real source of the educated middle class's dissatisfaction.

20.2 Swami Vivekananda

Dayanand against Vivekananda: the idol test
  • Ramakrishna's most famous disciple, a different kind of figure: intellectual, activist, globally engaged.
  • Universalism, genuine: a Christian could become a better Christian by studying Vedanta.
  • No conversion, no aggression: spread the values, not the label.
  • The teacher:
    • Vs SDS (Dayanand): Dayanand held that Hinduism was superior
    • Vivekananda believed in the superiority of Hindu philosophy but not as a negative revivalist
  • On the Upanishads: "Spirit = curiosity = Q and A": the Doctrine of Reason.
  • He had faith in the glory of Hindu civilisation but admitted degeneration in recent times, without blaming a particular religion.
  • "Service of Jiva is worship of Shiva":
    • Social service is spiritual
    • He popularised Daridra Narayan (God in the poor)
    • He thought existing reform too elitist
    • Real work meant going to the masses through service
  • Ramakrishna Mission (1897):
    • Two aims: spread Vedanta and undertake social service
    • An army of followers carrying the message of Vedanta, the Upanishads, Buddha and Jesus.
  • Also akhadas and gymnasiums:
    • Indian masculinity of physical strength and mental spirituality is superior to the Western
    • Indians were not "feminine", differently strong.
  • Idols and rituals: he accepted them as legitimate aids to religious feeling (the teacher again: vs SDS), which gave him far greater mass acceptance.
  • 1893:
    • Parliament of Religions, Chicago: Western materialism and Indian spiritualism must come together
    • He became internationally famous
  • No political message, nationalism purely cultural
  • Netaji Bose called him the spiritual father of the Indian National Movement in Bengal.
  • After his death (1902) revivalists selectively used him: Vedic supremacy, some rituals, Hindu glory, and recast "recent degeneration" as degeneration under Muslim and Christian rule.
  • Today the Mission runs schools, dispensaries, hospitals and relief work.
  • Sister Nivedita (Margaret Noble), Vivekananda's Irish disciple: nurtured cultural nationalism
  • Worked for women's education (the Nivedita School at Calcutta, 1898)
  • Saw independence as a spiritual necessity
  • gave secret support and guidance to revolutionary groups in Bengal, especially the Anushilan Samiti, bridging spiritual revival and militancy
  • Wrote The Master as I Saw Him (1910)
  • lived in India until her death in 1911.

24.5 William Adam's reports on vernacular education (1835-38)

Handout note

Adam's three reports are the most critical documentary evidence of indigenous education before Macaulay's Minute 1835 and Wood's Despatch 1854 dismantled it.

  • Context. Bentinck 28-35 appointed William Adam (a Scottish missionary who had co-founded the Calcutta Unitarian Committee with Roy in 1821) to survey indigenous education in Bengal and Bihar.
  • The three reports (1835-38) fall in the same window in which Macaulay's Minute settled the debate
  • They describe the system that policy then bypassed.

Findings.

  • Massive scale: over 1,00,000 pathshalas in Bengal and Bihar alone, almost every village.
  • It contradicts the colonial claim that "India was illiterate before the British."
  • Caste inclusivity:
    • Children of all castes (except the lowest or untouchables) often sat together
    • Poor agrarian families could attend
  • Trap: "Adam concluded the indigenous system was rigid and excluded lower castes" is false.
  • Extreme flexibility (most often tested):
    • No fixed fee (paid in kind, rice or grain, or cash by ability)
    • No printed books or fixed syllabus (oral instruction, slate practice, local texts)
    • No separate buildings (banyan trees, temples, mosques, the teacher's home)
    • No annual exams or timetables (continuous personal assessment)
    • Suspended during harvest.
  • Curriculum: strictly functional: basic literacy, arithmetic, accounts for daily agrarian and commercial life, exactly what Macaulay dismissed as "useless".
  • Impact. India had no "education vacuum" waiting to be filled
  • A functioning mass-scale vernacular system existed, starved of funds and destroyed by the shift to English-medium administrative education
  • (Wood's Despatch later proposed "vernacular primary schools" as a State innovation, which is hollow against Adam; its real innovation was grants-in-aid and an inspectorate.)

24.17 The overall impact of Western education

From Western education to the INM

By 1921: 92% illiteracy, and only 2% of women literate.

  • Failures:
    • No mass infrastructure ever built
    • Education stayed costly
    • vernacular education suffered after English became compulsory for jobs (1844)
    • An upper-caste and British officials nexus worked against education for backward communities
    • Female education neglected outside the three presidency towns

Unintended consequences:

  • The Doctrine of Reason led Indians to assess their own practices critically, which drove the social reform movements.
  • A culture of public debate through the press created civil society.
  • A passion for Western sciences among educated Indians (Roy and others saw it as the only route to modernisation).
  • Most consequential: the WEMC turned the tools of Western political thought (liberty, rights, representation, nationalism) against British rule itself.

25.1 How the press began (1556 to 1799)

  • 1556: the printing press reached India: brought by the Portuguese to Goa, operated by Joao de Bustamante, at first for religious texts.
  • It became a tool of political and social debate only two centuries later.
  • 1780:
    • Bengal Gazette (English), Calcutta, by James Augustus Hicky: India's first newspaper
    • shut down in 1782 by Warren Hastings 73-85 for criticising the British
  • 1784: Calcutta Gazette, Francis Gladwin, with EIC patronage: semi-official.
  • 1789: Bombay Herald, William Ashburner: the first Bombay paper (renamed Bombay Gazette, 1791).
  • 1790:
    • Bombay Courier, Luke Ashburner (William's brother)
    • Later merged with The Times of India.
  • 1799: Censorship Press Act by Wellesley 98-05 (wartime pre-censorship).
