1 · Speaker OF THE LOK Sabha · Powers · Independence · “ONCE A Speaker, Always A Speaker” · Reforms#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 20-22. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The Speaker presides over the Lok Sabha, decides if a Bill is a Money Bill, rules on anti-defection disqualification and guards members’ privileges. Safeguards protect the office, but real neutrality is doubted because the Speaker usually stays in a party and decides disqualification cases. Reforms include bipartisan selection, an independent tribunal for anti-defection and the convention “Once a Speaker, always a Speaker”.

Powers AND Functions OF THE Speaker

1. Presiding and disciplinary powers

  • Presides over the Lok Sabha and over joint sittings of Parliament (Article 108).
  • Maintains order and decorum and can direct disorderly members to withdraw or take action under House rules.
  • Decides points of order and interprets procedural rules and precedents.
  • Can adjourn or suspend a sitting in grave disorder or when there is no quorum (one-tenth of total membership).

2. Legislative and procedural powers

  • Decides the admissibility of questions, motions and resolutions.
  • Regulates debates, decides who speaks and for how long.
  • Can expunge objectionable remarks or unparliamentary language from House records.
  • Can allow a secret sitting under the rules.

3. Money Bill certification (Article 110)

  • The Speaker decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill. Article 110(3) says the Speaker’s decision is final.
  • But certification is subject to judicial review where a constitutional violation is alleged. In Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank (2019) the Supreme Court recognised that Money Bill certification is not completely immune from judicial review.

4. Anti-defection powers (Tenth Schedule)

  • The Speaker decides disqualification of Lok Sabha members on grounds of defection.
  • In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) the Supreme Court held that the Speaker’s decisions under the Tenth Schedule are subject to judicial review.

5. Committees and privileges

  • Guardian of the rights and privileges of the Lok Sabha, its committees and members.
  • Appoints chairpersons of several Lok Sabha committees and supervises them.
  • Chairs the Business Advisory Committee, Rules Committee and General Purposes Committee.
  • Ex-officio President of the Indian Parliamentary Group and chairs the Conference of Presiding Officers of Legislative Bodies in India.

6. Voting (Article 100)

  • Does not vote in the first instance in ordinary proceedings. Uses a casting vote in case of a tie. This helps keep the Chair neutral.

Independence AND Impartiality

Safeguards — the Speaker is expected to stay apart from daily party politics and protect all members whatever their party.

  • 1. Security of tenure: removal needs an effective majority and 14 days’ notice, protecting against sudden removal.
  • 2. Financial independence: salary and allowances are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India and are not voted annually.
  • 3. Limited judicial interference:
    • Under Article 122, courts cannot question parliamentary proceedings for mere procedural irregularity
    • Constitutional violations remain reviewable
  • 4. Casting vote only on a tie: supports neutrality.
  • 5. Anti-defection exemption: under the Tenth Schedule the Speaker may voluntarily give up party membership on election to the office without being disqualified, if conditions are met.
  • 6. High status: 7th rank in the Table of Precedence, alongside the Chief Justice of India.
  • 7. Parliamentary protection: the Speaker’s conduct is not normally discussed in the House except on a substantive motion.

Challenges to independence

  • Political affiliation: the Speaker generally keeps party membership, raising neutrality concerns.
  • Anti-defection decisions: delays and alleged partisan decisions raise doubts about impartiality.
  • Money Bill certification: disputes have raised concerns about the Rajya Sabha’s rights.
  • Procedural discretion: decisions on admission of motions, debate and discipline can look pro-government.
  • Dependence on a majority: pressure from a ruling party with a strong majority.

“ONCE A Speaker, Always A Speaker”

Meaning and origin

  • A person elected Speaker should stay politically neutral even after the term ends. In the UK the Speaker resigns from party affiliation and generally gets re-elected without opposition from major parties.
  • Indian precedent: Vithalbhai Patel (1925) resigned from the Swaraj Party after becoming President of the Central Legislative Assembly and later contested as an independent.
  • V. S. Page Committee (1967) recommended conventions to strengthen neutrality: the Speaker should sever party connections
  • Parties should seek consensus on keeping an impartial Speaker
  • Major parties should avoid contesting the Speaker’s constituency where such consensus exists
  • An elected Speaker should not seek ministerial or other political executive office at the same time
Arguments in favourChallenges
Ensures impartiality: less dependence on the ruling party, fair treatment of all MPsCareer disincentive: permanent political detachment may discourage capable politicians from taking the post
Protects parliamentary democracy: Speaker guards both government and oppositionWeak conventions: unlike the UK, neutrality conventions are not firmly established in India
Better anti-defection decisions: detachment reduces claims of partisan rulingsLimited accountability: permanent detachment may reduce political accountability
Institutional credibility: public confidence in the dignity of the ChairIncomplete reform: quitting a party alone may not ensure neutrality without changes to anti-defection adjudication and procedures
Note

Example (2025): the Supreme Court directed the Telangana Assembly Speaker to decide pending disqualification petitions against defecting MLAs within a specified period, highlighting concern over delays in anti-defection decisions. (verify)

Reforms and way forward

  • Bipartisan selection: consultation between ruling party and Opposition before choosing the Speaker.
  • Independent anti-defection adjudication: consider moving the power from the Speaker to an independent tribunal or other impartial authority.
  • Strengthen neutrality conventions such as “Once a Speaker, always a Speaker”, with safeguards against an immediate return to partisan office.
  • Better procedures: transparent and consistent decisions on motions, debates, Money Bills and discipline.
  • Capacity building: training in constitutional law, parliamentary procedure and impartial decision-making.

Quote

G. V. Mavalankar: “Once a person is elected Speaker, he is expected to be above parties, above politics. In other words, he belongs to all the members or belongs to none. He holds the scales of justice evenly, irrespective of party or person.”

2 · Parliamentary Privileges · Article 105 · Collective AND Individual · Breach vs Contempt · Judgments · Codification#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 22-24. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Parliamentary privileges are special rights and immunities of the Houses, committees and members (Article 105 for Parliament; Article 194 for State legislatures). They protect free functioning, not personal comfort. They are not codified. The Supreme Court has held that they are not beyond judicial review (Raja Ram Pal, 2007) and that MPs cannot claim immunity for bribery linked to speeches or votes (Sita Soren, 2024).

WHAT ARE Privileges?

Meaning

  • Special rights, immunities and exemptions enjoyed by the two Houses of Parliament, their committees and their members. They protect the independence, dignity and effective working of Parliament and shield members from outside interference.
  • Article 105: privileges at the Union level. Article 194: similar privileges for State Legislatures.
  • They also extend to persons who are entitled to take part in parliamentary proceedings, such as Union Ministers and the Attorney General.
  • They do not extend to the President, even though the President is an integral part of Parliament.

Sources of privileges

  • Laws: Parliament may define privileges by law, but no comprehensive law codifying all privileges has been made.
  • Rules of procedure governing business and privilege matters.
  • Parliamentary conventions (established practices).
  • Judicial interpretations: Supreme Court judgments on scope and limits.

Collective Privileges (OF EACH House)

Eight collective privileges

  • 1. Publication of proceedings: each House can publish and regulate its proceedings. Article 361A protects substantially true reports, except those of secret sittings, subject to conditions.
  • 2. Secret sittings: each House can exclude strangers and hold secret proceedings.
  • 3. Internal regulation: can frame rules and regulate its own proceedings.
  • 4. Punishment for contempt: can punish members and outsiders by reprimand, admonition, suspension, imprisonment or expulsion, as applicable.
  • 5. Information on arrest: the House must be told about the arrest, detention, conviction or imprisonment of its members.
  • 6. Summoning powers: committees can summon witnesses and demand documents, subject to rules.
  • 7. Judicial protection (Article 122): courts cannot question proceedings for mere procedural irregularity, but constitutional violations are reviewable.
  • 8. Protection within the precincts: arrest or service of legal process inside parliamentary precincts needs the Presiding Officer’s permission, subject to rules.

Individual Privileges (OF Members)

Four individual privileges

  • 1. Freedom of speech (Article 105): immunity from court proceedings for speeches and votes in Parliament, subject to constitutional limits.
  • 2. Freedom from civil arrest: during sessions and 40 days before and after. It does not apply to criminal cases or preventive detention.
  • 3. Exemption from court attendance: members are generally not compelled to appear as witnesses during sessions, subject to rules.
  • 4. Exemption from jury service: traditionally exempt, though jury trials are no longer part of India’s ordinary criminal justice system.
Value addition

Nature: privileges are functional protections, not personal privileges. They are also called the “indirect rights of citizens” because they let MPs represent the people freely.

Breach OF Privilege vs Contempt OF THE House

Value addition

Every breach of privilege may amount to contempt, but every contempt need not involve breach of a specific privilege.

Procedure for breach of privilege

  • 1. Notice: a member raises a question of privilege with the permission of the Speaker or Chairman.
  • 2. Preliminary examination: the Presiding Officer checks whether a prima facie case exists.
  • 3. Committee of Privileges: the matter may be sent to the Committee to investigate and recommend.
  • 4. Decision: the House may accept, modify or reject the Committee’s recommendations and decide the action.

Important Supreme Court Judgments

CaseBackground and holding
Raja Ram Pal v. Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007)MPs were expelled after the Cash-for-Query scandal (2005). The Supreme Court upheld Parliament’s power to expel members for misconduct under its privilege jurisdiction, but held that privileges are not completely immune from judicial review: courts can examine constitutional violations and substantive illegality, not mere procedural irregularities.
Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024)A seven-judge Constitution Bench ruled that MPs and MLAs cannot claim immunity from prosecution for bribery linked to their speeches or votes. It overruled P. V. Narasimha Rao v. State (1998). Principle: privileges protect legislative independence, not corruption or criminal misconduct.

Significance of privileges

  • Legislative independence from undue influence by the executive, judiciary and outside agencies.
  • Freedom of representation: MPs can raise public concerns without fear of legal harassment for protected parliamentary speech.
  • Institutional dignity and authority of Parliament.
  • Effective oversight: supports debates, inquiries and scrutiny of the government.

Codification Debate

Arguments for codificationArguments against codification
Legal clarity: removes doubt about the scope of privilegesLoss of flexibility: impossible to define every breach or contempt
Prevents misuse: clear limits on parliamentary punishmentPolitical influence: the ruling majority might shape privileges to its interest
Judicial harmony: fewer clashes between legislature and judiciaryMore litigation: codified provisions may draw more legal challenges
Protects Fundamental Rights: clear safeguards against arbitrary actionReduced autonomy: may restrict the House’s ability to handle new situations

Key concerns: undefined powers (breach vs contempt); uncertain limits of imprisonment, suspension and expulsion; balance with Articles 14, 19 and 21; distinction between internal procedure and constitutional violations.

  • Way forward:
    • Selective codification of major privileges and punishments while keeping procedural flexibility
    • Due process (fair hearing, reasoned decisions, proportionate punishment)
    • Judicial balance
    • Transparent and impartial Committees of Privileges
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: privileges are essential for legislative independence and democratic accountability, but must stay consistent with constitutional supremacy, fundamental rights and the rule of law.

3 · Bicameralism · Rajya Sabha vs LOK Sabha · Powers · Significance · Limitations#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 24-28. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Parliament has the President, Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha (Article 79). India chose two Houses for federal voice, deliberation, expertise and continuity. Rajya Sabha is equal to the Lok Sabha on ordinary Bills and constitutional amendments, weaker on Money Bills and government accountability, and has special powers (Articles 249 and 312, VP removal). It is neither as weak as the House of Lords nor as strong as the US Senate.

Constitutional Framework AND Rationale

Structure

  • Article 79: Parliament = the President + Rajya Sabha (Council of States) + Lok Sabha (House of the People).
  • The President is not a member of either House but is part of Parliament. He summons and prorogues, dissolves the Lok Sabha, addresses Parliament and gives assent to Bills.

Why India adopted bicameralism

  • Federal representation: the Rajya Sabha gives States a voice at the national level.
  • Better deliberation: a second chamber reconsiders what the Lok Sabha passes and reduces the risk of hasty or ill-considered laws.
  • Diverse representation: experts come in through Presidential nomination, State representatives through indirect election.
  • Continuity: the Rajya Sabha never dissolves, giving institutional memory even when the Lok Sabha is dissolved.
  • Constitutional safeguard: a second chamber helps stop unilateral constitutional change by a Lok Sabha majority.

Arguments against bicameralism

  • Delay: Bills need approval from both Houses.
  • Duplication: both Houses discuss the same issues, increasing time and cost.
  • Indirect election: Rajya Sabha members are not directly elected, which raises questions of accountability.
  • Weak federal role: members often vote on party lines instead of defending State interests.
  • Political accommodation: the Upper House may be used to accommodate defeated politicians or loyalists.
Value addition

Bicameralism adds a layer of deliberation, and the Constitution gives tools to settle disagreements: Joint Sitting (Article 108) and the Money Bill procedure.

Position AND Powers OF THE Rajya Sabha

A. Areas of parity (equal powers)

  • 1. Ordinary legislation: equal power to introduce, amend and pass ordinary Bills, subject to the joint sitting mechanism.
  • 2. Constitutional amendments (Article 368):
    • Both Houses must separately pass the Bill by the special majority
    • No joint sitting is available
  • 3. Election and removal of authorities:
    • Takes part in the election and impeachment of the President
    • The election and removal of the Vice-President (the removal resolution starts in the Rajya Sabha)
    • Parliamentary removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges, CAG and Chief Election Commissioner, as per their provisions
  • 4. Ordinances and Emergencies: both Houses ordinarily approve ordinances (Article 123) and Emergency proclamations (Articles 352, 356, 360).
  • 5. Other:
    • Discusses reports of CAG, UPSC, Finance Commission and other constitutional bodies
    • Takes part in legislation on the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and other Union institutions
    • Ministers including the PM can belong to either House

B. Areas of inferior powers

  • 1. Money Bills (Articles 109-110):
    • Introduced only in the Lok Sabha. The Rajya Sabha can only recommend amendments within 14 days
    • The Lok Sabha may accept or reject them. If the Rajya Sabha does not return the Bill within 14 days it is deemed passed in the form passed by the Lok Sabha
  • 2. Financial control: can discuss the Budget but cannot vote on Demands for Grants.
  • 3. Executive accountability:
    • Under Article 75(3) the Council of Ministers is responsible only to the Lok Sabha
    • A no-confidence motion cannot be moved in the Rajya Sabha
  • 4. Joint sitting (Article 108): the Lok Sabha’s numerical strength usually prevails. Example: the Prevention of Terrorism Bill, 2002 was passed in a joint sitting.

C. Special powers exclusive to the Rajya Sabha

  • 1. Legislation on a State List subject (Article 249): the Rajya Sabha can authorise Parliament to legislate on a State List subject in the national interest, by a resolution backed by two-thirds of members present and voting. Valid for one year, renewable one year at a time. Laws made this way lapse six months after the resolution ceases, except for things already done.
  • 2. Creating All-India Services (Article 312): by a two-thirds majority of members present and voting. Existing: IAS, IPS and Indian Forest Service.
  • 3. Removal of the Vice-President (Article 67):
    • The resolution can begin only in the Rajya Sabha
    • It needs a majority of all the then members and the Lok Sabha’s agreement
  • 4. Emergency proclamations: if the Lok Sabha is dissolved, the Rajya Sabha may approve proclamations under Articles 352, 356 and 360, ensuring continuity until the new Lok Sabha considers it within the set period.

Significance AND Limitations OF THE Rajya Sabha

Significance

  • House of Elders: senior statesmen, experienced politicians and experts can contribute.
  • Revisory chamber: prevents hasty or ill-considered laws and promotes informed debate.
  • Guardian of the Constitution: helps prevent constitutional change based only on a Lok Sabha majority.
  • Federal chamber: allows States to take part in Union decisions.
  • Intellectual representation: 12 nominated members with special knowledge in literature, science, art and social service.
  • It is sometimes called the “House of Corrections”, and the Lok Sabha the “House of Passions”.

Limitations and criticism

  • 1. Weak federal character:
    • States are not equally represented (unlike the US Senate)
    • The 2003 removal of the domicile requirement means a Rajya Sabha member from a State need not live there
    • Representation of Union Territories and nominated members adds non-federal features
  • 2. Limited financial powers: weak control over Money Bills. Controversies over Money Bill certification, including the Aadhaar legislation, raised fears of bypassing the Upper House.
  • 3. Doubtful effectiveness as a revisory chamber:
    • Parliament has held only three joint sittings
    • The Rajya Sabha could not stop major constitutional changes like the 42nd Amendment (1976)
  • 4. Political accommodation:
    • Critics say it becomes a platform for defeated politicians, retired bureaucrats and loyalists
    • Nominating former constitutional functionaries has raised concerns about independence
  • 5. Declining quality of debate: frequent disruptions and walkouts. Vice-President Hamid Ansari in 2013 used the expression “federation of anarchists”.

Why are States unequally represented?

  • Population differences: Indian States differ greatly in population and size.
  • Nature of the federation: India was not formed by an agreement among previously independent States, unlike the USA.
  • Small-State dominance: equal representation would give small States disproportionate influence over national law.
Way forward / key point

Overall position: the Rajya Sabha is neither as weak as the British House of Lords nor as powerful as the US Senate. Its worth lies in combining federal representation, legislative review and institutional continuity.

4 · Women IN Parliament · 106th Amendment · 131st Amendment BILL 2026 · Delimitation Issues#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 28-30. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Women are about half of India’s population but only about 14% of the Lok Sabha. The Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (106th Amendment, 2023) promises one-third reservation, but only after the Census and delimitation. The 131st Amendment Bill (16 April 2026) tried to expand the Lok Sabha and speed this up but was defeated. The delimitation debate is mainly about South vs North, federal balance and fairness.

Women’s Representation IN Parliament

Where we stand — women are nearly half the population but have limited representation.

  • 15th Lok Sabha: around 11% women MPs.
  • 16th Lok Sabha: around 11.4%.
  • 17th Lok Sabha: 78 women MPs, about 14%.
  • 18th Lok Sabha (2024): 74 women MPs, about 14% at the time of election.
  1. Constituent Assembly
    Reservation for women was debated, but many women members preferred social and economic empowerment over legislative quotas.
  2. 1996, 1998, 1999
    Women’s Reservation Bills were introduced but could not be enacted.
  3. 2008
    The 108th Constitutional Amendment Bill, passed by the Rajya Sabha in 2010, lapsed with the dissolution of the 15th Lok Sabha.
  4. 2023
    The 106th Constitutional Amendment Act brought in one-third reservation for women.

NARI Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, 2023 (106th Amendment)

Key provisions

  • One-third reservation: 33% in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies and the Delhi Legislative Assembly.
  • Constitutional changes: inserted Articles 330A, 332A and 334A and amended Article 239AA.
  • SC/ST women: one-third of the seats reserved for SCs and STs go to women from those communities.
  • Implementation: takes effect after the required Census figures are published and delimitation is done.
  • Rotation and duration: seats rotate after later delimitation exercises. A 15-year period is provided, subject to Parliament’s power to decide continuation.
  • Exclusion: does not apply to the Rajya Sabha or State Legislative Councils.
Arguments in favourConcerns and challenges
Political equality: corrects the historical under-representation of womenProxy representation: male relatives may control elected women
Policy priorities: women-led Panchayats give more attention to water supply, sanitation and local servicesRotation of seats: weakens constituency continuity and long-term political investment
Leadership development: encourages women’s participation in parties and decision-makingTokenism: a quota alone may not fix patriarchal structures and party-level discrimination
Inclusive governance: more diverse perspectives in debates and policyDelayed implementation: dependence on Census and delimitation creates uncertainty

Alternative reform proposals

  • Party ticket quotas: parties must give at least one-third of tickets to women (through electoral law). Limit: parties may field women in seats where their chance of winning is low.
  • Dual-member constituencies: some constituencies elect one man and one woman. Limit: needs big changes to the electoral system and may create unequal influence between the two representatives.
  • Long-term empowerment:
    • Women’s education, financial independence, safety and political training
    • Internal party democracy
    • Less criminalisation of politics

Constitution (131st Amendment) BILL, 2026

What was introduced

  • Three connected Bills were introduced in the Lok Sabha on 16 April 2026: the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, the Delimitation Bill and the Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill.
  • Expansion of the Lok Sabha: raise its maximum strength from 550 to 850, with up to 815 from States and 35 from Union Territories.
  • Population-based delimitation: reallocate seats so constituencies across States have roughly equal populations.
  • Census basis:
    • Let Parliament decide by law which Census is used for delimitation
    • The accompanying Bill would have allowed use of the 2011 Census
  • Women’s reservation: remove the need to wait for the first Census after the 106th Amendment Act, 2023, allowing earlier implementation.
Note

Outcome: the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill was defeated in the Lok Sabha on 17 April 2026; the two connected Bills became infructuous. (current affairs; verify the date and numbers)

Issues Related TO Delimitation

Six issues

  • 1. North-South federal imbalance. Southern States (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh) slowed population growth through health, education and family planning
  • Northern States (Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh) grew faster. A strictly population-based redistribution would raise Northern States’ share and cut Southern States’ political weight
  • 2. Reduced constitutional certainty. The proposed framework would let Parliament decide the timing of delimitation and the Census used by ordinary law, raising fears of more political discretion over representation.
  • 3. Gerrymandering risk. Gerrymandering is deliberately drawing boundaries to favour a party or dilute a community’s influence. Delimitation in Jammu and Kashmir and Assam has faced allegations of politically motivated changes.
  • 4. Judicial review concerns. Under the present law, Delimitation Commission orders have the force of law and are generally protected from direct court challenge
  • The proposals raised concerns about the scope of judicial scrutiny and accountability
  • 5. Weakening the Rajya Sabha. A large rise in Lok Sabha seats without a matching rise in Rajya Sabha seats reduces the Upper House’s relative weight in joint sittings.
  • 6. Link with women’s reservation. Delay or dispute over delimitation also delays women’s reservation under the current framework.

Way forward for India

  • National consensus: consult States and parties before large changes in seat allocation.
  • Safeguard federalism: balance population-based representation with regional equity.
  • Independent Commission: strengthen its autonomy and transparency.
  • Judicial safeguards: clear remedies against constitutional violations and arbitrary boundary changes.
  • Phased implementation: avoid sudden changes that may harm any region’s representation.

5 · Functioning OF Parliament · Representation · Salaries · Reforms · Mplads · Supreme BUT NOT Sovereign#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 30-35. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Parliament’s effectiveness has declined: fewer sitting days, disruptions, rushed Bills, delegated legislation, cabinet dominance, guillotine, ordinances, criminalisation and weak research support. Reforms include more sittings, stronger committees, a looser whip, ethics and fair delimitation. MPLADS (Rs 5 crore per MP per year) is controversial though upheld in Bhim Singh (2010). Parliament is supreme in law-making and accountability, but not sovereign, because the Constitution is supreme.

Declining Parliamentary Effectiveness

Seven problems

  • 1. Inadequate sitting days. Sitting days have fallen sharply since the early decades after Independence. Fewer sittings mean less debate, scrutiny and opposition accountability, and the government has great control over the timing and length of sessions.
  • 2. Disruptions and adjournments. Slogan shouting, walkouts and disruptions reduce productive time. They can draw attention to issues, but repeated adjournments also weaken government accountability. Example: the 15th Lok Sabha (2009-14) had heavy disruptions and low productivity.
  • 3. Declining quality of debate. Bills are sometimes passed with little discussion or committee examination. The Anti-Defection Law, party discipline and big majorities reduce independent thinking. Technical complexity makes it hard for MPs to scrutinise specialised laws.
  • 4. Delegated legislation. Parliament often passes broad laws and leaves the executive to frame detailed rules, which reduces control if delegated legislation gets little scrutiny.
  • 5. Cabinet dominance. The executive controls much of the legislative agenda while Parliament plays a limited part in starting major legislation: “the creation dominates the creator”.
  • 6. Increasing use of the guillotine. It puts outstanding Demands for Grants to a vote at the scheduled time without further discussion. Needed for timely Budget approval, but too much use cuts financial scrutiny.
  • 7. Frequent ordinances. Repeated use of Article 123 weakens debate and scrutiny
  • Ordinances must stay exceptional

Problems OF Representation AND Accountability

Five problems

  • 1. Criminalisation of politics. 16th Lok Sabha (2014): about 34% of MPs declared criminal cases. 17th (2019): 233 MPs (43%). 18th (2024): 251 MPs (46%), including 170 (31%) with serious cases. These are cases declared in election affidavits, not findings of guilt.
  • 2. An ageing Parliament. The share of younger MPs has fallen. In the 18th Lok Sabha only about 11% were aged 40 or below at the time of election.
  • 3. Inadequate representation. The population each Lok Sabha MP represents has risen a lot since the 1971 Census. With 543 seats, MPs represent very different population sizes, which raises concerns about equal representation and workload.
  • 4. Declining political conduct. Cash-for-Query, Cash-for-Vote allegations and MPLADS irregularities have raised ethical concerns
  • Stronger ethics committees, transparency and electoral reform are needed
  • 5. Weak research support. Modern laws need technical, economic, scientific and legal expertise
  • Parliamentary research and committee support must be strengthened

MPs’ salaries and resources (Article 106)

  • Article 106 lets Parliament fix members’ salaries and allowances by law: the Salary, Allowances and Pension of Members of Parliament Act, 1954.
  • Basic salary: Rs 1.24 lakh per month (from 1 April 2023). Constituency allowance: Rs 87,000 per month. Office expense allowance: Rs 75,000 per month. Daily allowance: Rs 2,500, subject to rules.
  • Debate: pay should not be so high that it becomes the main attraction of politics, nor so low that good candidates stay away. An independent mechanism like the UK’s Independent Parliamentary Standards Authority may improve public confidence.

Reforms FOR Strengthening Parliament

Eight reform areas

  • 1. More sittings: a fixed legislative calendar and minimum sitting days. Proposals suggest 120 days for the Lok Sabha and 100 for the Rajya Sabha.
  • 2. Stronger committees: make sure major Bills get adequate committee examination, with technical experts and research support.
  • 3. Reform the Anti-Defection Law: consider limiting whips mainly to confidence votes and essential government business, so MPs can deliberate independently while governments stay stable.
  • 4. Strengthen the Opposition’s role:
    • Adequate opportunities for debates, motions and scrutiny
    • Consider dedicated days for Opposition-chosen business, drawing on the UK model
  • 5. Electoral and ethical reforms:
    • Better disclosure of criminal antecedents and political funding
    • Stronger enforcement of parliamentary ethics
  • 6. Better representation:
    • Implement women’s reservation through a fair constitutional process
    • Delimit by national consensus, federal consultation and transparent procedure
  • 7. Greater parliamentary independence: stronger autonomy of parliamentary institutions, a neutral Speaker and less executive control over business.
  • 8. Research and deliberation: expand research services and train MPs so Parliament is a forum for analysis, scientific reasoning and informed policy.
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: Parliament is not merely a law-making body. It is the base of representative democracy, executive accountability and federal cooperation. Strengthening it needs effective scrutiny, fair representation, political accountability and respect for constitutional institutions.

Mplads (members OF Parliament Local AREA Development Scheme)

Key features

  • Launched in 1993 under the P. V. Narasimha Rao government so MPs can recommend local development works based on their constituencies’ needs.
  • Nodal ministry: Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI).
  • Allocation:
    • Rs 5 crore per MP per year
    • Funds are non-lapsable and unspent balances carry forward
  • Nature of work: durable community assets in drinking water, sanitation, education, health and roads.
  • Implementation:
    • MPs recommend
    • The District Authority sanctions and implements through eligible agencies
  • COVID-19: suspended for 2020-21 and initially for 2021-22, then restored during 2021-22.

Area of operation

  • Lok Sabha MP: within their own constituency.
  • Rajya Sabha MP: any district in the State they represent.
  • Nominated MP: any district or districts in one State chosen by the MP.
  • Restrictions: funds cannot ordinarily be used for religious buildings, routine maintenance of official buildings or assets named after individuals, subject to guidelines.
  • Audit: Chartered Accountants, Local Fund Auditors or Statutory Auditors, on the Accountant General’s recommendation.

Criticism of MPLADS

  • Separation of powers: legislators influence executive work by choosing projects.
  • Federalism: health, sanitation and roads largely fall under State and local governments.
  • Corruption and misuse: reports of diversion of funds, irregular sanctioning and delays.
  • Non-lapsable funds: may encourage hoarding and spending near elections.
  • Poor utilisation (unspent amounts):
    • 14th Lok Sabha Rs 176 crore
    • 15th Rs 551 crore
    • 16th Rs 1,734 crore
  • Weak asset maintenance: focus on creating assets, not maintaining them.
  • Unequal distribution: overlapping recommendations from Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha and nominated MPs can leave districts unequal.
  • Weakening local bodies: projects ideally belong to Panchayats and Municipalities, which already lack funds.

NCRWC (2002)Recommended discontinuing it, citing federalism and distribution of powers
Second ARCRecommended abolishing it, saying it weakens separation of powers between legislature and executive

Way forward: stronger monitoring to stop diversion and ensure timely completion; better coordination to avoid duplication and unequal spread; more involvement of Panchayats and Municipalities; accountability through better utilisation, independent audits, asset maintenance and transparency.

Parliament IS Supreme BUT NOT Sovereign

Why Parliament is supreme

  • Highest legislative body at the Union level
  • Legislates on Union and Concurrent List subjects
  • Residuary powers (Article 248): exclusive power on subjects in no list.
  • Constitutional amendment (Article 368), though some amendments need State ratification.
  • Control over the executive: the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha (Article 75), which can remove the government by no-confidence.
  • Authority over judicial structure: can raise the number of Supreme Court judges (Article 124) and create tribunals, within limits.
  • Financial supremacy:
    • Controls Union taxation and spending
    • No money leaves the Consolidated Fund without its authority
  • Impeachment and removal powers: can impeach the President and remove judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.

Why it is not sovereign

  • Constitutional supremacy: Parliament gets its powers from the Constitution and must act within it.
  • Judicial review: courts can strike down laws that violate the Constitution.
  • Basic Structure doctrine: Parliament cannot destroy the basic structure (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
  • Federal structure: cannot ordinarily legislate on State List subjects except in constitutionally specified conditions.
  • Fundamental Rights: laws must respect them and other constitutional limits.
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: the Indian Parliament is supreme in its law-making and in holding the executive to account at the Union level, but not sovereign, because the Constitution is supreme. Unlike the British Parliament, its powers are limited by the Constitution.
