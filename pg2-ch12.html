1 · DPSP · Classification · Fundamental Rights VS DPSP#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · DPSP and Fundamental Duties handout pp. 1-3. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • DPSPs (Part IV, Articles 36-51) are goals the State must follow to build a welfare State. Courts cannot enforce them, but use them to interpret Fundamental Rights. Courts first put Fundamental Rights above DPSPs (Champakam 1951), then moved step by step to the present view that the two must be balanced and harmonised (Minerva Mills 1980): two wheels of one chariot.

WHAT ARE DPSPs?

The Directive Principles of State Policy are in Part IV, Articles 36 to 51. They aim to set up a Welfare State based on social, economic and political justice.

  • Why they are non-justiciable (cannot be enforced in court):
  • Financial constraints: socio-economic rights need large resources.
  • Administrative capacity: some principles need institutions and infrastructure first.
  • Social conditions: reforms like the Uniform Civil Code need careful thought about social diversity.
  • Nature of the principles: some, such as international peace, are broad policy aims and not individual rights that a court can enforce.
  • Important: being non-justiciable does not make DPSPs legally irrelevant. Courts use them to interpret Fundamental Rights and to examine the constitutional purpose of laws.

Classification OF DPSPs

DPSPs are commonly sorted into Socialist, Gandhian and Liberal-Intellectual principles. This is an academic classification, not written in the Constitution, and some Articles fit more than one group.

ClassificationArticlesMain objective
Socialist38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 43ASocial and economic justice, welfare and protection of labour
Gandhian40, 43, 43B, 46, 47, 48Village self-government, cottage industries, weaker sections and rural welfare
Liberal-Intellectual44, 45, 48A, 49, 50, 51Civil reform, education, environment, heritage, judicial independence and international peace

What the main Articles say (general knowledge, verify)

  • 38:
    • Promote a social order based on justice and reduce inequalities. 39: equal right to livelihood, fair distribution of resources, no concentration of wealth, equal pay, protection of workers and children. 39A: equal justice and free legal aid. 40: village panchayats. 41: right to work, education and public assistance. 42: just working conditions and maternity relief. 43: living wage
    • Cottage industries. 43A: workers' participation in management. 43B: cooperative societies. 44: Uniform Civil Code. 45: early childhood care and education. 46: promote the interests of SCs, STs and weaker sections. 47: nutrition, public health and prohibition of intoxicants. 48: modern agriculture and animal husbandry, cow protection. 48A: protect environment, forests and wildlife. 49: protect monuments of national importance. 50: separate judiciary from executive. 51: promote international peace and respect for international law

Fundamental Rights vs DPSP: Judicial Evolution

The relationship between Part III (Fundamental Rights) and Part IV (DPSP) has been shaped by court decisions.

  1. State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951)
    The Supreme Court held that Fundamental Rights prevail over DPSPs in a direct conflict. DPSPs cannot override enforceable Fundamental Rights.
  2. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)
    The Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights. This limited Parliament's power to amend rights in order to carry out DPSPs.
  3. Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)
    Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights to implement DPSPs, but an amendment cannot violate the Basic Structure.
  4. Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)
    The Court held that harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs is part of the Basic Structure. Giving absolute priority to either Part III or Part IV would upset the constitutional balance. The two are complementary and supplementary, not inherently in conflict.

Present constitutional position

  • Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights to implement DPSPs, subject to the Basic Structure doctrine.
  • Courts try harmonious construction between Parts III and IV.
  • DPSPs help courts interpret the scope of Fundamental Rights, especially Article 21.
  • Article 31C gives special protection to qualifying laws that implement Articles 39(b) and 39(c).
Way forward / key point

Core principle: Fundamental Rights and DPSPs are like two wheels of a chariot. Both are needed to reach the Constitution's goal of a social revolution.

2 · Article 39(b)(c) · Right TO Property · Eminent Domain · Nine-judge Verdict 2024#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · DPSP and Fundamental Duties handout pp. 3-4. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Article 39(b) wants community resources shared for the common good and 39(c) wants wealth not concentrated. The right to property is no longer fundamental (44th Amendment 1978); it is a constitutional right under Article 300A. In Property Owners Association (5 Nov 2024) a nine-judge Bench said not every private property is a community resource, but some can be, and Article 31C continues to protect qualifying laws.

Article 39(b) AND (c)

Article 39(b): the State should ensure that the ownership and control of the material resources of the community are distributed so as to serve the common good.

  • Article 39(c): the economic system should not result in the concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment.
  • Together they aim at economic justice and at preventing too much concentration of economic power.

Right TO Property Under THE Constitution

The Right to Property was first a Fundamental Right under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31. The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 removed its Fundamental Right status. It is now a constitutional right under Article 300A: no person shall be deprived of property except by authority of law.

  • Important features
  • Constitutional right: no longer a Fundamental Right.
  • Authority of law: the executive cannot take away property by a mere administrative order without legal authority.
  • Public purpose: compulsory acquisition must serve a genuine public purpose.
  • Compensation: the protection does not guarantee market-value compensation in every case, but compensation and acquisition procedures remain open to constitutional scrutiny.
  • Judicial review: property acquisition laws and actions can be challenged in court.

Eminent domain is the State's power to acquire private property for a public purpose under a valid law.

  • Key issues:
    • Balancing private ownership and public interest
    • Fair compensation and rehabilitation
    • Protection against arbitrary acquisition
    • Distributing resources for social and economic justice

Kolkata Municipal Corporation v. Bimal Kumar Shah (2024)

  • The Supreme Court listed seven procedural safeguards for lawful property acquisition:
  • 1. Right to notice: the owner must be told of the proposed acquisition.
  • 2. Right to be heard: objections must be properly considered.
  • 3. Reasoned decision: authorities must explain the acquisition decision.
  • 4. Public purpose: acquisition must serve a legitimate public purpose.
  • 5. Fair compensation: the framework must provide fair compensation or restitution.
  • 6. Timely process: efficient procedure without unreasonable delay.
  • 7. Conclusion of proceedings: the process must reach a lawful end.
  • Significance: Article 300A protects more than the formal need for a law. It also needs safeguards against arbitrary deprivation.
Memory aid · NHRP-FTC

Notice · Hearing · Reasons · Public purpose · Fair compensation · Timely · Conclusion (the seven safeguards) (own mnemonic)

Property Owners Association v. State OF Maharashtra (2024)

Background: the case concerned provisions of the Maharashtra Housing and Area Development Act, 1976 on acquiring certain properties for redevelopment. The central question: can privately owned property be treated as a material resource of the community under Article 39(b)?

  • Judgment: on 5 November 2024 a nine-judge Constitution Bench gave a 7:2 majority judgment.
  • Key holdings
  • 1. Not every private property is a community resource. Not all privately owned resources qualify as material resources of the community under Article 39(b), though some may.
  • 2. No automatic State claim. The State cannot treat all private property as a community resource just because redistribution may serve a public purpose.
  • 3. Criteria for identifying community resources:
    • The nature and characteristics of the resource
    • Its impact on community welfare
    • Its scarcity
    • And the consequences of it being concentrated in private hands
  • 4. Constitutional flexibility. The Constitution does not prescribe a single economic model
  • Governments may adopt different economic policies within constitutional limits
  • 5. Article 31C continues to operate. It still protects qualifying laws that implement Articles 39(b) and 39(c) from challenge under Articles 14 and 19. But this does not remove every constitutional limit or stop scrutiny of whether a law truly satisfies Article 31C.

Significance of the judgment

  • Protection of property rights: stops every private resource being automatically labelled a community resource.
  • Economic justice: allows redistribution where resources genuinely satisfy Article 39(b).
  • Judicial clarity: gives criteria to identify material resources of the community.
  • Balanced economic model: recognises both public welfare and private ownership.
  • Constitutional balance: reinforces the relationship between DPSPs and constitutional rights.
Way forward / key point

Core principle: Article 39(b) promotes distribution of resources for the common good, but public welfare cannot justify treating every privately owned resource as community property.

3 · Uniform Civil CODE · Article 44 · Personal LAWS · Uttarakhand#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · DPSP and Fundamental Duties handout pp. 4-7. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Article 44 asks the State to try for a Uniform Civil Code, one set of civil laws on marriage, divorce, maintenance, adoption and succession for all religions. India has separate personal laws, so the debate is equality and gender justice against religious freedom and diversity. Key cases: Shah Bano, Danial Latifi, Shayara Bano, Sarla Mudgal, Abdul Samad. The 21st Law Commission (2018) preferred gradual reform. Uttarakhand has a State UCC since 27 January 2025.

Article 44 AND Personal LAWS

Article 44 directs the State to endeavour to secure a Uniform Civil Code (UCC) throughout India.

  • A UCC would give common civil laws on marriage, divorce, maintenance, adoption, succession and inheritance, irrespective of religion.
  • India has different personal laws on these matters. That creates a debate between equality, religious freedom, gender justice and cultural diversity.
Community / frameworkImportant legislation

MuslimsMuslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1937; Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939; other applicable laws

Religion-neutral civil marriageSpecial Marriage Act 1954

Special Marriage Act, 1954

  • Provides a civil marriage framework irrespective of religion or caste.
  • Lets interfaith couples marry without religious conversion.
  • Contains provisions about public notice and objections.
  • Concern: the notice procedure can expose couples to family pressure, harassment or threats.

Judicial Developments ON Personal LAWS

  1. Shah Bano (1985)
    The question was whether a divorced Muslim woman could claim maintenance under Section 125 CrPC, 1973. The Supreme Court held that Section 125 is religion-neutral, so she could claim maintenance if she met the statutory conditions, and payment of Mahr did not automatically end the husband's maintenance duty.
  2. Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986
    Created a separate statutory framework for the rights of divorced Muslim women. Widely seen as a response to the Shah Bano judgment.
  3. Danial Latifi v. Union of India (2001)
    The Supreme Court upheld the validity of the 1986 Act and held that the husband must make a reasonable and fair provision for the divorced wife within the Iddat period, and this is not limited to her needs during Iddat alone.
  4. Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017)
    By a 3:2 majority the Supreme Court set aside instant Triple Talaq (Talaq-e-Biddat).
  5. Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019
    Declared instant Triple Talaq void and illegal and made pronouncing it a criminal offence with up to three years imprisonment.
  6. Mohd. Abdul Samad v. State of Telangana (2024)
    The Supreme Court reaffirmed that divorced Muslim women can claim maintenance under Section 144 BNSS, alongside remedies under the 1986 Act.

Criticism of the 2019 Act

  • Criminalisation: critics say a marital issue already made legally ineffective need not mean imprisonment.
  • Family welfare: imprisonment can hurt the financial security of women and children where the husband is the sole earner.
  • Abandonment: the law does not directly address abandonment of spouses across all communities.
  • Significance of Abdul Samad:
    • Maintenance rights are not defeated merely because a personal law exists (secular protection)
    • It strengthens financial protection of divorced Muslim women (gender justice)
    • Personal-law legislation and general maintenance provisions can work together (harmonious interpretation)

Polygamy and personal laws

  • Polygamy is having more than one spouse at the same time.
  • Hindu marriage law generally prohibits a second marriage during the lifetime of an existing spouse.
  • Traditional Muslim personal law allows a Muslim man up to four wives, subject to applicable legal conditions. The position differs across communities and statutes.
  • Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India (1995): a Hindu husband cannot escape the law against bigamy merely by converting to Islam without dissolving the first marriage.
  • Issue: the debate is about religious autonomy, equality before law and women's rights.

FOR AND Against A Uniform Civil CODE

Arguments in favour

  • 1. Gender justice. Many personal laws grew in patriarchal conditions. A UCC may remove discriminatory practices in marriage, divorce, maintenance and inheritance. Example: the debate on Triple Talaq and maintenance for divorced Muslim women.
  • 2. National integration. Separate personal laws mean separate legal frameworks for communities
  • Supporters say a common civil framework promotes equal citizenship and national consolidation
  • 3. Secularism. A religion-neutral civil framework may lessen the role of religious identity in deciding legal rights and duties.
  • 4. Simplification of laws. A common framework can reduce procedural differences and simplify disputes on marriage, divorce and succession.
  • 5. Equality before law. Similar civil matters should be governed by common principles whatever the religion.

Arguments against

  • 1. Religious and cultural diversity. India has many religions, denominations and communities with different customs
  • A single code may fail to fit them all
  • 2. Minority apprehensions. Minorities may see a UCC as imposing the personal laws or culture of the majority, causing distrust and opposition.
  • 3. Freedom of religion. Personal laws may involve religious practice
  • Reform must balance Articles 25 and 26 with equality, dignity and other rights
  • 4. Difficulty framing uniform rules. Communities follow different rules on marriage, divorce, inheritance and adoption
  • Finding common rules acceptable to all is hard
  • 5. Selective reform. Critics question the heavy focus on Article 44 when other DPSPs on employment, health, education and social security remain poorly implemented.
  • 6. Uniformity vs equality. Uniform rules do not automatically give substantive equality. The aim should be to remove discrimination without needlessly destroying cultural diversity.

21st Law Commission consultation paper (2018) observed that a UCC was neither necessary nor desirable at that stage. It preferred reforming discriminatory provisions within existing family laws while keeping legitimate diversity.

  • Suggestions:
    • Remove discriminatory practices from personal laws
    • Strengthen equality and dignity, especially for women
    • Reform family laws gradually through consultation
    • Avoid treating diversity itself as a problem

Uttarakhand Uniform Civil CODE

The Uniform Civil Code of Uttarakhand, 2024 came into force on 27 January 2025. Uttarakhand became the first State in independent India to adopt such a comprehensive State-level UCC.

  • Features:
    • A common legal framework on marriage, divorce, maintenance and succession for persons covered
    • Compulsory marriage registration
    • Polygamy prohibited within the framework
    • Registration and legal requirements for live-in relationships
    • Scheduled Tribes and certain constitutionally protected customary rights are excluded
  • 2026 development: Uttarakhand amended its UCC framework through the UCC Amendment Ordinance, 2026, followed by the UCC Amendment Act, 2026. (current affairs; verify)
  • Concerns:
    • Privacy (mandatory live-in registration raises personal-autonomy questions)
    • Administrative oversight (more State involvement in private relationships)
    • Cultural diversity (exemptions raise questions about what uniformity means)
    • Implementation (needs public awareness and easy procedures)
  • Significance: an important State-level example to study the benefits and difficulties of a UCC.

Way forward

  • Gradual reform: remove discriminatory provisions through consultation with the affected communities.
  • Gender justice: equality in marriage, divorce, maintenance and inheritance.
  • Optional civil framework: strengthen accessible, religion-neutral alternatives under civil law.
  • Stakeholder consultation: women, legal experts, minority communities and civil society.
  • Constitutional balance: protect equality and dignity without needless interference in religious and cultural freedom.
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: the aim of a UCC should be equality and gender justice, not uniformity for its own sake. Reform should be gradual, inclusive and consistent with constitutional values.

4 · DPSP Significance AND Criticism · Fundamental Duties · Article 51A#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · DPSP and Fundamental Duties handout pp. 7-10. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • DPSPs give long-term direction to every government and a yardstick for citizens, but they are non-justiciable, costly and unevenly implemented. Fundamental Duties (Article 51A, Part IVA) were added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) on the Swaran Singh Committee's advice; the 86th Amendment (2002) made them eleven. They are non-justiciable, so courts use them to support rights and policies rather than to punish citizens directly.

Significance OF DPSPs

1. Welfare State: DPSPs deepen the Preamble's commitment to social and economic justice.

  • 2. Policy direction: a common framework for Central and State governments even when political leadership changes.
  • 3. Complement to Fundamental Rights:
    • Part III protects individual liberty
    • Part IV creates the social conditions needed to enjoy those rights
  • 4. Democratic accountability: a yardstick for citizens to judge government policy.
  • 5. Continuing relevance: inequality, environmental damage, unemployment and social exclusion make DPSPs more important.

Criticism OF DPSPs

1. Non-justiciable nature. Courts cannot enforce DPSPs directly, so implementation depends on political will and State capacity. Critics called them "pious superfluities" and compared them to a cheque that cannot be cashed until resources become available.

  • 2. Financial and administrative constraints. Employment, health, education and social security need large public spending. States differ in financial and administrative capacity, which affects implementation.
  • 3. Unsystematic enumeration. The Constitution mixes broad objectives such as justice and equality with specific policies such as prohibition and cattle protection. This causes differences of scope and interpretation.
  • 4. Lack of clarity. Some principles are broadly worded and leave much discretion to governments. But this flexibility also lets them adapt to changing social and economic conditions.
  • 5. Tension between individual freedom and public welfare. Some directives can clash with liberty, religious freedom and economic choice. Examples: prohibition (Article 47), cattle slaughter (Article 48) and the Uniform Civil Code (Article 44).
  • 6. Uneven implementation. Directives on labour welfare, social security, weaker sections and equal opportunity are only partly fulfilled. The problem is not just missing laws but weak implementation and unequal access to benefits.
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: DPSPs remain essential to India's vision of a Welfare State. Success depends on sustained political commitment, enough resources and effective implementation. The goal is social and economic justice without weakening Fundamental Rights.

Fundamental Duties: Article 51A

Fundamental Duties are in Part IVA, Article 51A. They were added by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. The 86th Amendment Act, 2002 added one more duty, so there are now 11 Fundamental Duties.

  • Rationale:
    • To reduce indifference to nation-building and larger public interests
    • To balance individual freedom with social responsibility
    • To encourage citizens to take an active part in national development

The 11 duties (general knowledge, verify wording): to (a) respect the Constitution, its ideals, the National Flag and the National Anthem; (b) cherish the noble ideals of the freedom struggle; (c) uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India; (d) defend the country and render national service when called; (e) promote harmony and brotherhood and renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women; (f) value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture; (g) protect and improve the natural environment and have compassion for living creatures; (h) develop scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform; (i) safeguard public property and abjure violence; (j) strive towards excellence in individual and collective activity; (k) the parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to a child aged 6-14 years (added in 2002).

Rights and duties: two sides of one coin. Rights protect individual freedom; duties remind citizens of their responsibilities to society.

Fundamental RightRelated Fundamental Duty
Article 15: prohibition of discriminationArticle 51A(e): promote harmony and renounce practices derogatory to women
Article 19: freedom of speech and expressionArticle 51A(h): develop scientific temper and the spirit of inquiry
Article 21: right to lifeArticle 51A(g): protect the environment and living creatures
Article 21A: right to educationArticle 51A(k): parents must provide educational opportunities to children aged 6-14
Quote

Gandhi's view: citizens should give importance to performing their duties; rights and duties are closely connected.

Constitutional position

  • Fundamental Rights: mainly protect individuals against arbitrary State action.
  • Fundamental Duties: encourage responsible behaviour towards society and the nation.
  • DPSPs: direct the State toward social and economic justice.
  • Together, Parts III, IV and IVA support the vision of individual dignity and collective welfare.

Judicial Interpretation OF Fundamental Duties

1. Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986): freedom of conscience. Three students of the Jehovah's Witnesses refused to sing the National Anthem because of their religious beliefs, though they stood respectfully while it was sung. The Supreme Court held that students cannot be forced to sing the Anthem against genuine religious belief. Their conduct was protected under Articles 19(1)(a) and 25. Respect for national symbols does not require compulsory singing in every case. Significance: Fundamental Duties cannot override freedom of conscience and religion.

  • 2. M. C. Mehta v. Union of India: environment. The Court used Article 51A(g) to stress citizens' duty to protect the environment, and directed steps to promote environmental education, including teaching environmental awareness in educational institutions. Significance: duties can support judicial directions for environmental protection.
  • 3. Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996). The Court recognised key environmental principles: the Precautionary Principle, the Polluter Pays Principle and Sustainable Development, strengthening environmental protection under Articles 21, 48A and 51A(g).
  • 4. National Anthem in cinema halls: Shyam Narayan Chouksey. In 2016 the Court directed cinema halls to play the Anthem before films. In 2018 it modified this and made playing the Anthem optional, keeping safeguards for respectful conduct and persons with disabilities.
  • Important: the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 punishes specified acts of disrespect. It does not create a general duty to sing the National Anthem.

Laws that give effect to Fundamental Duties. Duties are non-justiciable, but several laws support them.

Value addition

Important distinction: these laws give effect to related constitutional objectives. Not every breach of a Fundamental Duty automatically becomes a criminal offence.

Committee Recommendations

Justice J. S. Verma Committee (1999) studied how duties can be promoted through education and awareness. It recommended: include Fundamental Duties in school curricula and civic education; use educational material and mass media; train teachers in constitutional values; identify existing laws that give effect to the duties.

  • NCRWC (2002) recommended:
    • A duty to vote (encourage participation in elections)
    • A duty to pay taxes as a civic responsibility
    • Responsible parenthood
    • And stronger civic education on duties. These were recommendations and have not all been put into Article 51A

Rights vs Duties: THE Debate

For greater emphasis on dutiesFor prioritising rights
Promote responsible citizenship and social disciplineFundamental Rights are enforceable; duties are generally non-justiciable
Strengthen national unity and public welfareToo much emphasis on duties may weaken protection against arbitrary State action
Encourage protection of the environment and public propertyGovernments may use duties to justify needless limits on dissent
Build a culture of active democratic participationThe State must fulfil its own constitutional obligations and not shift responsibility to citizens

Balanced position. Rights and duties are complementary, not competing. Citizens should respect their duties, but duties must not become an excuse to weaken individual liberty, equality or constitutional safeguards.

  • Way forward:
    • Teach duties alongside rights (civic education)
    • Promote constitutional values through schools, media and community programmes
    • Use specific laws where necessary, subject to Fundamental Rights
    • Governments must fulfil their own constitutional duties
    • Encourage voting, environmental protection, public service and community cooperation
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: Fundamental Duties make citizens active participants in nation-building. Their purpose is to strengthen constitutional responsibility, not weaken individual freedom. A healthy democracy needs both enforceable rights and responsible citizens.
