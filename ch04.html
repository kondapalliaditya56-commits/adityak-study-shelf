1 · Chhatrapati Shivaji 74-80: who he was and why he rose#

1.1 Who he was

  • Shivaji 74-80 Bhonsle was born on 19 February 1630 to Shahji Bhonsle and Jijabai.
  • His father was a Jagirdar of Poona and served as a General and Mansabdar in Bijapur (1489-1686), ruled by the Adil Shahi dynasty. This dynasty, together with the Qutub Shahi (1518-1687), had replaced the Bahmani Sultanate (1347-1527) in the Deccan.
  • Early training came from his guardian Dadaji Kondadev, who shaped him as a soldier and administrator. The deeper inspiration came from his mother Jijabai and his own fierce ambition.
  • He captured his first fort at the age of 16, in 1646. Working from the Maval region, a hilly, forested terrain in today's Pune district running through the Sahyadri range, he gathered an army of local warriors and peasants.
Memory hook

Sixteen in sixteen-forty-six.

He took his first fort at 16, in 16-46: the "16" shows up twice.

Decode: born 19 February 1630 to Shahji Bhonsle (Jagirdar of Poona, General and Mansabdar in Bijapur) and Jijabai (the deeper inspiration); trained by guardian Dadaji Kondadev ("Dada" = elder guardian); first fort 1646; base = Maval region in the Sahyadri.

Why it sticks: 1630 + 16 = 1646, so age and year match.

1.2 Guerrilla warfare: the teacher's boards

Teacher's board

Tactics = guerrilla warfare: hide and seek, attack and withdraw, sabotage. It is not traditional warfare. It uses surprise attacks, speed and geography, and creates shock and awe in the enemy. It is the only way to defeat a much more militarily stronger opponent.

Teacher's board

Guerrilla warfare is a tool used for positive, neutral or negative goals. Examples on the board: Naxals in India; Spain (1808-12) against Napoleon; 1857 Revolt (Kunwar Singh and Tatya Tope); Ganimi Kava of the Marathas; Alluri Sitarama Raju (1922-24).

1.3 Why he rose against Bijapur and the Mughals

  • From 1657 onwards, Shivaji 74-80 launched systematic attacks on the Kingdom of Bijapur (1489-1686), which followed economically oppressive and religiously discriminatory policies. This was also the period when Aurangzeb 58-07 became Mughal emperor.
  • In 1659, Ali Adil Shah II, Sultan of Bijapur, sent his General Afzal Khan to kill Shivaji 74-80 under the pretext of negotiations. Instead, Shivaji 74-80 killed the seven-feet-tall Afzal Khan using his concealed Bagh Nakh (Tiger Claws) and a Bichhwa (dagger).
  • When Aurangzeb 58-07 launched his campaign for the conquest of the Deccan, Shivaji 74-80 defeated the Mughal General Shaista Khan 64-88 in 1663 and sacked the wealthy port of Surat, which enraged Aurangzeb 58-07.
  • Aurangzeb 58-07 sent General Jai Singh 99-43, who used an encirclement strategy to cut off supplies to Maratha territories. In 1665 Jai Singh 99-43 laid siege to Fort Purandhar (40-50 km southeast of Pune).
Teacher's board

BG Tilak (1890s) used this cultural history (a Maratha against a Mughal general) to arouse nationalism against the British.

Teacher's board

Triangle on the board: Aurangzeb 58-07, the Marathas and Bijapur. The Marathas' goal is Swarajya (boxed). 1674 = coronation of Chhatrapati Shivaji 74-80; 1686 = Aurangzeb 58-07 annexes Bijapur.

Teacher's board

Gherao = starve the enemy. Jai Singh 99-43 was a powerful Mansabdar of the Hindu group of the Mughal State. The standard books say Shivaji 74-80 never accepted Mughal sovereignty; the teacher's correction: the Treaty of Purandhar 1665 was a temporary tactic.

Memory hook

Shivaji 74-80's chain: Fort, Fight, Fangs; Surat, Siege, Sadhu; Crown, Close.

46-57-59 = Fort, Fight, Fangs; 63-65-66 = Surat, Siege, Sadhu; 74-80 = Crown, Close.

Decode: 1646 first fort at 16; 1657 systematic attacks on Bijapur begin; 1659 Afzal Khan killed with Bagh Nakh (tiger claws) and Bichhwa; 1663 Shaista Khan 64-88 defeated and Surat sacked; 1665 Jai Singh 99-43 besieges Purandhar; 1666 Agra imprisonment and the Sadhu escape; 1674 coronation at Raigarh; 1680 natural death (Shivaji 74-80).

Why it sticks: three triples of alliterating words, then the two ends of his reign.

1.4 Treaty of Purandhar 1665: a tactical pause, not a permanent defeat

To prevent the massacre of his soldiers and people, Shivaji 74-80 negotiated the Treaty of Purandhar (1665). Under its terms:

  1. He surrendered 23 of his 35 forts to the Mughals.
  2. He accepted Aurangzeb 58-07 as Emperor but declined a personal mansab for himself.
  3. His son Sambhaji 80-89 was made a Mughal Mansabdar with a rank of 5,000.
  4. He agreed to support the Mughals in their war against Bijapur.
Memory hook

Fat Elephants Squash Bananas.

F-E-S-B: the four terms of the Treaty of Purandhar (1665).

Decode: Forts (23 of 35 surrendered, so 12 kept); Emperor (Aurangzeb 58-07 accepted, but no personal mansab for Shivaji 74-80); Sambhaji 80-89 made a Mughal Mansabdar of 5,000; Bijapur (support the Mughals against it). Jai Singh 99-43 forced it by a gherao (starving siege).

Why it sticks: "two-three of three-five" = 23 of 35. Teacher's ruling: a temporary tactic; 1665 bows, 1674 crowns (the coronation was its legal reversal). Treaties are named after the place where signed.

  • In 1666, Shivaji 74-80 and Sambhaji 80-89 visited the Mughal court at Agra. They were insulted and imprisoned. Their escape became legendary: disguised as Sadhus with bodies smeared in ash, they went to Mathura. Shivaji 74-80 left Sambhaji 80-89 there with a Brahmin family and took a long route back to his capital.
  • Aurangzeb 58-07 marched to the Deccan in 1681, when his own son Prince Akbar 56-05 defected to Chhatrapati Sambhaji 80-89.
Teacher's board

Treaties are named after the place where they were signed. The coronation = the legal reversal of the Treaty of Purandhar, giving a fully independent kingdom. Common feature of the Jat kingdom, Svarajya and the Sikh state: the result of people / mass participation (the Jat kingdom = the result of peasant revolts). After the escape, the teacher's ink reads: rebellion resumes.

1.5 The 1674 coronation at Raigarh: Svarajya

  • In 1674, Shivaji 74-80 was coronated at Raigarh as Chhatrapati, legally declaring himself independent from the Mughals. His kingdom, called Svarajya ("own rule"), covered the Marathi-speaking region. (The handout's heading places the headquarters at Satara; see the capital note in section 8.)
  • The Maratha kingdom had thus been founded in the second half of the 17th century, built through wars against both Bijapur and Aurangzeb 58-07. Shivaji 74-80 died of natural causes in 1680.
Teacher's board

Vatan = hereditary. In Mughal terms, a Vatan right could mean: getting the LR of a Jagir (a Vatan Jagir); collecting LR and being paid a commission (the Zamindar); a job such as Qanungo (maintaining land records); or Khudkasht cultivation of a plot.

2 · Military tactics: army, Jagirs, navy and forts#

2.1 The army: light cavalry and guerrilla warfare

  • Shivaji 74-80 built a well-organised army of around 30,000 to 40,000 cavalry. He used the mountainous terrain and dense forests to master guerrilla warfare and captured 240 forts, many of them in the Konkan.
  • Their strategy was built on surprise (shock and awe) combined with guerrilla tactics. The major fighting force was a horse-mounted army with light cavalry for faster movement, in sharp contrast to the Mughals, who relied on heavy cavalry (elephants carrying a support system like food).
Teacher's board

Heavy cavalry = elephants plus horses, carrying supplies, slow to move. Light cavalry = horses only; food and shelter on the way (by love or force). Speed = the focus for guerrilla warfare.

Memory hook

Light Lives off the land; Heavy Hauls its kitchen.

L-L and H-H: light cavalry = horses only, food and shelter on the way (by love or force); heavy cavalry = elephants plus supply carriers, slow.

Decode: Shivaji 74-80's army = about 30,000 to 40,000 light cavalry and 240 forts (many in the Konkan); the Mughals relied on heavy cavalry. Speed is the focus of guerrilla warfare.

Why it sticks: two numbers, two lines: "240 forts, 30-40 thousand horses".

2.2 The Saranjami system: military land grants

  • Unlike the Mughals, Maratha civil officials did not hold military roles. Shivaji 74-80 never gave Jagirs and paid officials in cash, leading to a centralised polity.
  • Later Chhatrapati Shahu 19-49 gave Tankha Jagirs to military commanders called Saranjams, but never Vatan Jagirs. This prevented military loyalty from getting tied to hereditary land grants and gave the ruler more control.
  • Under this system revenue assignments on Jagirs were given to the Maratha Sardars, the Peshwa (Prime Minister of the Chhatrapati) and various military commanders, each drawing income from designated territories to maintain troops and administration. This led to a fragmented polity as revenue and military power were dispersed rather than centralised.

Teacher's board

Land = the source of power. Whoever controls land and its resources is more powerful, therefore there was greater concentration of power in Shivaji 74-80. A Saranjam will be taken away if not in service.

Memory hook

Zero, One, Never.

Shivaji 74-80 gave zero Jagirs; Shahu 19-49 gave one kind (Tankha, i.e. Saranjam); and never the other kind (Vatan).

Decode: Shivaji 74-80 never gave Jagirs (cash pay, centralised); Shahu 19-49 gave Tankha Jagirs = Saranjam to military commanders (temporary, tied to service, not heritable) but never Vatan Jagirs. Vatan (hereditary) belongs to civil officials: Patils and Deshmukhs.

Why it sticks: Saranjam = Soldier, Service-bound; Vatan = Vansh (family line), civil.

2.3 The navy: Father of the Modern Indian Navy

  • Shivaji 74-80 established a naval force of 20 ships in 1657, a full navy of 200 ships by 1664, and the peak Maratha navy reached 700 ships.
  • His doctrine was not about matching the British or Portuguese in deep-ocean warfare. It was a specialised, asymmetric coastal defence strategy designed to:
  • Protect Maratha merchant ships.
  • Secure the Konkan coastline from the Siddis of Janjira.
  • Prevent European powers from dominating Konkan trade routes.
  • He built forts in coastal areas, established naval bases in the Arabian Sea, and focused on intelligence gathering at sea.
  • In 1668 Shivaji 74-80 allowed the French to establish a trading factory at Rajapur. In return he secured high-quality military supplies: European cannons, lead and saltpetre for gunpowder.
  • He is called the Father of the Modern Indian Navy because he relied entirely on indigenous human resources, engineering and strategy, never on European naval architects or shipbuilders. He recruited from local coastal communities, the Kolis and Bhandaris, master seafarers who knew the tides, hidden rocks and shallow creeks of the Konkan. He appointed commanders like Mayanak Bhandari and Daulat Khan.
Memory hook

20, 200, 700: zeros trail.

20 ships in '57, 200 by '64, a peak of 700: ten times in seven years, then more.

Decode: Shivaji 74-80's navy = 20 ships (1657), full navy of 200 (1664), peak Maratha navy 700; asymmetric coastal defence, not deep-ocean war. 1668: French factory at Rajapur in return for Cannons, Lead, Saltpetre (C-L-S). Crew: Kolis and Bhandaris; commanders Mayanak Bhandari and Daulat Khan; all indigenous, hence "Father of the Modern Indian Navy".

Why it sticks: 1664 - 1657 = 7 years for the tenfold jump; C-L-S = the three supplies he secured.

Teacher's board

Saltpetre = potassium nitrate, used in gunpowder. Desi = indigenous tech, human resources, tactics. "Enemy's enemy = friend": the British were the common enemy. The Maratha navy was comparable in tech and strength to the European navies (the teacher's "modern" box).

2.4 The fleet: tactical ship design

A key insight was tactical ship design. Copying the massive deep-water (blue water) ships of the Europeans would have been a strategic disaster in the shallow estuaries of the Konkan coast. Instead he built a lighter, faster fleet for coastal defence and hit-and-run:

VesselRole
GhurabsGunboats carrying heavy artillery
GalbatsFast, highly manoeuvrable rowboats with light sails that could tow the Ghurabs into position during dead winds or chase fleeing enemy ships
Shibars and PalsLarger vessels for transport and heavier engagements

To ensure self-reliance, Shivaji 74-80 set up shipbuilding yards, sourcing timber from the teak forests of the Sahyadri.

Memory hook

Ghurab Guns, Galbat Gallops.

Ghurab = gunboat; Galbat = fast rowboat that gallops ahead or tows; Shibars and Pals = the big ships.

Decode: Ghurabs: gunboats with heavy artillery; Galbats: fast, manoeuvrable rowboats with light sails that tow Ghurabs in dead winds or chase fleeing ships; Shibars and Pals: larger vessels for transport and heavier engagements. Shipyards used Sahyadri teak.

Why it sticks: G-G pairs the two small, fast partners (gun and tow); the big pair comes last.

2.5 The forts: the Jaladurg doctrine

  • His greatest strategic innovation was the Jaladurg doctrine: sea forts on rocky islands or peninsulas along the coast. They provided security to docked vessels, and cannons in these forts and along the coast kept enemy ships at a distance. Key examples: Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg and Suvarnadurg.
  • In Gujarat the EIC so feared the Marathas that they made Bombay their main base for trade, leading to the Bombay Presidency in 1687. The Marathas actively captured ports from the Portuguese: in 1736 they removed the Portuguese and Ethiopian Muslims from the Konkan coast, and by 1739 effectively controlled Salsette and Bassein.
Memory hook

S-V-S: Shivaji 74-80's Very Strong sea forts.

Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg, Suvarnadurg: the three named Jaladurg examples.

Decode: Jaladurg doctrine = sea forts on rocky islands or peninsulas; they sheltered docked ships and their cannons kept enemy ships away. Teacher's Bombay chain: 1668 EIC already in Bombay; 1687 Bombay Presidency (a group of seven islands), because Surat feared Maratha cavalry.

Why it sticks: three S-V-S forts; and "68 in, 87 Presidency".

Teacher's board

1668: EIC already in Bombay. 1687: Bombay becomes the Presidency (HQ). Bombay = a group of seven islands. Reasons to shift from Surat: British navy strong, goods better protected at Bombay; at Surat there was fear of (Maratha) cavalry.

Why it sticks: the second chunk is the three sea-forts (S-V-S) you already know.

2.6 After Shivaji 74-80: Admiral Kanhoji Angre

  • After Shivaji 74-80 the dominant naval figure was Admiral Kanhoji Angre, called the "Shivaji 74-80 of the Seas", who dominated the Arabian Sea for 30 years in the early 1700s until his death in 1729.
  • Kanhoji fought the British, Portuguese, Dutch, the Sawants (a small kingdom since 1627) and the Siddis or Habshis (people of African ancestry, now found in Gujarat, Karnataka, Andhra and parts of Sindh and Baluchistan in Pakistan).
  • From 1714 to 1724 he frequently attacked the EIC in Bombay with guerrilla warfare at sea. The bigger, heavier British ships could not pursue the small, fast Maratha vessels in shallow waters. The result was a stalemate, and Kanhoji eventually offered peace to the British.
  • The British defeated the Maratha Navy in 1756, one year before the EIC defeated the Bengal Suba at Plassey (1757).
Memory hook

Kanhoji: 14-24, dies 29; navy dies 56, Plassey 57.

Five foes: Big Pigs Don't Swim Slowly = British, Portuguese, Dutch, Sawants, Siddis.

Decode: Admiral Kanhoji Angre, the "Shivaji 74-80 of the Seas", dominated the Arabian Sea for about 30 years until his death in 1729; raided the EIC at Bombay 1714-24 (stalemate); the British defeated the Maratha navy in 1756, one year before Plassey (1757). He switched from Tarabai to Shahu 19-49 in 1714.

Why it sticks: the 14 repeats (switch in 1714, raids from 1714) and 56 sits right before 57.

3 · Sambhaji 80-89 and Rajaram 89-00: the troubled succession#

  • Chhatrapati means Emperor. Shivaji 74-80 was the first Chhatrapati, ruling from 1674 until his death from natural causes in 1680.
  • After him, his elder son Sambhaji 80-89 took charge. He was captured by Aurangzeb 58-07 and, after weeks of torture, was assassinated in 1689.
  • The younger son Rajaram 89-00 then took over but died of tuberculosis in 1700 at just 30 years of age.
  • With no adult male heir ready, Rajaram 89-00's widow Tarabai stepped in. She ruled as regent in the name of their infant son Shivaji II. This held until 1707, when the Mughals released Shahu 19-49, the elder son of Sambhaji 80-89, who had been in Mughal imprisonment.
  • Shahu 19-49's release triggered a civil war between Shahu 19-49 and Tarabai that lasted until 1719 (section 6).
Teacher's board

Regent = rules in the name of the king, because the king cannot rule (age, health). A war is either internal (civil war) or external.

Memory hook

Shivaji 74-80's family chain: S-S-R-T-S.

"Sweet Sons Rule; Tarabai Stops; Shahu 19-49 returns."

Decode: Shivaji 74-80 (natural death) → elder son Sambhaji 80-89 (captured, tortured, assassinated by Aurangzeb 58-07) → younger son Rajaram 89-00 (TB at 30) → widow Tarabai, regent for infant Shivaji II (1700-07) → 1707 the Mughals release Shahu 19-49 (elder son of Sambhaji 80-89) → civil war to 1719.

Why it sticks: each code ends where the next starts (80, 89, 00); and 7 years of regency + 12 years of civil war = 19, the year Shahu 19-49 starts. Elder dies by torture, younger by illness.

4 · The Ashtapradhan and Maratha administration#

4.1 The Chhatrapati was not an absolute ruler

  • The Chhatrapati governed with a council of eight ministers, the Ashtapradhan.
  • This was similar in concept to the British Monarch and his Privy Council, or the Mughal Emperor and his Darbar.
  • Separation of powers (a separate legislature, executive, judiciary) is a modern concept. The Ashtapradhan was simply a council of ministers serving the Chhatrapati.

Teacher's board

The Chhatrapati was like a British monarch with a Privy Council (he had ministers), while the Mughal emperor had a Darbar of Mansabdars (Irani, Turani, Hindu, Deccani and others). Today = Prime Minister plus Council of Ministers.

4.3 The Peshwa's own council: Darakhdars and the Barbhai Mandal

  • Just as the British PM had his own Council of Ministers beneath the monarch, the Peshwa had his own council of senior civil servants, the Darakhdars.
  • In 1774, twelve of these powerful men came together under Nana Fadnavis to form the Council of Twelve Ministers, the Barbhai Mandal.
  • The Peshwa's secretariat at Poona was the Hazur Daftar. Around 200 people worked there; all records important for governance, including financial records, were kept there.
Teacher's board

Barbhai = "12 Bros" ("Bar" underlined on the board). Power drifted downwards: as the Chhatrapati weakened after Shahu 19-49, it moved to the Peshwa, exactly like the British monarch's power moving to the PM and Council of Ministers as democracy grew.

Memory hook

Bar-bhai: 12 Bros in '74.

1674 crown, 1774 Twelve: exactly a century apart.

Decode: Barbhai Mandal (Council of Twelve, "12 Bros") formed in 1774 by twelve Darakhdars (the Peshwa's own senior civil servants) under Nana Fadnavis; the Hazur Daftar = the Peshwa's secretariat at Poona, about 200 staff. Shivaji 74-80 was crowned in 1674.

Why it sticks: the "74" repeats exactly 100 years apart (1674, 1774).

4.4 Provincial and village administration

  • Within Svarajya the territory was divided into Sarkars (provinces), each under a Governor. Notably, the office of Governor was auctioned.
  • There were checks: many hereditary officers had dual reporting lines, to the Governor and directly to the Peshwa. The Governor's accounts submitted to the Peshwa were tallied against those submitted independently by the Deshmukhi directly to the Peshwa. This cross-checking prevented Governors from manipulating revenue figures.
  • At the lowest level villages functioned as self-contained units. Each was under a Patil, who held all powers (financial, executive, judicial) including revenue collection, and reported directly to the Peshwa. Village accounts were kept by Kulkarnis.
  • Both Patils and Deshmukhi (revenue collectors) held heritable Vatan rights.
Teacher's board

Note the Mughal terminology: a Sarkar = a District. The Governor got a share of LR during his term, so he recovers what he paid in the auction and makes some profit. Deshmukhs collected LR and kept a 10% commission.

4.5 The judicial system

  • In Svarajya the final judicial decision rested with the Peshwa; in Samrajya, with the respective Maratha Sardar.
  • There was a hierarchy of courts from the Panch at the village level up to the Peshwa. The Peshwa, as far as possible, respected the decisions of the Panchs.
  • The law used was from the Dharma Shastras. The policing was as efficient as the London Police of that era, with a very good information network. Crucially there was a rule of law: even the highest authorities could be and were imprisoned for violations.

4.6 Sources of revenue

  • Land revenue was the primary source, fixed on the basis of the quality of soil, irrigation facilities and the crop sown.
  • Beyond this: (1) customs duty and octroi on trade; (2) judicial proceeds: Nazrana paid by the winning party and Jurmana paid by the losing party; (3) fees from permits and licences for cutting wood, manufacturing drugs and private minting of coins.
  • To support peasants, dams and canals were built and loans given at low interest. The Deshmukhi and Patils were kept under check so they would not oppress the peasantry.
Teacher's board

Octroi = taxes on local / domestic trade; customs = international trade. The Maratha LR assessment used proper field surveys, like MQK's in Bengal (1717-27) (see Chapter 3). Canals serve two purposes: transport (e.g. the Suez Canal) and irrigation.

5 · Shahu 19-49's release (1707): was the Mughal "pacifist policy" really pacifist?#

  • After the death of Aurangzeb 58-07 it is often said the Mughals adopted a pacifist policy towards adversaries, including the Sikhs and Marathas, the policy under which Shahu 19-49 was released in 1707.
  • This is a flawed interpretation. What emerged was pragmatic real-politik: reconciliation plus opportunism plus divide-and-rule. The Mughals were weakened and relied more on diplomacy, but the diplomacy included conspiracies and manipulation. It was nominal pacifism with plausible deniability for hostile acts by Mughal Governors.
  • The Sikh case (1707-08): in 1707 Bahadur Shah (1707-12) granted Guru Gobind Singh 76-08 a high Mansab and promised him Anandpur. Very soon after, the Faujdar of Sirhind, Wazir Khan, had the Guru assassinated in 1708 at Nanded, Maharashtra, where the Guru had accompanied Bahadur Shah. No action was taken against Wazir Khan and Anandpur was never handed over.
  • Note from the handout: Sirhind was a Sarkar (a district) within the Lahore Suba, and a Faujdar held military, judicial and land-revenue functions over a district. He was not a Governor, as some books incorrectly describe him, though Wazir Khan had much greater influence than a typical Faujdar.
  • The Maratha case (1707 onwards): Shahu 19-49's release was a gesture of reconciliation, yet the Mughals did not stop attacking the Marathas, and his release triggered a civil war with Tarabai. The Mughals encouraged divided loyalties among Maratha Sardars and Deshmukhs, who kept switching sides between Shahu 19-49, Tarabai and the Mughals. This was shrewd diplomacy, not pacifism.
Memory hook

Mughals ruled by ROD.

Reconciliation, Opportunism, Divide-and-rule: pragmatic real-politik, not pacifism.

Decode: after Aurangzeb 58-07, R-O-D = reconciliation plus opportunism plus divide-and-rule, "nominal pacifism with plausible deniability". Teacher: why release Shahu 19-49? To keep better relations with a rival, and because a Maratha civil war (1707-19) would weaken the enemy from within.

Why it sticks: a ROD in the hand, not a dove on the flag.

6 · Tarabai and the Maratha civil war (1707-1719)#

6.1 Why the civil war broke out

  • After Rajaram 89-00's death from TB in 1700, Tarabai stepped in as regent for infant Shivaji II. This arrangement held the Maratha state together for seven years.
  • In 1707 the Mughals released Shahu 19-49, the elder son of Sambhaji 80-89. This triggered a civil war between Shahu 19-49 and Tarabai that lasted till 1719. Sardars and Deshmukhs kept switching sides between Shahu 19-49, Tarabai and the Mughals, which the Mughals deliberately encouraged.
Teacher's board

By 1705 the Marathas had begun winning against the Mughals, and the credit goes to Tarabai (1700+).

6.2 Treaty of Warna 1731: the family settlement

  • The dispute was finally settled by the Treaty of Warna (1731). Shahu 19-49 gave Kolhapur to Sambhaji II, the son of Rajaram 89-00 from his second wife Rajasbai, who had taken control from Shivaji II around 1714.
  • From this point there were two Chhatrapati dynasties: Shahu 19-49's line at Satara and Sambhaji II's line at Kolhapur.
Memory hook

Warna 1731: two thrones from one family.

Satara for Shahu 19-49 (S for S); Kolhapur for the second wife's son, Sambhaji II ("second son, second seat").

Decode: Treaty of Warna (1731): Shahu 19-49 gave Kolhapur to Sambhaji II, son of Rajaram 89-00 by his second wife Rajasbai (in control since about 1714); two Chhatrapati dynasties, Satara and Kolhapur. Later: Ramraja (Rajaram II) 1749-77 ceremonial; Satara ended with EIC annexation in 1848; Kolhapur merged in 1947.

Why it sticks: one treaty, one split house: Satara and Kolhapur.

6.3 After Shahu 19-49: the Chhatrapati becomes ceremonial

  • After Shahu 19-49 his adopted son Ramraja (Rajaram II) ruled 1749-77. From here the Chhatrapatis became largely ceremonial, like a powerless President. Real power had moved to the Peshwa (section 8).
  • The last Chhatrapati of the Satara line was Shahaji (1839-48), when the EIC annexed Satara. Kolhapur survived much longer, as a Princely State from 1858 until its merger into India in 1947.

8.2 Chauth: one-fourth, externally collected

  • Both Chauth and Sardeshmukhi were tribute systems that existed even before the Marathas.
  • Chauth = one-fourth (25%) of the revenue paid by a protected state to the Marathas in exchange for military protection. It was also a deliberate tool of foreign policy: the annual payment weakened the protected state economically and militarily over time. When it could no longer pay, the Marathas annexed it. Chauth thus functioned as a tool of empire-building.

8.3 Sardeshmukhi: an additional 10%, internal and external

  • Sardeshmukhi was an additional levy of 10% of land revenue, collected over and above the regular land revenue. Deshmukhs collected Sardeshmukhi from the ryots and deposited it to the Chhatrapati. Shivaji 74-80 declared himself Sardeshmukh, the head of all Deshmukhs, so payment by the Deshmukhs was a symbol of their recognition of the Chhatrapati's authority.
  • When extracted from states outside the Maratha Empire, Sardeshmukhi served as a symbol of Maratha authority being recognised by that state.
  • Important distinction: Chauth was collected only externally, while Sardeshmukhi could be collected both internally and externally.
Memory hook

Chauth = Chaar, one door; Sardeshmukhi = two doors.

Chauth is a quarter (25%), collected only outside; Sardeshmukhi is an extra 10%, collected inside and outside; together 25 + 10 = 35.

Decode: Chauth = one-fourth of revenue paid by a protected state for military protection (external only; a tool of empire-building); Sardeshmukhi = additional 10% (Deshmukhs deposit it to the Chhatrapati; Shivaji 74-80 declared himself Sardeshmukh), internal and external.

Why it sticks: "Chauth - Chaar (four) - quarter"; Chauth has one door (outside), Sardeshmukhi has two.

8.4 The teacher's boards: how Chauth and Sardeshmukhi work

Teacher's board

Prelims. Tributes are of two kinds: Chauth and Sardeshmukhi (1/10). Other names for tribute we have seen earlier: Nazrana, Abwab, Peshkash.

Chauth: on states outside the Maratha Empire, 1/4 of the external state's annual revenue. "If paid, Marathas will not attack" (protection from Maratha attack). Steps: (1) the Marathas defeat the external state; (2) in the peace treaty the external state agrees to pay Chauth.

Example

Sardeshmukhi internally (the teacher's example): a Deshmukh collects Rs 100 as land revenue from ryots. His commission = Rs 10; Rs 90 goes to the state. Sardeshmukhi = 10% of the Deshmukh's Rs 10 = Rs 1 to the Chhatrapati. So the state or Chhatrapati gets Rs 90 + Rs 1 = Rs 91.

Sardeshmukhi externally: after defeating an external state, the peace treaty makes it pay Chauth + Sardeshmukhi = 25% + 10% = 35% of its annual revenue.

Note

The teacher's worked example applies the 10% to the Deshmukh's Rs 10 commission when it is collected internally (the Deshmukh is paying respect to the Chhatrapati). Both are in the class material, so learn the table wording and the Rs 100 / Rs 10 / Rs 1 / Rs 91 example for the internal case.

8.7 How the Peshwa office became hereditary; the power shift and Sangola 1750

  • The Peshwa's office was originally not hereditary. Balaji Vishwanath 13-20, the sixth Peshwa, changed this in practice. His son Baji Rao 20-40 became the next Peshwa, then his son Balaji Bajirao 20-40 / Nana Saheb (1740-61) followed. Once this custom held across three generations, the office became hereditary in effect.
  • Shahu 19-49 was an effective ruler but progressively delegated more and more power to the Peshwa. After his death a crisis emerged when Tarabai claimed that Shahu 19-49's adopted son Ramraja was not her grandson, as had been stated at the time of adoption.
  • To prevent the state being left without a Chhatrapati, Peshwa Nana Saheb (1740-61) backed Ramraja, but made him sign the Treaty of Sangola in February 1750, which transferred all real power from the Chhatrapati to the Peshwa.

9 · Wars, treaties and territories up to the Third Battle of Panipat (1761)#

9.1 The 1719 turning point: alliance with the Sayyid Brothers

  • 1719 was a turning point. Peshwa Balaji Vishwanath 13-20 allied with the Sayyid Brothers, the kingmakers of the Mughal court. Together they removed Farrukhsiyar 13-19 and placed Muhammad Shah 19-48 on the throne as a puppet.
  • In return the Marathas secured three concessions:
  • The Mughals formally recognised Maratha autonomous status in Maharashtra.
  • The Marathas received the Chauth of Malwa and Gujarat.
  • They gained the right to collect Chauth and Sardeshmukhi from six Mughal provinces of the Deccan: Khandesh, Hyderabad, Aurangabad, Berar, Bidar and Bijapur. Since Bijapur was later merged into Golconda, some sources name Golconda as the sixth instead. The Nizam's Hyderabad State was later constituted from precisely these six erstwhile Mughal provinces.
  • This did not last. In 1720 the Nizam (1724-48), Governor of Hyderabad Suba, defeated the Sayyid Brothers. Muhammad Shah 19-48 became a joint puppet of the Irani and Turani factions of Mansabdars. When the Nizam established Hyderabad Suba as an independent successor state in 1724, he refused to pay Chauth and Sardeshmukhi to the Marathas.
Memory hook

10.3 Impact of the defeat

  • Military losses: Sadasiv Rao Bhau and Vishwas Rao died fighting. Nana Saheb had not fought (someone was needed to guard other frontiers in the south); he arrived with reinforcements when the battle was already lost and died within weeks. The Marathas lost 50,000 men in total, combatants and non-combatants.
  • Factionalism deepened: young, inexperienced Peshwas followed one another: the young but brilliant Madhav Rao 61-72, then the incompetent Narayan Rao 72-73, then the infant Madhav Rao II 74-95.
  • Abdali too paid a heavy price: serious losses and an anti-Abdali revolt among his own generals back in Afghanistan. He went home with his loot but could not consolidate any lasting presence in India.
  • The deeper consequence: a power vacuum at the centre. Panipat did not decide who would rule India; it decided that neither the Marathas nor Abdali would. The vacuum was eventually filled by the British EIC, which defeated the regional Indian states one by one.

12 · Why couldn't the Marathas establish all-India rule?#

The Marathas came closer than any other power in the 18th century to an all-India empire, but the reasons they never crossed that threshold are structural:

  1. Tribute, not annexation. Often after a victory they limited themselves to Chauth and Sardeshmukhi rather than annexing and establishing direct rule. Extracting tribute is not ruling. Ruling means making and implementing policies through your own administration on the ground; for one central authority to rule effectively, power must be centralised: one country, one ruler, one law, rule of law.
  2. Weak centre, strong Sardars. The centre was never strong enough and factionalism weakened it further; the Sardars had always been autonomous. Shivaji 74-80 never gave Jagirs to his officers and Shahu 19-49 never gave Vatan Jagirs to military commanders, so ambitious Sardars seeking their own principalities ventured out on their own to build Samrajya. In territories brought under Maratha control through the Sardars' own efforts, the Chhatrapati's authority would naturally be weak.
  3. The Treaty of Sangola 1750. As power shifted down to the Peshwa, the Sardars felt even less constrained and factionalism grew; after 1761 a succession of weak Peshwas accelerated this.
  4. Power was not properly centralised even in Svarajya: local assemblies of Vatan right-holders (Deshmukhs and Patils) asserted themselves and pressured the Peshwa.
  5. In Samrajya, effective centralised rule did not exist at all in many areas: certain areas were under a Maratha Sardar militarily, but no effective government machinery was present on the ground.
Teacher's board

"Rule" = administration on the ground: making and implementing laws by the King (= the Centre). Control, tick; Rule, cross: not 100%. Even in Svarajya the local assemblies of Vatan-holders show a "democratic spirit" (smiley) but not a "strong centre" (sad face).

  1. Shivaji 74-80: born 19 Feb 1630; Shahji and Jijabai; Dadaji Kondadev; first fort 1646; Maval; Bagh Nakh 1659; Shaista Khan 64-88 1663, Surat; Purandhar 1665 (23 of 35 forts, Sambhaji 80-89 mansab 5,000); Agra 1666; coronation Raigarh 1674; died 1680.
  2. Army 30-40k light cavalry, 240 forts. Shivaji 74-80 paid cash, no Jagirs; Shahu 19-49 gave Tankha Jagirs (Saranjam), never Vatan. Saranjam temporary, Vatan hereditary (Patil, Deshmukh).
  3. Navy: 20 ships 1657, 200 by 1664, peak 700; Kolis and Bhandaris; Mayanak Bhandari, Daulat Khan; French at Rajapur 1668; Ghurab, Galbat, Shibar, Pal; Jaladurg: Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg, Suvarnadurg; Bombay Presidency 1687.

  5. Succession: Shivaji 74-80, Sambhaji 80-89 (killed 1689), Rajaram 89-00 (TB 1700), Tarabai regent for Shivaji II, Shahu 19-49 (released 1707, civil war to 1719), Warna 1731 (Satara and Kolhapur), Ramraja 1749-77, Shahaji last Satara (1848).
