Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • Prelims 1995: pair each Sardar with his capital: Gaikwad = Baroda, Holkar = Indore, Scindia (Shinde) = Gwalior, Bhonsle = Nagpur
  • Prelims 2010: the immediate reason for Abdali's invasion and the Third Battle of Panipat: to avenge the Marathas' expulsion of his son Timur Shah from Lahore (March 1758), not Maratha expansion in general
  • Boxed by the teacher for Prelims: Chauth (25%, external only) against Sardeshmukhi (an extra 10%, internal and external), and the three Maratha gains of 1719 from the Sayyid alliance
  • Figures to hold: the Chauth split Mokasa 66, Babti 25, Sahotra 6, Nadgaunda 3; the Ashtapradhan's eight posts; the eight treaties from Purandhar 1665 to Salbai 1782

Mains hooks

  • Mains 2014: why have so many empire-shaking battles been fought near Panipat? (the gateway to Delhi from the Khyber Pass, not the town; geography; the six battles from Thanesar 1014 to Panipat 1761)
  • Boxed by the teacher for Mains: why could the Marathas not establish all-India rule? (tribute, not annexation; weak centre, strong Sardars; Sangola 1750; Svarajya not centralised; no machinery on the ground in Samrajya)
  • Why Abdali won at Panipat in 1761: alliance, logistics, terrain, firepower, leadership and factionalism (apply the teacher's checklist for victory in any war)
  • The Maratha Confederacy as the chapter's example of a weak centre with strong provinces, and Sangola 1750 as the moment real power shifted down

Whenever real power shifts down to a lower authority, factionalism rises and everything depends on the personality of that authority: Sangola 1750 handed the Peshwa the power, and Panipat 1761 showed the cost.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: One strong man builds Svarajya with guerrilla war, a navy and forts; after him a weak centre lets the Sardars build Samrajya and live on Chauth. The Peshwa takes the real power (Sangola 1750), factions grow, and Abdali beats a divided Maratha camp at Panipat (1761); the vacuum is filled by the EIC.

  1. A boy from the Maval. Shivaji 74-80 Bhonsle is born on 19 February 1630 to Shahji (Jagirdar of Poona, a General and Mansabdar in Bijapur) and Jijabai. Guided by Dadaji Kondadev, he takes his first fort at 16, in 1646, working from the hilly, forested Maval region with local warriors and peasants.
    → But a boy with a few forts must beat much bigger armies, so he needs a method.
  2. Guerrilla war and the Purandhar squeeze. From 1657 he attacks oppressive Bijapur, and in 1659 he kills Afzal Khan with the concealed Bagh Nakh and a Bichhwa. In 1663 he defeats Shaista Khan 64-88 and sacks Surat, so Aurangzeb 58-07 sends Jai Singh 99-43, who cuts off supplies and in 1665 besieges Purandhar. To spare his soldiers and people Shivaji 74-80 signs the Treaty of Purandhar: 23 of 35 forts go, Sambhaji 80-89 becomes a Mansabdar of 5,000, and he backs the Mughals against Bijapur.
    → Now a man who has survived the squeeze must turn a raiding band into a State.
  3. Crown, army and navy. In 1666 he and Sambhaji 80-89 are insulted and imprisoned at Agra, and escape disguised as Sadhus. In 1674 he is crowned at Raigarh as Chhatrapati, declaring himself independent of the Mughals, and Svarajya covers the Marathi-speaking region. His army of 30,000 to 40,000 light cavalry holds 240 forts; his navy grows from 20 ships in 1657 to 200 by 1664 (peak 700), guarded by Jaladurg sea-forts, and he pays officials in cash, never in Jagirs.
    → But a State also needs ministers, officers and courts.
  4. How the Chhatrapati ruled. He governs with the Ashtapradhan, eight ministers headed by the Peshwa as Prime Minister, like a British monarch with a Privy Council. Under the Peshwa sit the Darakhdars, and in 1774 twelve of them form the Barbhai Mandal under Nana Fadnavis. Provinces (Sarkars) have Governors whose office is auctioned, cross-checked by the Deshmukhi, while villages run under a Patil and justice climbs from the Panch to the Peshwa.
    → Yet the whole machine hung on one strong man, and he died in 1680.
  5. A troubled succession. Sambhaji 80-89 is captured by Aurangzeb 58-07 and killed in 1689 after weeks of torture, and Rajaram 89-00 dies of tuberculosis in 1700, aged 30. His widow Tarabai rules as regent for the infant Shivaji II until 1707, when the Mughals release Shahu 19-49, the elder son of Sambhaji 80-89. The release triggers a civil war that lasts until 1719, while Sardars and Deshmukhs keep switching sides, which the Mughals encourage.
    → So the question is who wins, and which side has the better minister.
  6. Shahu wins, the Sardars rise. The sixth Peshwa, Balaji Vishwanath 13-20, wins Brahmin banking families and the new Sardars for Shahu 19-49 and persuades Admiral Kanhoji Angre to switch in 1714. The Treaty of Warna (1731) splits the house into Satara and Kolhapur. Four Sardars become kingmakers, Gaikwad at Baroda, Holkar at Indore, Scindia at Gwalior and Bhonsle at Nagpur, so Svarajya (inside Maharashtra) is ruled by the Peshwa and Samrajya (outside, from 1719) by the Sardars.
    → Now the Sardars rule far from home, so how do they pay for it?
  7. Chauth and Sardeshmukhi. Chauth is 25% of the revenue of a protected state, paid for military protection; the annual drain weakens that state until it cannot pay and is annexed after a short war, which beats a long, costly war of direct annexation. Sardeshmukhi is an extra 10%, collected both inside and outside. When Sardars collect Chauth abroad they keep 66% (Mokasa), send 25% (Babti) to the Chhatrapati, give 6% (Sahotra) to the Pant Sachiv and 3% (Nadgaunda) to the Chhatrapati's discretion.
    → Chauth feeds the empire, and the Peshwa now bargains with the Mughal kingmakers.
  8. The bargain of 1719 and the decade of growth. In 1719 Balaji Vishwanath 13-20 allies with the Sayyid Brothers, who remove Farrukhsiyar 13-19 and install Muhammad Shah 19-48 as a puppet. The Marathas win autonomy in Maharashtra, the Chauth of Malwa and Gujarat, and Chauth plus Sardeshmukhi of six Deccan provinces, but the Nizam beats the Sayyids in 1720 and stops paying in 1724. A decade of expansion follows (1727-39): Gujarat at 60%, Malwa in 1729, Delhi plundered in 1737 by Baji Rao 20-40, the Treaty of Bhopal in 1738 and Bassein in 1739, then Odisha, Khandesh, Ahmedabad and Alamgir II 54-59 as a puppet in 1754.
    → But as the Marathas grow, power slides from the Chhatrapati down to the Peshwa.
  9. Sangola 1750. Balaji Vishwanath 13-20, then Baji Rao 20-40, then Nana Saheb (1740-61) hold the Peshwa's chair, so after three generations it is hereditary in effect, and Shahu 19-49 keeps delegating to him. When Tarabai says Shahu's adopted son Ramraja is not her grandson, Nana Saheb backs Ramraja but makes him sign the Treaty of Sangola in February 1750, and all real power passes to the Peshwa. The teacher's family table shows the eldest brother taking the father's seat.
    → Now a house with a weaker centre meets a united enemy from the north-west.
  10. Panipat, 1761. From 1756 Abdali invaded, took Lahore and Kashmir and made his son Timur Shah Governor, but in March 1758 Raghunath Rao 73-74 expelled Timur from Lahore, the peak of Maratha power and Abdali's immediate reason to return. Abdali (60,000) joins the Rohillas under Najib-ud-Daula 57-70, the Bangash Pathans under Ahmad Khan (1750-82) and Awadh under Shuja-ud-Daula 54-75, while the Marathas (50,000) under Sadasiv Rao Bhau face them with the Sikhs, Rajputs and Jats neutral and their supply route from Delhi cut at Panipat. Swivel guns pierce their armour, Raghuji Bhonsle refuses to fight, and about 50,000 Marathas die.
    → Neither side rules India afterwards, so the Marathas fight on in decline.
  11. Decline, and why no all-India rule. Young and weak Peshwas follow (Madhav Rao 61-72, Narayan Rao 72-73, the infant Madhav Rao II 74-95), though the Marathas win the First Anglo-Maratha War (1775-82) and Mahadji Scindia 68-94 retakes Delhi in 1771 and builds the Campoo army in 1784. The EIC takes Delhi in 1803 and annexes Svarajya in 1817-19. The Marathas never ruled all India because they took tribute instead of ruling, and their centre was weak: they had control but not rule.

In simple words#

  • Shivaji 74-80 (crowned at Raigarh in 1674) built Svarajya with guerrilla warfare, a light-cavalry army, a coastal navy and sea-forts, and paid his officials in cash instead of giving Jagirs.
  • After his death came a succession crisis (Sambhaji 80-89, Rajaram 89-00, Tarabai) and a civil war with Shahu 19-49 that ran until 1719
  • Shahu won because the Peshwa Balaji Vishwanath 13-20 brought him Brahmin bankers and the new Sardars
  • The Marathas became a confederacy: Svarajya under the Peshwa, Samrajya under four Sardars (Gaikwad, Holkar, Scindia, Bhonsle). They lived on Chauth (25%) and Sardeshmukhi (10%), a tribute that weakened a state until it could be annexed.
  • The 1719 Sayyid deal, then 1727-39 and 1745-54, made them the top power in India
  • The Treaty of Sangola (1750) then moved all real power from the Chhatrapati to the Peshwa
  • At Panipat (1761) Abdali, backed by Awadh, the Rohillas and Farrukhabad, won through alliance, supply, terrain, swivel guns and Maratha factionalism
  • The vacuum was filled by the EIC
  • They never ruled all India because they collected tribute instead of building rule on the ground, and the centre was weak.

1 · Chhatrapati Shivaji 74-80: who he was and why he rose#

Story link

Chapter 1 of the story: a boy of 16 with a fort. Because Bijapur was oppressive and the Mughals were closing in, he rose. Ask: what were the two issues against Bijapur?

1.1 Who he was

  • Shivaji 74-80 Bhonsle was born on 19 February 1630 to Shahji Bhonsle and Jijabai.
  • His father was a Jagirdar of Poona and served as a General and Mansabdar in Bijapur (1489-1686), ruled by the Adil Shahi dynasty.
  • This dynasty, together with the Qutub Shahi (1518-1687), had replaced the Bahmani Sultanate (1347-1527) in the Deccan.
  • Early training came from his guardian Dadaji Kondadev, who shaped him as a soldier and administrator.
  • The deeper inspiration came from his mother Jijabai and his own fierce ambition.
  • He captured his first fort at the age of 16, in 1646.
  • Working from the Maval region, a hilly, forested terrain in today's Pune district running through the Sahyadri range, he gathered an army of local warriors and peasants.

1.2 Guerrilla warfare: the teacher's boards

Teacher's board
  • Tactics = guerrilla warfare:
    • Hide and seek, attack and withdraw, sabotage
    • Not traditional warfare.
  • It uses surprise attacks, speed and geography and creates shock and awe.
  • The only way to defeat a much stronger opponent.
Teacher's board
  • Guerrilla warfare is a tool for positive, neutral or negative goals.
  • Examples:
    • Naxals in India
    • Spain (1808-12) against Napoleon
    • 1857 Revolt (Kunwar Singh, Tatya Tope)
    • Ganimi Kava of the Marathas
    • Alluri Sitarama Raju (1922-24).

1.3 Why he rose against Bijapur and the Mughals

  • From 1657 onwards, Shivaji 74-80 launched systematic attacks on the Kingdom of Bijapur (1489-1686), which followed economically oppressive and religiously discriminatory policies.
  • This was also the period when Aurangzeb 58-07 became Mughal emperor.
  • In 1659, Ali Adil Shah II, Sultan of Bijapur, sent his General Afzal Khan to kill Shivaji 74-80 under the pretext of negotiations.
  • Instead, Shivaji 74-80 killed the seven-feet-tall Afzal Khan using his concealed Bagh Nakh (Tiger Claws) and a Bichhwa (dagger).
  • When Aurangzeb 58-07 launched his campaign for the conquest of the Deccan, Shivaji 74-80 defeated the Mughal General Shaista Khan 64-88 in 1663 and sacked the wealthy port of Surat, which enraged Aurangzeb 58-07.
  • Aurangzeb 58-07 sent General Jai Singh 99-43, who used an encirclement strategy to cut off supplies to Maratha territories.
  • In 1665 Jai Singh 99-43 laid siege to Fort Purandhar (40-50 km southeast of Pune).
Teacher's board
  • Triangle:
    • Aurangzeb 58-07, the Marathas, Bijapur. The Marathas' goal = Swarajya. 1674 = coronation of Chhatrapati Shivaji 74-80
    • 1686 = Aurangzeb 58-07 annexes Bijapur
  • BG Tilak (1890s) used this cultural history (a Maratha against a Mughal general) to arouse nationalism against the British.
Teacher's board
  • Gherao = starve the enemy. Jai Singh 99-43 = a powerful Hindu Mansabdar of the Mughal State.
  • Standard books say Shivaji 74-80 never accepted Mughal sovereignty
  • Correction: the Treaty of Purandhar 1665 was a temporary tactic.

1.4 Treaty of Purandhar 1665: a tactical pause, not a permanent defeat

  • To prevent the massacre of his soldiers and people, Shivaji 74-80 negotiated the Treaty of Purandhar (1665). Under its terms:
  • 1. He surrendered 23 of his 35 forts to the Mughals.
  • 2. He accepted Aurangzeb 58-07 as Emperor but declined a personal mansab for himself.
  • 3. His son Sambhaji 80-89 was made a Mughal Mansabdar with a rank of 5,000.
  • 4. He agreed to support the Mughals in their war against Bijapur.

1.5 The 1674 coronation at Raigarh: Svarajya

  • In 1674, Shivaji 74-80 was coronated at Raigarh as Chhatrapati, legally declaring himself independent from the Mughals.
  • His kingdom, called Svarajya ("own rule"), covered the Marathi-speaking region.
  • (The handout's heading places the headquarters at Satara; see the capital note in section 8.)
  • The Maratha kingdom had thus been founded in the second half of the 17th century, built through wars against both Bijapur and Aurangzeb 58-07.
  • Shivaji 74-80 died of natural causes in 1680.
Teacher's board

Vatan = hereditary. A Mughal Vatan right could be: LR of a Jagir (a Vatan Jagir); collecting LR for a commission (Zamindar); a job such as Qanungo (maintaining land records); or Khudkasht cultivation of a plot.

2 · Military tactics: army, Jagirs, navy and forts#

Story link

Because the Marathas could not match Mughal strength, Shivaji 74-80 built a light, fast army, a coastal navy and sea-forts. Ask: why was copying European deep-sea ships a strategic disaster in the Konkan?

2.1 The army: light cavalry and guerrilla warfare

  • Shivaji 74-80 built a well-organised army of around 30,000 to 40,000 cavalry.
  • He used the mountainous terrain and dense forests to master guerrilla warfare and captured 240 forts, many of them in the Konkan.
  • Their strategy was built on surprise (shock and awe) combined with guerrilla tactics.
  • The major fighting force was a horse-mounted army with light cavalry for faster movement, in sharp contrast to the Mughals, who relied on heavy cavalry (elephants carrying a support system like food).
Teacher's board
  • Heavy cavalry = elephants plus horses carrying supplies, slow.
  • Light cavalry = horses only, food and shelter on the way (by love or force).
  • Speed = the focus of guerrilla warfare.

2.2 The Saranjami system: military land grants

  • Unlike the Mughals, Maratha civil officials did not hold military roles.
  • Shivaji 74-80 never gave Jagirs and paid officials in cash, leading to a centralised polity.
  • Later Chhatrapati Shahu 19-49 gave Tankha Jagirs to military commanders called Saranjams, but never Vatan Jagirs.
  • This prevented military loyalty from getting tied to hereditary land grants and gave the ruler more control.
  • Under this system revenue assignments on Jagirs were given to the Maratha Sardars, the Peshwa (Prime Minister of the Chhatrapati) and various military commanders, each drawing income from designated territories to maintain troops and administration.
  • This led to a fragmented polity as revenue and military power were dispersed rather than centralised.

  • Land = the source of power
  • whoever controls land and its resources is more powerful, hence greater concentration of power under Shivaji 74-80.

2.3 The navy: Father of the Modern Indian Navy

  • Shivaji 74-80 established a naval force of 20 ships in 1657, a full navy of 200 ships by 1664, and the peak Maratha navy reached 700 ships.
  • His doctrine was not about matching the British or Portuguese in deep-ocean warfare.
  • It was a specialised, asymmetric coastal defence strategy designed to:
  • 1. Protect Maratha merchant ships.
  • 2. Secure the Konkan coastline from the Siddis of Janjira.
  • 3. Prevent European powers from dominating Konkan trade routes.
  • He built forts in coastal areas, established naval bases in the Arabian Sea, and focused on intelligence gathering at sea.
  • In 1668 Shivaji 74-80 allowed the French to establish a trading factory at Rajapur.
  • In return he secured high-quality military supplies: European cannons, lead and saltpetre for gunpowder.
  • He is called the Father of the Modern Indian Navy because he relied entirely on indigenous human resources, engineering and strategy, never on European naval architects or shipbuilders.
  • He recruited from local coastal communities, the Kolis and Bhandaris, master seafarers who knew the tides, hidden rocks and shallow creeks of the Konkan.
  • He appointed commanders like Mayanak Bhandari and Daulat Khan.
Teacher's board
  • Saltpetre = potassium nitrate, used in gunpowder.
  • Desi = indigenous tech, human resources, tactics.
  • "Enemy's enemy = friend": the British were the common enemy.
  • The Maratha navy was comparable in tech and strength to European navies ("modern").

2.4 The fleet: tactical ship design

Teacher's board
  • Navy serves Merchant and Military. Patrol = Jaladurg: forts in coastal areas and on islands.
  • The land coast and shallow waters are hiding places for small Maratha boats against British ships.
  • A key insight was tactical ship design.
  • Copying the massive deep-water (blue water) ships of the Europeans would have been a strategic disaster in the shallow estuaries of the Konkan coast.
  • Instead he built a lighter, faster fleet for coastal defence and hit-and-run:

  • To ensure self-reliance, Shivaji 74-80 set up shipbuilding yards, sourcing timber from the teak forests of the Sahyadri.

2.5 The forts: the Jaladurg doctrine

  • His greatest strategic innovation was the Jaladurg doctrine: sea forts on rocky islands or peninsulas along the coast.
  • They provided security to docked vessels, and cannons in these forts and along the coast kept enemy ships at a distance.
  • Key examples: Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg and Suvarnadurg.
  • In Gujarat the EIC so feared the Marathas that they made Bombay their main base for trade, leading to the Bombay Presidency in 1687.
  • The Marathas actively captured ports from the Portuguese: in 1736 they removed the Portuguese and Ethiopian Muslims from the Konkan coast, and by 1739 effectively controlled Salsette and Bassein.
Teacher's board
  • 1668:
    • EIC already in Bombay. 1687: Bombay becomes the Presidency (HQ)
    • Bombay = a group of seven islands.
  • Why shift from Surat:
    • British navy strong, goods better protected at Bombay
    • Surat feared (Maratha) cavalry.
  • Britain became the No. 1 naval power.
  • China:
    • Chaos, kingdom wars and plunder, so goods go through Hong Kong (British, ringed by navy)
    • India: goods go through Bombay (British)

2.6 After Shivaji 74-80: Admiral Kanhoji Angre

  • After Shivaji 74-80 the dominant naval figure was Admiral Kanhoji Angre, called the "Shivaji 74-80 of the Seas", who dominated the Arabian Sea for 30 years in the early 1700s until his death in 1729.
  • Kanhoji fought the British, Portuguese, Dutch, the Sawants (a small kingdom since 1627) and the Siddis or Habshis (people of African ancestry, now found in Gujarat, Karnataka, Andhra and parts of Sindh and Baluchistan in Pakistan).
  • From 1714 to 1724 he frequently attacked the EIC in Bombay with guerrilla warfare at sea.
  • The bigger, heavier British ships could not pursue the small, fast Maratha vessels in shallow waters.
  • The result was a stalemate, and Kanhoji eventually offered peace to the British.
  • The British defeated the Maratha Navy in 1756, one year before the EIC defeated the Bengal Suba at Plassey (1757).

4.4 Provincial and village administration

  • Within Svarajya the territory was divided into Sarkars (provinces), each under a Governor.
  • Notably, the office of Governor was auctioned.
  • There were checks: many hereditary officers had dual reporting lines, to the Governor and directly to the Peshwa.
  • The Governor's accounts submitted to the Peshwa were tallied against those submitted independently by the Deshmukhi directly to the Peshwa.
  • This cross-checking prevented Governors from manipulating revenue figures.
  • At the lowest level villages functioned as self-contained units.
  • Each was under a Patil, who held all powers (financial, executive, judicial) including revenue collection, and reported directly to the Peshwa.
  • Village accounts were kept by Kulkarnis.
  • Both Patils and Deshmukhi (revenue collectors) held heritable Vatan rights.
Teacher's board
  • Mughal terminology: Sarkar = District. The Governor got a share of LR during his term, recovering his auction price plus some profit.
  • Deshmukhs collected LR and kept a 10% commission.

4.5 The judicial system

  • In Svarajya the final judicial decision rested with the Peshwa
  • In Samrajya, with the respective Maratha Sardar.
  • There was a hierarchy of courts from the Panch at the village level up to the Peshwa.
  • The Peshwa, as far as possible, respected the decisions of the Panchs.
  • The law used was from the Dharma Shastras.
  • The policing was as efficient as the London Police of that era, with a very good information network.
  • Crucially there was a rule of law: even the highest authorities could be and were imprisoned for violations.

4.6 Sources of revenue

  • Land revenue was the primary source, fixed on the basis of the quality of soil, irrigation facilities and the crop sown.
  • Beyond this:
    • (1) customs duty and octroi on trade
    • (2) judicial proceeds: Nazrana paid by the winning party and Jurmana paid by the losing party
    • (3) fees from permits and licences for cutting wood, manufacturing drugs and private minting of coins
  • To support peasants, dams and canals were built and loans given at low interest.
  • The Deshmukhi and Patils were kept under check so they would not oppress the peasantry.
Teacher's board
  • Octroi = taxes on local / domestic trade
  • Customs = international trade.
  • Maratha LR assessment used proper field surveys, like MQK's in Bengal (1717-27) (Chapter 3).
  • Canals serve transport (e.g. the Suez Canal) and irrigation.

5 · Shahu 19-49's release (1707): was the Mughal "pacifist policy" really pacifist?#

Story link

Because the Mughals released Shahu 19-49, people call it a pacifist policy. Ask: why does the chapter call it real-politik with divide and rule?

  • After the death of Aurangzeb 58-07 it is often said the Mughals adopted a pacifist policy towards adversaries, including the Sikhs and Marathas, the policy under which Shahu 19-49 was released in 1707.
  • This is a flawed interpretation. What emerged was pragmatic real-politik: reconciliation plus opportunism plus divide-and-rule.
  • The Mughals were weakened and relied more on diplomacy, but the diplomacy included conspiracies and manipulation.
  • It was nominal pacifism with plausible deniability for hostile acts by Mughal Governors.
  • The Sikh case (1707-08): in 1707 Bahadur Shah (1707-12) granted Guru Gobind Singh 76-08 a high Mansab and promised him Anandpur.
  • Very soon after, the Faujdar of Sirhind, Wazir Khan, had the Guru assassinated in 1708 at Nanded, Maharashtra, where the Guru had accompanied Bahadur Shah.
  • No action was taken against Wazir Khan and Anandpur was never handed over.
  • Note from the handout: Sirhind was a Sarkar (a district) within the Lahore Suba, and a Faujdar held military, judicial and land-revenue functions over a district.
  • He was not a Governor, as some books incorrectly describe him, though Wazir Khan had much greater influence than a typical Faujdar.
  • The Maratha case (1707 onwards): Shahu 19-49's release was a gesture of reconciliation, yet the Mughals did not stop attacking the Marathas, and his release triggered a civil war with Tarabai.
  • The Mughals encouraged divided loyalties among Maratha Sardars and Deshmukhs, who kept switching sides between Shahu 19-49, Tarabai and the Mughals.
  • This was shrewd diplomacy, not pacifism.
Teacher's board

Why release Shahu 19-49? For better relations with a rival, and because a Maratha civil war (1707-19) would weaken the enemy from within.

6 · Tarabai and the Maratha civil war (1707-1719)#

Story link

Because Shahu 19-49 returned, he and Tarabai fought a civil war until 1719. Ask: what settled the family dispute in 1731?

6.1 Why the civil war broke out

  • After Rajaram 89-00's death from TB in 1700, Tarabai stepped in as regent for infant Shivaji II.
  • This arrangement held the Maratha state together for seven years.
  • In 1707 the Mughals released Shahu 19-49, the elder son of Sambhaji 80-89.
  • This triggered a civil war between Shahu 19-49 and Tarabai that lasted till 1719.
  • Sardars and Deshmukhs kept switching sides between Shahu 19-49, Tarabai and the Mughals, which the Mughals deliberately encouraged.
Teacher's board

By 1705 the Marathas had begun winning against the Mughals; credit to Tarabai (1700+).

6.2 Treaty of Warna 1731: the family settlement

  • The dispute was finally settled by the Treaty of Warna (1731).
  • Shahu 19-49 gave Kolhapur to Sambhaji II, the son of Rajaram 89-00 from his second wife Rajasbai, who had taken control from Shivaji II around 1714.
  • From this point there were two Chhatrapati dynasties: Shahu 19-49's line at Satara and Sambhaji II's line at Kolhapur.

6.3 After Shahu 19-49: the Chhatrapati becomes ceremonial

  • After Shahu 19-49 his adopted son Ramraja (Rajaram II) ruled 1749-77.
  • From here the Chhatrapatis became largely ceremonial, like a powerless President.
  • Real power had moved to the Peshwa (section 8).
  • The last Chhatrapati of the Satara line was Shahaji (1839-48), when the EIC annexed Satara.
  • Kolhapur survived much longer, as a Princely State from 1858 until its merger into India in 1947.

8.2 Chauth: one-fourth, externally collected

  • Both Chauth and Sardeshmukhi were tribute systems that existed even before the Marathas.
  • Chauth = one-fourth (25%) of the revenue paid by a protected state to the Marathas in exchange for military protection.
  • It was also a deliberate tool of foreign policy: the annual payment weakened the protected state economically and militarily over time.
  • When it could no longer pay, the Marathas annexed it.
  • Chauth thus functioned as a tool of empire-building.

8.3 Sardeshmukhi: an additional 10%, internal and external

  • Sardeshmukhi was an additional levy of 10% of land revenue, collected over and above the regular land revenue.
  • Deshmukhs collected Sardeshmukhi from the ryots and deposited it to the Chhatrapati.
  • Shivaji 74-80 declared himself Sardeshmukh, the head of all Deshmukhs, so payment by the Deshmukhs was a symbol of their recognition of the Chhatrapati's authority.
  • When extracted from states outside the Maratha Empire, Sardeshmukhi served as a symbol of Maratha authority being recognised by that state.
  • Important distinction: Chauth was collected only externally, while Sardeshmukhi could be collected both internally and externally.

8.7 How the Peshwa office became hereditary; the power shift and Sangola 1750

  • The Peshwa's office was originally not hereditary.
  • Balaji Vishwanath 13-20, the sixth Peshwa, changed this in practice.
  • His son Baji Rao 20-40 became the next Peshwa, then his son Balaji Bajirao 20-40 / Nana Saheb (1740-61) followed.
  • Once this custom held across three generations, the office became hereditary in effect.
  • Shahu 19-49 was an effective ruler but progressively delegated more and more power to the Peshwa.
  • After his death a crisis emerged when Tarabai claimed that Shahu 19-49's adopted son Ramraja was not her grandson, as had been stated at the time of adoption.
  • To prevent the state being left without a Chhatrapati, Peshwa Nana Saheb (1740-61) backed Ramraja, but made him sign the Treaty of Sangola in February 1750, which transferred all real power from the Chhatrapati to the Peshwa.
Teacher's board
  • Shahu 19-49 = powerful personality and effective Chhatrapati, BUT he over-delegated to the Peshwa because of trust (the Peshwa also never backstabbed Shahu 19-49).
  • Ramraja (1749-77) was adopted with controversy over his identity, and a weak personality, so his legitimacy as Chhatrapati fell, leading to Sangola 1750: the Chhatrapati purely symbolic, all power with the Peshwa.
  • Whenever real power shifts downward to a lower authority: (1) factionalism increases (Peshwa versus Sardars, Sardars versus Sardars; e.g. Bhonsle did not fight at the Third Battle of Panipat, 1761), and (2) there is more dependence on the lower authority's personality to prevent factionalism.

9 · Wars, treaties and territories up to the Third Battle of Panipat (1761)#

Story link

Because the Sayyid deal of 1719 gave the Marathas rights in six Deccan provinces, the Nizam's refusal in 1724 sparked a decade of expansion. Ask: which three gains of 1719 did the teacher box for Prelims?

9.1 The 1719 turning point: alliance with the Sayyid Brothers

  • 1719 was a turning point. Peshwa Balaji Vishwanath 13-20 allied with the Sayyid Brothers, the kingmakers of the Mughal court.
  • Together they removed Farrukhsiyar 13-19 and placed Muhammad Shah 19-48 on the throne as a puppet.
  • In return the Marathas secured three concessions:
  • 1. The Mughals formally recognised Maratha autonomous status in Maharashtra.
  • 2. The Marathas received the Chauth of Malwa and Gujarat.
  • 3. They gained the right to collect Chauth and Sardeshmukhi from six Mughal provinces of the Deccan: Khandesh, Hyderabad, Aurangabad, Berar, Bidar and Bijapur.
  • Since Bijapur was later merged into Golconda, some sources name Golconda as the sixth instead.
  • The Nizam's Hyderabad State was later constituted from precisely these six erstwhile Mughal provinces.
  • This did not last. In 1720 the Nizam (1724-48), Governor of Hyderabad Suba, defeated the Sayyid Brothers.
  • Muhammad Shah 19-48 became a joint puppet of the Irani and Turani factions of Mansabdars.
  • When the Nizam established Hyderabad Suba as an independent successor state in 1724, he refused to pay Chauth and Sardeshmukhi to the Marathas.
The 1719 bargain

9.2 The Maratha decade of expansion (1727-39)

  • The Marathas responded to the Nizam's refusal with a decade of relentless military expansion:
  • 1. 1727-28: defeated the Mughals in Gujarat and extracted 60% of revenue as annual tribute.
  • 2. 1728: defeated Hyderabad and regained the Chauth and Sardeshmukhi of the Deccan.
  • 3. 1729: captured Malwa and pushed their reach into Rajasthan.
  • 4. 1731: defeated a joint attack by the Nizam and local Gujarat chiefs.
  • 5. 1736: defeated the Portuguese and Ethiopian Muslims on the Konkan coast.
  • 6. 1737: Peshwa Baji Rao 20-40 plundered Delhi itself and held Muhammad Shah 19-48 prisoner temporarily.
  • 7. 1738: defeated a large Mughal army commanded by the Nizam and signed the Treaty of Bhopal (1738), under which the Marathas received the Suba of Malwa (formalising what they had controlled de facto since 1729) and sovereignty over all lands between the rivers Chambal and Narmada.
  • 8. 1739: Treaty of Bassein with the Portuguese led to the annexation of Bassein (Vasai).

9.3 Expansion continues on many fronts (1745-54)

  • 1. 1745-51: Raghuji Bhonsle of Nagpur regularly attacked Bengal Suba and forced Alivardi Khan 40-56 to give up Odisha and pay the Chauth of Bengal and Bihar, paid until Plassey (1757).
  • 2. 1751: Peshwa Nana Saheb attacked Hyderabad and forced Salabat Jung 51-62 to cede Khandesh via the Treaty of Bhalke.
  • 3. 1752: the Marathas took Ahmedabad, completing the annexation of Gujarat.
  • The Peshwa appointed Damaji Gaikwad as Chief and Governor of Gujarat, HQ Baroda.
  • 4. From 1751 the Marathas regularly attacked the Rajputs, extracting annual tributes and interfering in succession disputes, but never fully conquered Rajputana.
  • They also failed to dominate the Sikhs.
  • 5. 1752: the weakened Mughal Empire came under Maratha protection, driven by fear of Afghan invasions.
  • 6. Ahmad Shah Abdali 47-72 launched at least seven invasions between 1748 and 1767.
  • The first, 1748, was checked at the Battle of Manupur, where the Mughal army fought the pitched battle while the Sikh misls harassed his supply lines.
  • 7. 1754: the Marathas interfered in the Mughal succession and installed Alamgir II 54-59 as their puppet.
  • By the mid-18th century the Maratha State had emerged as the top power in India.

Story link

10.1 Sides and background

10.3 Impact of the defeat

  • Military losses: Sadasiv Rao Bhau and Vishwas Rao died fighting.
  • Nana Saheb had not fought (someone was needed to guard other frontiers in the south)
  • He arrived with reinforcements when the battle was already lost and died within weeks.
  • The Marathas lost 50,000 men in total, combatants and non-combatants.
  • Factionalism deepened: young, inexperienced Peshwas followed one another: the young but brilliant Madhav Rao 61-72, then the incompetent Narayan Rao 72-73, then the infant Madhav Rao II 74-95.
  • Abdali too paid a heavy price: serious losses and an anti-Abdali revolt among his own generals back in Afghanistan.
  • He went home with his loot but could not consolidate any lasting presence in India.
  • The deeper consequence: a power vacuum at the centre.
  • Panipat did not decide who would rule India
  • It decided that neither the Marathas nor Abdali would.
  • The vacuum was eventually filled by the British EIC, which defeated the regional Indian states one by one.

11 · After Panipat: decline with moments of strength (1761-1819, about 60 years)#

Story link

Because Panipat left a power vacuum, decline followed, with moments of strength. Ask: which Anglo-Maratha war did the Marathas win, and which one ended Svarajya?

  • 1. 1765: the Marathas defeated Mysore and extracted tribute.
  • 2. 1766 Treaty of Hyderabad: Hyderabad gave the Northern Sarkars to the EIC for an annual payment of seven lakh rupees and protection against both the Marathas and Mysore.
  • The Northern Sarkars (Northern Circars) were initially four districts: Chicacole (Srikakulam), Rajmandri (Rajahmundry), Ellore (Eluru) and MustafaNagar (Kondapalli), with MurtuzaNagar (Guntur) added later as a fifth.
  • Guntur was not transferred to the EIC in 1766.
  • 3. 1771: Mahadji Scindia 68-94 recaptured Delhi from the Rohilla Afghans, who had taken it in 1761.
  • 4. 1775-82 First Anglo-Maratha War: the Marathas defeated the EIC, the only war in this period the EIC lost.
  • 5. 1782 Treaty of Salbai: a Maratha-EIC military alliance.
  • 6. 1784: Mahadji Shinde (Scindia) hired the French military adventurer Benoit de Boigne, who raised and trained the Fauj-i-Hind, also called the Campoo system (each Campoo was an integrated force with its own infantry, own cavalry, own artillery, thus self-sufficient units).
  • It transformed the army from a traditional light-cavalry force into a modern, disciplined European-style infantry-cavalry-artillery machine (similar to Haider Ali 61-82's army (1761-82), also modernised with French help).
  • Other Sardars followed: Holkar hired the Frenchman Dudrenec.
  • 7. 1795 Battle of Kharda: a Maratha victory over Hyderabad.
  • The EIC stayed neutral as the Marathas were still its ally under Salbai 1782.
  • The last battle between Hyderabad and the Marathas.
  • 8. 1798: Hyderabad became the first Indian state to sign a Subsidiary Alliance (also called a subordinate alliance) with the EIC, securing guaranteed protection and ending its vulnerability to Maratha pressure.
  • 9. 1803: the EIC captured Delhi in the Second Anglo-Maratha War.
  • 10. 1817-19 Third Anglo-Maratha War: Svarajya was annexed by the EIC, ending the last Peshwa Baji Rao II 96-18's rule.

12 · Why couldn't the Marathas establish all-India rule?#

Story link

Because the Marathas controlled but did not rule, all-India rule slipped away. Ask: why is extracting tribute not ruling?

  • The Marathas came closer than any other power in the 18th century to an all-India empire, but the reasons they never crossed that threshold are structural:
  • 1. Tribute, not annexation. Often after a victory they limited themselves to Chauth and Sardeshmukhi rather than annexing and establishing direct rule. Extracting tribute is not ruling.
  • Ruling means making and implementing policies through your own administration on the ground
  • For one central authority to rule effectively, power must be centralised: one country, one ruler, one law, rule of law.
  • 2. Weak centre, strong Sardars. The centre was never strong enough and factionalism weakened it further
  • The Sardars had always been autonomous.
  • Shivaji 74-80 never gave Jagirs to his officers and Shahu 19-49 never gave Vatan Jagirs to military commanders, so ambitious Sardars seeking their own principalities ventured out on their own to build Samrajya.
  • In territories brought under Maratha control through the Sardars' own efforts, the Chhatrapati's authority would naturally be weak.
  • 3. The Treaty of Sangola 1750. As power shifted down to the Peshwa, the Sardars felt even less constrained and factionalism grew
  • After 1761 a succession of weak Peshwas accelerated this
  • 4. Power was not properly centralised even in Svarajya: local assemblies of Vatan right-holders (Deshmukhs and Patils) asserted themselves and pressured the Peshwa.
  • 5. In Samrajya, effective centralised rule did not exist at all in many areas: certain areas were under a Maratha Sardar militarily, but no effective government machinery was present on the ground.

What was the Barbhai Mandal?Council of twelve of the Peshwa's ministers under Nana Fadnavis, 1774.
What was the Hazur Daftar?The Peshwa's secretariat at Poona, about 200 staff.
How was a Sarkar Governor appointed and checked?The office was auctioned; accounts were cross-checked against the Deshmukhi's accounts.
Who held all powers in a village?The Patil; Kulkarnis kept the accounts.
Nazrana vs Jurmana?Nazrana paid by the winning party; Jurmana by the losing party.
What was Guru Gobind Singh 76-08's fate in 1708?Assassinated at Nanded by the Faujdar of Sirhind, Wazir Khan, after Bahadur Shah had promised Anandpur.
Why was Shahu 19-49 released in 1707 (teacher)?Better relations with a rival and a Maratha civil war to weaken the enemy from within; not real pacifism.
What was the Treaty of Warna 1731?Shahu 19-49 gave Kolhapur to Sambhaji II: Satara and Kolhapur lines.
Who was Sambhaji II?Son of Rajaram 89-00 by his second wife Rajasbai.
When did the Satara line end?1848, when the EIC annexed Satara (last: Shahaji 1839-48).
Why did Shahu 19-49 win?Balaji Vishwanath 13-20 won him Brahmin banking families and the new Sardars and turned Kanhoji Angre in 1714.
What is a Sardar (teacher)?A local chief, a mini-king with private army and fort; equal to misl, Bundela and Rajput chiefs and Poligars.
What was the Maratha Empire by 1750?A Confederation of five provinces (Peshwa plus four Sardars) with a symbolic Chhatrapati.
Svarajya vs Samrajya?Svarajya: inside Maharashtra, direct rule. Samrajya: beyond Maharashtra from 1719, ruled by Sardars.
Chauth and Sardeshmukhi?Chauth 25% external only for protection; Sardeshmukhi extra 10%, internal and external, recognising the Chhatrapati.
Why was Chauth a tool of empire-building?The 25% drain weakens the state; when it cannot pay, a short war brings easy annexation.
Teacher's harshness scale for treaties?1 Chauth; 2 Chauth plus Sardeshmukhi; 3 Chauth x2 plus Sardeshmukhi; 4 annexation.
How was Chauth divided?Mokasa 66 to Sardars, Babti 25 to Chhatrapati, Sahotra 6 to Pant Sachiv, Nadgaunda 3 at his discretion.
What did Sangola 1750 do?Ramraja signed; all real power to the Peshwa, the Chhatrapati became symbolic.
What happens when power shifts downward (teacher)?Factionalism rises and more dependence on the lower authority's personality.
How did Madhav Rao II 74-95 die?By suicide in 1795, driven by Nana Fadnavis's overbearing control.
Who was Nana Fadnavis?Barbhai leader; modern Chanakya; first to see the EIC's territorial ambitions.
What did the Sayyid alliance of 1719 give the Marathas?Recognised autonomy; Chauth of Malwa and Gujarat; Chauth and Sardeshmukhi of six Deccan provinces.
Six Deccan provinces?Khandesh, Hyderabad, Aurangabad, Berar, Bidar, Bijapur (some sources: Golconda).
What did the Nizam do in 1724?Established Hyderabad Suba independently and refused Chauth and Sardeshmukhi.
What did Baji Rao 20-40 do in 1737 and 1738?Plundered Delhi; Treaty of Bhopal gave Malwa and Chambal-Narmada lands.
Who were the 1745-54 gains made by?Raghuji Bhonsle (Odisha and Chauth of Bengal and Bihar); Nana Saheb (Bhalke 1751); Damaji Gaikwad (Ahmedabad 1752).
Who was Timur Shah?Abdali's eleven-year-old son, Governor of Punjab and Kashmir, removed from Lahore in 1758.
Why did the Sikhs, Rajputs and Jats stay neutral at Panipat?Years of Maratha plunder and tribute.
Why did Awadh join Abdali?It felt strategically encircled by the Marathas.
Outcome of Panipat 1761?Power vacuum; neither the Marathas nor Abdali rule India; the EIC fills it.
What was the Fauj-i-Hind?Campoo system raised by De Boigne for Mahadji Shinde in 1784.
Treaty of Hyderabad 1766?Northern Sarkars to the EIC for Rs 7 lakh a year plus protection against Marathas and Mysore.
Four Northern Sarkars originally?Chicacole, Rajmandri, Ellore, MustafaNagar (Guntur added later).
Why no all-India Maratha rule?Tribute not annexation; weak centre strong Sardars; Sangola 1750; Svarajya not fully centralised; no machinery in Samrajya.
Why so many battles at Panipat (Mains 2014)?It is the gateway to Delhi; open fields, weather, warriors, farm supply.
