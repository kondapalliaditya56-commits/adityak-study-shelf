Constitutional History of EIC's British India (1773–1858)

Class slides: Lecture 18 (slides 44 to 62) and Lecture 19 (slides 1 to 65)Handout pages 166-179

How the British State tightened its grip on the East India Company in six steps, the Regulating Act 1773, Pitt's India Act 1784, and the Charter Acts of 1793, 1813, 1833 and 1853, until the Government of India Act 1858 ended Company rule and put the Crown in charge. Every Act is read through the teacher's two lenses, @Br State (London over the EIC) and @GG (the Governor General's council in India).

Therefore, instead of ending the Company State, the Br State regulated it, that is the EIC's political matters, from 1773.

1 · Why the Br State wanted to regulate the EIC#

Remember

The 1857 revolt is often seen as the reason EIC rule ended. But if one reads the laws Parliament passed from 1773 onwards, the story is clear: the decline of the EIC's autonomy began nearly a century before 1857. Each Charter Act and Regulating Act slowly stripped the EIC of powers and transferred them to the British State. 1857 was the final nail, not the first wound.

1.1 The five drivers

DriverDetail
1. MoneyAfter Plassey (1757) and the ToA 1765, the EIC got both plunder and Bengal's land revenue. In 1767 the Br State demanded 400,000 pounds a year from the EIC, arguing the State had helped the EIC in conquest. The State wanted its share
2. Fear of another AmericaThe American Revolution began in 1765 (the Stamp Act crisis marks the start of the political revolution) and by 1783 the USA was created. The armed War of Independence began in 1775. What if EIC officials, growing rich and powerful in India, did the same?
3. Protecting the taxpayerIn 1772 the EIC went bankrupt and was bailed out with a 1.4 million pound loan from the State ("too big to fail"). The State wanted oversight to prevent reckless war expenditure
4. White Man's Burden betrayedThe Great Bengal Famine of 1770 and the "Nabobs" (corrupt EIC officers who returned to Britain filthy rich) embarrassed the State and went against the civilising mission narrative
5. Career ambitions of EIC menSenior officials such as Robert Clive 57-67 and Warren Hastings 73-85 also wanted a formal relationship with the State: the Crown gave legitimacy and career advancement

1.2 Select Committee of Parliament (1772)

  • Parliament set up a Select Committee to study the EIC's affairs in India and to advise on the relationship between the British State and the EIC with regard to the EIC's territorial possessions. Only political functions were examined (in capitalism the State does not interfere in a company's business).
  • Result: the EIC kept its monopoly over trade with India. But the Court of Directors (CoD) in London was now made accountable for the EIC's political functions in India.
  • This led directly to the Regulating Act of 1773.

2 · Regulating Act of 1773#

2.1 @GG: provisions shaping the Company State in India

  1. The Governor of the Bengal Presidency was elevated to Governor-General of the Bengal Presidency (GG/Bengal). He stayed Governor of Bengal but was now a General over the Governors of Bombay and Madras in specific matters.
  2. The Governors of Bombay and Madras were subordinated to GG(Bengal) in matters of war and peace, except in emergency circumstances. They could be suspended if they disobeyed. The logic: high war expenditure caused the 1772 bankruptcy, so the GG was made accountable; and accountability without power is meaningless.
  3. Commercial affairs were untouched. In commerce the Governors stayed independent regional managers.
  4. A Supreme Council of Bengal was created with four councillors (GG + 4). All decisions were by majority vote of the four councillors. The GG had only a casting vote, a tiebreaker. This was a check on any empire-building ambitions of the GG.
  5. Legislative function was given to the GG's Council: it could make laws governing Bengal and on matters of war and peace.
Teacher's board

"GG is not boss in commerce matters" (row 3): in commerce the Governors stayed independent. "[Treaty = Law]" (row 5): in the teacher's reading, a treaty on war and peace is made by law of the GG's Council. The Governor in "Governor-General" = ruler of the Bengal Presidency, in charge of making laws and implementing them; the General = he is senior to G(Bo) and G(Ma) (Lecture 19, slide 27, below).

Teacher's board

Decision making in the Supreme Council. The council is a check against the empire-builder GG. (1) The GG proposes. (2) Only the four councillors C1 to C4 vote. (3) If it is a tie, the tie-breaker (casting) vote is the GG's. So the GG needs the support of at least 2 councillors to get his decision passed (2 for and 2 against is a tie, which he then breaks).

Memory hook

4 votes, 4 Cs, GG waits for a tie.

Decode: RA 1773 council = GG + 4C. Only C1 to C4 vote. GG votes only on a tie (casting vote), so he needs at least 2 councillors with him.

Why it sticks: the GG is the referee, not a player.

2.2 @Br State: provisions shaping the EIC-State relationship

  1. For the first time Parliament's right to regulate the EIC's political affairs was legally recognised. Commercial affairs stayed outside supervision. (Since 1600 the EIC had commercial functions; since 1765 also political functions, civil, military and revenue.)
  2. The GG collects information on commercial and political matters and sends it to the Court of Directors (CoD) in London. The CoD submits information on political matters to the British State.
  3. A Supreme Court in Calcutta: one Chief Justice and three puisne judges ("puny", junior judges), all chosen by the Crown. The first Chief Justice was Sir Elijah Impey (1774). It heard cases involving British-born subjects and their Indian servants in the Bengal Presidency, under British law. Similar courts followed at Madras in 1800 and Bombay in 1823 (earlier cases involving British subjects went to Mayor's Courts): thus 3 Supreme Courts.
  4. To curb corruption, EIC officials were barred from accepting gifts from Indians.
  5. Tenures: Directors of the EIC 4 years, not immediately re-eligible; GG(Bengal) 5 years, re-appointable; Councillors 4 years; all first appointees were named in the Act itself, future ones by the CoD.
Teacher's board

"[not today's SC]": this Supreme Court is not today's Supreme Court of India; it is a British-law court at Calcutta. "(not BY EIC)": the judges were chosen by the Crown, not by the EIC, so the court was the State's own instrument. "Hastings 73-85 [GG's terms]": the first GG's 5 years is the tenure on the slide.

2.3 Impact of the 1773 Act

  1. The Supreme Court was set up at Calcutta in 1774. (The Act was passed in 1773; Warren Hastings 73-85 assumed office as the first GG of Bengal in 1774.) Conflict arose between the Supreme Court and the GG's Council over cases arising from acts done by EIC officials in their official capacity.
  2. Poor supervision: the huge distance between India and London and no modern communication.
  3. Centralisation of power in the GG began: the journey of India's central executive and central legislature starts here. (The teacher's sketch: three equal G's become one GG with two G's below.)
  4. Bombay and Madras still acted independently because of the vague phrase "emergency circumstances." Example: the Treaty of Surat (1775) was signed by the Governor of Bombay without the GG's approval, leading directly to the 1st Anglo-Maratha War (1775-82).
  5. The GG had no veto over his council. With factional politics this led to paralysis, the indecision problem. Philip Francis, a councillor who wanted to replace Hastings 73-85, kept opposing the GG and wrote complaint letters to London. This contributed to Edmund Burke leading an impeachment trial against Hastings 73-85 in Parliament on corruption charges; he spent his life savings and was acquitted. (Robert Clive 57-67 was likewise tried in Parliament in 1773 and acquitted.)
Memory hook

"Distance, Surat, Francis": three failures of 1773.

Decode: Distance (poor supervision from London); Surat (Treaty of Surat 1775 by Bombay without the GG, then the 1st Anglo-Maratha War 1775-82, the "emergency circumstances" gap); Francis (Philip Francis blocks Hastings 73-85, Burke's impeachment, Hastings 73-85 acquitted).

Why it sticks: three words, three headaches.

Why it sticks: both walked free but lost their peace.

2.4 Amending Act of 1781 (Bengal Judicature Act)

The Amending Act of 1781 (also called the Bengal Judicature Act or Declaratory Act) resolved the jurisdiction conflict. The Supreme Court's jurisdiction was now:

  • All cases within Calcutta's territory.
  • All cases of British subjects in the Bengal Presidency (excluding cases arising from official acts of EIC officials, which went to the GG's Council).
  • All cases related to EIC property in Fort William.
Memory hook

"Calcutta, Britons, Fort": the 1781 three.

Decode: Calcutta territory; British subjects in Bengal (official acts of EIC officials go to the GG's Council); EIC property in Fort William.

Why it sticks: place, people, property.

3.2 @Br State: provisions shaping the EIC-State relationship

  1. The Act used the phrase "British territorial possessions in India" (for the first time) but left it ambiguous whether British India belonged to the EIC or the British State.
  2. Double Government of EIC affairs (1784-1833): political affairs went under the supreme control of the Br State through a new Board of Control (BoC) in London, effectively a ministry for Indian affairs. On commercial affairs the top authority remained the CoD of the EIC; on political affairs it was now the BoC.
  3. The BoC had six members: one Secretary of State (a Cabinet minister) as President of the BoC, the Chancellor of the Exchequer (the Finance Minister) and four members of the Privy Council. (The Privy Council is the Crown's advisory body; earlier it worked like the Mughal Darbar or the Chhatrapati's Ashtapradhan.) Its role: to superintend, direct and control all acts relating to Civil, Military and Revenue government of the British territorial possessions in the East Indies. "East Indies" was deliberate: territories annexed outside India (for example Burma later) also came under the BoC.
  4. A Secret Committee of three directors within the CoD, through which the BoC could send confidential orders directly to India, bypassing the full CoD, for sensitive military and diplomatic matters.
  5. The CoD now had to send all incoming and outgoing letters on India to the BoC. (In 1773 it was information; now original letters: the State's anxiety had grown after 1776-83.)

  1. The EIC retained commerce and patronage in 1784: the right to independently appoint EIC officials, including the Principal Servants (GG, Governors, C-in-C, Councillors). 3.3 @GG: provisions shaping the Company State in India

Aim: resolve the indecision problem of 1773.

  1. GG(Bengal)'s council cut from 4 councillors to 3. One seat was mandated for the Commander-in-Chief (CIC) of the EIC army as an ex-officio ordinary member. (Ordinary = mandated in the Act; ex-officio = member because he is the CIC; extra-ordinary = the CoD chooses whether to appoint him.) Council of 1784 = GG + 2 councillors + CIC.
  2. The GG was given both a regular vote and a casting vote. With just one councillor's support the GG could create a tie and break it in his favour. (Earlier he needed at least two. In effect one councillor was removed and his vote was given to the GG.)
  3. Bombay and Madras Governors were now subordinated to GG(Bengal) in war, peace and revenue (earlier only war and peace). This is how the Permanent Settlement of 1793 could extend to parts of Madras (the Northern Sarkars; see Chapter 16).
  4. Responsibilities of the GG and the Governors were more clearly defined, removing the vagueness of "except in emergency circumstances" (RA 1773).
  5. A Council of 3 was created under each of the Governors of Bombay and Madras (the beginning of today's State assemblies and Council of Ministers, without separation of powers).
  6. "Conquest in India is repugnant to the wish, policy and honour of this nation." The Act thus banned imperial expansion, a response to the heavy war expenditure of the 1st Anglo-Maratha War (1775-82) and the 2nd Anglo-Mysore War (1780-84). (The handout's aside: in reality only fiscal decline was repugnant, not empire-building.)
Teacher's board

Why the 1784 council helps the GG. Before (1773): the GG proposes and the votes are 2 for, 2 against, a tie, he uses the casting vote: 3 against 2. After (1784): the council is C1, C2, C3, and the GG now also votes: with only C3 for him and C1, C2 against, the votes are 2-2 (C3 + GG against C1 + C2), a tie, he casts: 3 against 2. So C4's normal vote is given to the GG. The aim, as he writes: to end factionalism and indecision and make the GG more powerful.

3.4 Impact of the 1784 Act and the Amending Act of 1786

  1. Dual control in practice meant no effective control: the GG could play his two masters (CoD and BoC) against each other, given the distance and no modern communication.
  2. The GG still had no veto over his council: if all three councillors opposed him, the indecision problem continued. (The teacher's note: "indecision continued, BUT" the position improved because he needed only one councillor.)
  3. Conflict between the GG and the C-in-C: the decision of war and peace rested with the GG, but the right to use the army rested with the C-in-C.
  4. The Amending Act of 1786 amended Pitt's Act by: (i) giving the GG a veto over his council in extraordinary circumstances; (ii) letting the EIC appoint the same person to both posts, GG and C-in-C, to avoid the rift (the posts were not merged, and it was not compulsory, just permitted). This was done on the insistence of Cornwallis 86-93 (GG 1786-93), who became the first person to hold both posts.

4 · Charter Act of 1793#

4.1 @Br State

  1. "Subjects of the Crown cannot acquire sovereign powers over territory on their own but only on behalf of the Crown." So the EIC was now only a manager or agent of the British State. Sovereignty over British India was with the British State, not the EIC. (However, Mughal sovereignty over the whole of India was accepted until 1858.)
  2. The size of the BoC was decreased: the President was retained with only two junior members under him, who need not be Privy Councillors. This concentrated real authority in the President and reduced factionalism.
  3. The salary of BoC members was now paid by the EIC. (This later connects to the Home Charges under the Drain of Wealth theory of the Moderates, Chapter 27.)
  4. The EIC's charter was renewed for 20 years, and for the first time by Parliament, not the Monarch.
  5. Principal Servants (GG, Governors, C-in-C, Councillors) were appointed by the EIC but only after the approval of the Crown. The State took hold of the head of the Company State in India while its body stayed under the EIC, making a declaration of independence impossible.
  6. The EIC retained commerce and patronage: it independently appointed officials other than the Principal Servants.

5 · Charter Act of 1813: Westernisation begins#

5.1 Context

  1. The narrative for ending the monopoly. Adam Smith's "Wealth of Nations" (1776) argued for a free market (laissez faire), so the monopoly was under intellectual attack since 1776. Liberals, Utilitarians and Evangelists argued that British manufacturers would industrialise India through technology transfer and FDI, fulfilling the White Man's Burden (WMB). There was also heavy lobbying by manufacturers and non-EIC traders. (Teacher: "[Recall: interlopers]", the non-EIC British traders of Chapter 10.)
  2. British national interest. The Industrial Revolution (1750-1850) was progressing; British manufacturers wanted India as a source of raw materials and an export market. It was no longer in the British national interest for a trading company to hold the monopoly over British business with India.
  3. The push to westernisation. Liberals, Utilitarians and Evangelists argued that the idea of a Great Ancient India was a myth and that westernising India fulfilled the White Man's Burden.
Teacher's board

Br national interest in 1813: before and after the Industrial Revolution matured. Till the IR (1750-1850) matured: Britain wanted finished goods (FGs) from India to sell at a profit to the rest of the world, so it wanted a big MNC, the EIC, to buy Indian FG in bulk, therefore monopoly to the trader EIC. Once the IR matured: Britain wanted raw material (RM) from India, not FGs, and India as an export market to sell British machine-made FG, therefore the monopoly of a trader is not in the British national interest.

Memory hook

Decode: Before the Industrial Revolution matured: India's finished goods are bought in bulk by the EIC (monopoly makes sense). After: Britain wants India's raw material and sells its own machine-made goods into India (monopoly of a trader does not).

Why it sticks: goods go from India first, then goods go into India.

Teacher's board

Westernisation has three arms: polity and administration (west like), economy (west like: machines, factories of manufacturers, machine-based industry) and society (education, and customs and religion, which leads to social reforms).

  5. The Charter was renewed for 20 years.

6.2 @GG

Teacher's board

GG(Bengal) versus GG(India) in one word-play. 1773: "Governor" = ruler of the Bengal Presidency, in charge of making laws and implementing them; "General" = senior to the Governors of Bombay and Madras. 1833: "General" is the same as before; "Governor" is now the ruler of all British India (Bengal, Bombay, Madras), therefore he is now a true PM of British India, and the law-making powers of the Governors of Bombay and Madras are taken away.

  1. A landmark: GG(Bengal)-in-Council was elevated to GG(India)-in-Council. As the EIC's territory expanded and Britishers entered India in larger numbers after 1813, uniform laws and administration across British India became necessary: one country, one law, one executive (like GST today or the Napoleonic Code of 1804). (Teacher: "implementation of law.")
  2. The GG(India) was made Executive and Legislative head of British India: empowered to make and administer laws for the whole of British India, equally applicable to Indians and Britishers.
  3. The legislative powers of the Governors of Bombay and Madras were taken away (restored by the Indian Councils Act 1861, Chapter 25).
  4. Centralisation in the GG peaked in 1833; decentralisation to the provinces began in 1861 and peaked in 1935 (the journey of federalism and Centre-State relations).
  1. One Law Member (Thomas Macaulay) was added to the GG(India)'s council to draft uniform laws. This introduced the grain of separation of the executive and legislative functions at the council level (not at the GG's level, he still headed both). The journey of a separate legislature begins here.

9 · Conquest in India: policy versus drift#

Note

No class slides on this section until Lecture 19, slides 41 to 43 (typed "MMP" slides without ink); the handout gives the same points. It is the "Br State responsibility for conquest" Mains angle.

9.1 No official policy of conquest

On the surface, Britain had no official policy of conquest. Imperial expansion was largely driven by Sub-Imperialism: the policies, actions and personal ambitions of senior EIC officials in India, many of whom built political careers back home after their India tenures. Pitt's India Act (1784) explicitly banned conquest in India, a response to the heavy expenditure of the 1st Anglo-Maratha War (1775-82) and the 2nd Anglo-Mysore War (1780-84).

9.2 British State responsibility

However, the British State was responsible for conquest, for these reasons:

  • a) Tools of conquest given by the State: the EIC's Charter gave it the right to wage war, acquire and administer territory; the Charter was issued first by the Crown and later by Parliament.
  • b) Military aid at critical junctures: Plassey (1757) was won with the help of the Royal British Military (Chapter 10); when the 1857 revolt nearly overthrew Company rule, the arrival of the Royal Military in October 1857 turned the tide. The State helped both the initiation and the survival of conquest.
  • c) The State financed the Company: trade monopoly over Britain's East Indies trade (1600-1813). King Charles II 60-85 rented Bombay (received as a dowry from the Portuguese in 1661) to the EIC for just 10 pounds a year in 1668. Thomas Roe was sent as a State representative to Jahangir 05-27's court to lobby. The State bailed out the EIC with a 1.4 million pound loan in 1772.
  • d) Follow the money trail: over 1,50,000 pounds to the Monarch in the 17th century for Charter renewals; in 1698 Parliament gave the monopoly to a "New Company" because it offered a 2 million pound loan against the EIC's 7,00,000; in 1767 the EIC had to pay 4,00,000 pounds a year; many Parliamentarians and even Queen Elizabeth I 58-03 were shareholders receiving dividends.
  • e) The State controlled the empire-builders: after 1784 Principal Servants (the sub-imperialists themselves) could be appointed only after Crown approval, and binding orders could go directly to the GG (empire-builders were, in effect, favourites of the State).
  • f) The EIC as a foreign-policy tool: Britain dominated world trade through the EIC, as large MNCs today serve a State's foreign policy (the handout's examples: Google or private banks for sanctions on Russia, Airtel in Africa). The 1st Afghan War (1838-42) fought by the EIC was part of the Great Game between Britain and Russia.

Recall sheet#

Read once, hide it, then rebuild the chapter from memory.

  1. Two lenses: @GG = structure and functions of the Company State in India (Br India); @Br State = EIC-Br State relationship (CoD of the EIC and the Br State, Britain). The 1857 revolt was the final nail; each Act stripped the EIC of power.
  2. Why the State did not end the Company State: two incomes (trade, land revenue), the burden of ruling borne by the EIC, indirect gain in empire and geopolitical power; so it regulated the political (CMR) matters from 1773. Commerce since 31 Dec 1600; political since 1765.
  3. Five drivers: Money (1767, 400,000 pounds a year); America (1765-83, USA 1783); Taxpayer (1772 bankrupt, 1.4 million pound loan); White Man's Burden betrayed (GBF 1770, Nabobs); Careers (Clive 57-67, Hastings 73-85). Select Committee 1772: trade monopoly kept, CoD made accountable for political functions.
  4. Regulating Act 1773 @GG: GG(Bengal) over Bombay and Madras in war and peace (except emergency; can be suspended); commerce untouched; Supreme Council GG + 4, majority vote, GG casting vote only (needs 2 councillors); law-making for Bengal and war and peace.
  5. RA 1773 @Br State: Parliament's right to regulate political affairs recognised; GG to CoD to State information; SC Calcutta (1 CJ + 3 puisne, by the Crown, Impey 1774; Madras 1800, Bombay 1823); no gifts; tenures 4 (Directors), 5 (GG), 4 (Councillors).
  6. RA 1773 impact: SC versus GG's council; distance; centralisation; Surat 1775 and the 1st Anglo-Maratha War; no veto, Philip Francis, Burke's impeachment, Hastings 73-85 acquitted (Clive 57-67 tried 1773). Amending Act 1781: SC covers Calcutta, British subjects in Bengal, EIC property in Fort William.
  7. Pitt's India Act 1784 @Br State: "Br territorial possessions" (ambiguous); BoC of 6 (SoS President, Chancellor of the Exchequer, 4 Privy Councillors) over Civil, Military, Revenue in the East Indies; Secret Committee of 3; original letters to BoC; Double Government 1784-1833; Dual Control 1784-1858.
  8. PIA 1784 @GG: council GG + 2 + CIC (ordinary, ex-officio); GG regular + casting vote (one councillor suffices); Governors under the GG in war, peace and revenue; Councils of 3 under the Governors; "Conquest in India is repugnant..."; Amending Act 1786: veto, GG + C-in-C allowed, Cornwallis 86-93 first.
  9. Firsts: Hastings 73-85 (GG Bengal 1773), Bentinck 28-35 (GG India 1833), Canning 56-62 (Viceroy 1858), Mountbatten (partitioned India), Rajagopalachari (first Indian GG), Rajendra Prasad (President). Double Government (commerce CoD, CMR BoC) versus Dual Government in Bengal versus Subsidiary Alliance.
  10. Charter 1793: Crown sovereignty (agent, manager); BoC = President + 2; BoC salary by EIC; 20 years by Parliament first time; Principal Servants after Crown approval; patronage for the rest; GG can supersede Governors; CIC extra-ordinary (GG + CIC + 3); Cornwallis 86-93 Code (written law, vernacular, Hindu and Islamic laws, PS 1793).
  11. Charter 1813: Adam Smith 1776, Industrial Revolution 1750-1850, raw material and export market; undoubted Crown sovereignty; India trade monopoly ended (Tea, Opium, China kept); missionaries; Rs 1 lakh on education; 20 years; contributed to 1857.
  12. Charter 1833 (St Helena Act): commercial function ended; BoC supervises all administration; President of BoC = Minister for Indian Affairs; GG(India)-in-Council; Governors lose law-making; Law Member Macaulay; Law Commission 1834 (1837); CPC 1859, IPC 1860, CrPC 1861; UCS opened; centralisation peak 1833.
  13. Charter 1853: no fixed term; patronage lost (Civil Services exam, first 1855 London); CCS opened to Indians; Budget to Parliament; 6 Legal Members (4 provincial + CJ + 1 judge); 12 in legislative council; LG of Bengal (Halliday 1854); Rent Act 1859.
  14. Council: 1773 GG + 4; 1784 GG + 2 + CIC (ordinary); 1793 GG + CIC (extra-ordinary) + 3; 1833 executive GG + CIC + 3, legislative + Law Member; 1853 executive GG + CIC + 4, legislative [GG + CIC + 4] + 6 = 12. "Members" = councillors only.
  15. Governor versus LG (post 1858, not imp): presidencies versus provinces; Crown versus GG appointment; aristocrat versus ICS; direct to SoS versus through the GG; council versus sole executive.
