Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • The six PYQs, all single-fact answers: WEMC opposed the revolt (1998); Aitchison's remark = Revolt of 1857 (2000); Kunwar Singh = Bihar, Jagdishpur (2005); Chittor not affected (2005); Tantia Tope betrayed by Man Singh (2006); Governor-General = Canning 56-62 (2006)
  • Opening shots dates (29 March, 8 April, 22 April, 10 May, 11 May, 20 September 1857); the 1856 allowance cut and Awadh annexation; Brown Bess versus Enfield; Lex Loci Act 1850; leader-and-centre pairs
  • GOI Act 1858 and the 1 November 1858 Proclamation; Police Act 1861 chain (IG, SP, SI); Statutory Civil Service (handout 1870 and 1878, Lytton 76-80)
  • Peel Commission: July 1858, March 1859, European Forces (India) Act 1860, ratios 1:2 and 1:3, artillery barred, martial races

Mains hooks

  • Sepoy mutiny plus civil rebellion; the sepoy as a peasant in uniform (the culmination-of-rebellions question)
  • The War of Independence debate: three positions, arguments against (Scam B, Punjab, Bengal, WEMC, loyal princes) and for (Hindu-Muslim unity, Aitchison, federal vision)
  • Why the British won: no money or command, the Delhi mistake, localised revolt, half the army loyal
  • The watershed (teacher's 'Please tell impact of 1857 revolt'): polity, ideology, policing, civil services, army, society

1857 was the hinge: the Company's improvised rule ended, the Crown built a coup-proof army, a Police Raj and a shut door, and that shut door seeded modern nationalism.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Pressed on every side (pay, caste, Awadh, land, religion), the Bengal Army and the dispossessed villages rose together; they lost for lack of money, command and unity; and the Crown that replaced the Company rebuilt the army, police and services to prevent a repeat, shutting the very door that created modern nationalism.

  1. A village in uniform. The Bengal Army is the largest of the three armies, filled with upper-caste sepoys from Awadh and some from Bihar, and Indians far outnumber the British in it. The sepoy is a peasant in uniform: he is paid less than a European, is called Suar, and cannot rise beyond Subedar at 60 to 70 rupees a month.
    → But the British were about to press on every sore at once.
  2. 1856: four blows. To offset war costs the British cut sepoy pay and abolish the allowance for serving outside the home region, a loss of money and of caste. Awadh, the biggest recruiting ground, is annexed on grounds of maladministration and feels like personal betrayal. The Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act is passed, and Canning 56-62 announces that the Mughals will be only 'Princes'.
    → All that was missing was a spark.
  3. The fat on the cartridge. The new Enfield rifle replaces the Brown Bess, and its cartridge, greased with cow and pig fat, must be bitten before loading. With rumours of cow and pig bone dust in flour, and missionaries in cantonments since the Charter Act 1813, sepoys fear a plot against Hindu and Muslim identity. The teacher is clear: the cartridge was only the trigger.
    → And the first sepoy fired.
  4. Barrackpur to Delhi. On 29 March 1857 Mangal Pandey of the 34th BNI fires at European officers; he is hanged on 8 April and Jemadar Ishwari Prasad on 22 April, and the 34th BNI is disbanded. On 10 May Meerut mutinies and on 11 May the sepoys reach Delhi and proclaim Bahadur Shah Zafar 37-57 Emperor of Hindustan. Delhi is held until 20 September, and for the first time in a century the British monopoly of organised violence is broken.
    → Now the villages were waiting.
  5. The village joins. Annexation and the Doctrine of Lapse have stripped rulers and their courts; Mahalwari, disbanded forts and equality before law have stripped the taluqdars of estates, arms and status; high land revenue, moneylenders and courts that side with lenders have ruined the peasants. Cheaper British goods ruin the artisans. Above all, the whole countryside fears for its religion: missionaries 1813, Sati 1829, Lex Loci 1850, Widows 1856.
    → Each region found its leader in the dispossessed.
  6. Lamps in eight hands. Nana Sahib rises at Kanpur, Hazrat Mahal at Lucknow, Khan Bahadur Khan at Bareilly, Lakshmi Bai at Jhansi, Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah at Faizabad, Kunwar Singh at Jagdishpur in Bihar and Zafar at Delhi, with Tantia Tope as the great guerrilla. Lakshmi Bai dies at Gwalior in June 1858; Tantia Tope is betrayed by Man Singh and hanged in April 1859; Zafar is exiled to Rangoon and dies in 1862.
    → Yet eight lamps are not one flame.
  7. Why the flame went out. The rebels have no money, weapons or training, and no central command. The teacher's board shows the mistake: all the sepoys go to Delhi, so the fall of Delhi is the fall of the sepoy mutiny and of the revolt. The revolts are local, with no control of the telegraph; Bombay and Madras stay quiet and Punjabis and Gurkhas help the British.
    → Whatever it was, what do we call it?
  8. Mutiny, war or more? The colonial view says sepoy mutiny; Savarkar (1909) and Marx say war of independence; mainstream historians say far more than a mutiny but without modern nationalism. Against the war: Scam B areas, Punjab, Bengal, the WEMC, loyal princes (Kota the exception), no manifesto, an old-order goal. For more than a mutiny: a common enemy, Hindu-Muslim unity (Aitchison), a federal vision, a mass movement.
    → Whatever the name, Britain could not go back to the Company.
  9. The Crown takes over. The GOI Act 1858 ends the EIC: Queen Victoria is sovereign and a Cabinet-rank Secretary of State replaces the President of the Board of Control. The Proclamation of 1 November 1858 promises tolerance and shelves social reform; the teacher adds no more annexations, with British India holding 63% of the territory. Britain now sees Indians as racially inferior and more authoritarian rule follows.
    → And the state itself was rebuilt: police, services, army.
  10. A Police Raj and a shut door. Canning 56-62's 1st Police Commission of 1860 leads to the Police Act of March 1861: Inspector General, Superintendent of Police, Sub-Inspector, with Indians excluded from important posts. The civil services exam is no longer held simultaneously in India, the age cap is 19 to 23, and the Statutory Civil Service of Lytton 76-80 admits only hand-picked elites: co-option, not inclusion.
    → But the deepest reform was the army.
  11. The coup-proof army. The Peel Commission (appointed July 1858, report March 1859) leads to the European Forces (India) Act 1860, ratios of 1:2 in Bengal and 1:3 in Madras and Bombay, and an Indian ban on artillery. Regiments mix castes and religions, recruitment favours martial races, loyalty to the salt is taught, and the army is kept in barracks and used against peasant revolts and the INM.
    → Yet the shut door had made a new class.
  12. The door that made its own key. The WEMC, schooled in British liberalism, had wanted a share in governance and was locked out. That frustration became the seed of the modern nationalism-based INM, and by 1929 its demands would escalate into Purna Swaraj. 1857 was the hinge: more efficient and more repressive reforms that planted what would later demand independence.

In simple words#

  • 1857 = Sepoy Mutiny + Civil Rebellion: the Bengal Army (largest, upper-caste, Awadh-heavy) rose, and dispossessed elites, taluqdars and peasants joined, all afraid for their religion.
  • The sepoy was a peasant in uniform:
    • racism, the 1856 allowance cut, the Awadh annexation and agrarian ruin hurt him and his family
    • The greased cartridge was only the trigger
  • It began at Barrackpur (29 March 1857), broke out at Meerut (10 May) and reached Delhi (11 May)
  • The British won because the rebels had no money, no command, went all to Delhi and stayed local
  • Historians split three ways: mutiny (colonial), war of independence (Savarkar, Marx), or more than a mutiny but without modern nationalism (mainstream).
  • After 1857 the Crown replaced the Company (GOI Act 1858, Proclamation 1 November 1858), built a Police Raj (1861), narrowed the civil services, and made the army coup-proof (Peel Commission).
  • The shut door backfired: the WEMC, locked out, began the modern nationalism that grew into Purna Swaraj by 1929.

2 · The opening shots#

Story link

Because the grievances had piled up, one rebel's shot was enough. Follow the dates: Barrackpur, Meerut, Delhi.

The opening shots of 1857

  • 29 March 1857:
    • Mangal Pandey, sepoy of the 34th Bengal Native Infantry (BNI), Barrackpur near Calcutta, fires at European officers
    • His fellow sepoys refuse to arrest him
  • 8 April 1857: Mangal Pandey hanged
  • 22 April 1857:
    • Jemadar Ishwari Prasad (who refused to restrain Pandey) hanged
    • The 34th BNI disbanded as collective punishment
  • 10 May 1857: sepoys at Meerut mutiny
  • 11 May 1857: they march to Delhi and proclaim Bahadur Shah Zafar 37-57 (1837-1857, last Mughal emperor) Emperor of Hindustan
  • By end of May:
    • The revolt spreads across the North-West Provinces and Awadh
    • the civil population joins in
  • 11 May to 20 September 1857 in Delhi, and broadly until spring 1858: British rule collapses across a large swathe of north and central India
Key takeaway
  • Banner: for the first time in a century, the British monopoly over organised violence in India was broken.
  • Violence on both sides was enormous.

3 · Why the sepoys mutinied#

Story link

Because the sepoy was a peasant in uniform, every wound of pay, caste, Awadh and land was his own. Ask: which was the cause and which only the trigger?

3.1 Service-related grievances

  • Racism. Indian sepoys earned far less than European soldiers, got smaller pensions, and faced routine slurs: they were called "Suar" (pigs).
  • The highest rank an Indian could reach was Subedar, worth only 60 to 70 rupees a month.
  • Economic. To offset high war expenditure, the British cut sepoy salaries and abolished the allowance for serving outside one's home region in 1856.
  • That allowance mattered doubly: losing it was an economic blow and, for many upper-caste men, a religious insult, since serving far from home or crossing water was seen as causing loss of caste.

3.2 Socio-religious grievances

  • Early on, the British recruited upper castes and respected caste customs: ruling the Indian army the Indian way.
  • This was the ideology of Orientalism: to govern India, work within Indian traditions, at least initially.
  • As the empire expanded and the army became more heterogeneous, respecting caste customs became inconvenient.
  • The British now wanted a uniform army culture where loyalty ran to the employer, not to caste or religion.
  • i) Caste privileges were reduced: food restrictions, religious symbols, and the right to refuse service in polluting regions were curtailed.
  • Ii) Sepoys were forced to serve in Burma, Sindh or Afghanistan, risking loss of caste
  • Those who refused were dismissed or punished.
  • iii) Christian missionaries in cantonments since the Charter Act 1813 created anxiety about forced conversion.
  • Iv) The rumours. Cow and pig bone dust in flour
  • And the new Enfield rifle cartridges (which replaced the Brown Bess musket) were greased with cow and pig fat and had to be bitten before loading.
  • Whether true or not, the rumours landed on fertile ground: the fear that the British were engineering a loss of Hindu and Muslim identity to make conversion easier.
Teacher's board

The whole chain is examinable: Orientalism, expansion, recruitment from other castes, uniform military culture, forced service abroad, loss of caste, punishment, missionaries, Charter Act 1813, cartridge rumours.

3.3 Political grievance: Awadh

  • The single largest source of recruits for the Bengal Army was Awadh.
  • Nearly every family there had a member in the British Indian Army.
  • The annexation of Awadh in 1856 felt like a personal betrayal: their loyalty had cost them their homeland.
Teacher's board
  • The Bengal Army was dominated by sepoys from Awadh, plus some from Bihar
  • upper castes among them: Brahmins, Bhumihars, etc. Starred rule "Bor DnR": do not keep sepoys from a State in the army of that State.
  • Peasant logic:
    • The first livelihood of anyone from a peasant family is being a peasant
    • poor peasants use family labour
    • step 2 he became a sepoy of the EIC army. Sepoy = peasant IN uniform.
  • In any agrarian economy the masses are peasants (AGRI triangle inside the GDP circle).
Teacher's board
  • Undivided Awadh was taken in two bites. 1801: the Doab, Gorakhpur and Rohilkhand. 1856: the rest.
  • Land revenue:
    • Mahalwari 1822 in the 1801 half
    • Summary Settlement (no detailed field survey) in the half annexed in 1856
  • Names:
    • 1801 land = Ceded Districts, renamed NWP 1836
    • then 1877 "NWP and Oudh"
    • 1902 "UP of Agra and Oudh"
    • 1937 = United Provinces.

3.4 Agrarian grievances

  • i) Summary settlements in Awadh fixed LR without proper field surveys, so demands were unreasonable.
  • ii) Peasants unable to pay: lands auctioned by the government, or lost to moneylenders through debt.
  • iii) New British courts backed moneylenders, completing the dispossession.
  • iv) The sepoy was a peasant in uniform: these wounds were his family's wounds.

4 · Why the civil population rebelled#

Story link

Because the same policies ruined the elites, the taluqdars and the peasants, they found leaders in the dispossessed. Ask: who lost what, and who led from where?

4.1 Feudal elites and dispossessed rulers

Teacher's board
  • Civil rebellion = North and Central India, split into LEADERS and FOLLOWERS.
  • Leaders = ex-rajas and Mansabdars, who lost status through annexation and Doctrine of Lapse: political
  • Economic (no jagirs, no Khalisa land)
  • Social (British rule of law, equality before law).
  • Zamindars (Taluqdars) lost status too: economic (Mahalwari: lost milkiyat, ownership), social (equality before law), political (private army and forts, disbanded). Followers = peasants and artisans.
  • Peasants:
    • Already done (agrarian grievances): higher LR, the Rs lender, loss of lands
    • the lender is the "villain of 1857," with the British beside him
  • Artisans:
    • The Industrial Revolution (1750-1850) in Britain
    • Indian economy exposed to industrialising Britain through the Charter Act 1813.
Teacher's board
  • British manufactured goods were cheaper in rupees (MRP) and later better in quality too, against Indian handicrafts.
  • Hence the destruction of Indian industry that rebels also complain about (Section 6.3).
  • Annexations under the Doctrine of Lapse and other policies stripped ruling families of power, and with each fallen ruler the entire dependent court aristocracy fell too.
  • These displaced elites had both motive and, often, remaining private resources to lead a rebellion.

4.2 Taluqdars and big landowners

  • The Mahalwari settlement in the North-West Provinces (half of Awadh had been under SA since 1801) and in Awadh (annexed 1856) transferred land rights to actual cultivators in many cases, stripping Taluqdars of their personal estates.
  • The British also disbanded private forts and armies.
  • Then rule of law, equality before the law, removed their social superiority.
  • Having lost economically, militarily and socially, Taluqdars threw their lot in with the peasants.

4.3 Peasants

  • High LR led to debt to moneylenders, then loss of land through government auctions and moneylender takeovers.
  • Traditional occupancy rights were gone. British courts sided with moneylenders.
  • So peasant anger in 1857 was directed both at the British government and at the moneylenders: both were seen as oppressors.

4.4 Religion: a fear shared across society

  • The whole rural society shared a fear that its religion was under threat. The fear was not invented:
  • i) Since 1813, Christian missionaries had operated in India, at times under police protection.
  • Ii) Socio-religious reforms, however well-intentioned, escalated anxiety: a) Sati Abolition Act, 1829
  • B) Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856
  • C) Removal of Caste and Religious Disabilities Act, 1850, also called the Lex Loci Act (Latin for "law of the place"): it gave inheritance rights to those who converted to another religion, so it incentivised conversion to Christianity.

6.3 Arguments that it was much more than a mutiny

  • Modern nationalism was absent, yes. But something more than narrow self-interest was present:
  • i) Common enemy and common hatred: against annexations, high LR, loss of land, oppression by moneylenders and EIC officials, destruction of Indian industry.
  • ii) The sepoys' own declarations went far beyond personal grievances: high LR, increased Chowkidari tax, rising unemployment of artisans and learned men, toll taxes on public roads, and the declining status of the Indian ruling class.
  • They were, in effect, speaking for all Indians.
  • iii) Hindu-Muslim unity: a shared belief that Hindustan belonged to Hindus and Muslims alike.
  • Aitchison: "In this instance we could not play off the Mohammedans against the Hindus." This unity is why divide and rule became central to post-1857 policy.
  • iv) The rebels wanted the British out of all of Hindustan, not just their own area.
  • (Fighting for your village is self-interest; fighting to free the whole country is fraternity, an embryonic national consciousness.)
  • v) Regional leaders did communicate with each other, however imperfectly.
  • vi) The rebels did not seek a return to 17th-century centralised Mughal rule.
  • They envisioned the 18th-century decentralised setup: real power at the provincial level, the Mughal emperor a symbolic weak centre (an early instinct towards federalism).
  • A movement of the masses, not just elites: vii) In many cases elites were pushed into leadership: Bahadur Shah Zafar 37-57, Nana Sahib and the Rani of Jhansi took up roles partly because circumstances compelled them. viii) Peasants and artisans in many regions continued fighting after their Taluqdar leaders had surrendered. ix) The sepoy was a peasant in uniform: when he removed that uniform, the initiative returned to where it had always come from: the common people.

7.2 Ideological impact: racism, authoritarianism and the unintended birth of modern nationalism

  • Post-1857, British attitudes hardened: Indians were now seen as racially inferior, incapable of self-governance and beyond reform.
  • The British became more autocratic and unwilling to give Indians any senior role in bureaucracy, police or military.
  • This backfired. The Western Educated Middle Class (WEMC), educated in British institutions and believing in British liberalism, had wanted a share in governance. They were shut out.
  • That frustration became the seed of the modern nationalism-based INM that began developing in the second half of the 19th century.
  • (The British, in trying to lock the door, inadvertently created the very class that would eventually break it down.)

7.3 Administrative impact: policing

  • 1857 made clear how vulnerable the British were without effective policing and intelligence.

8 · Changes in the army: the Peel Commission#

Story link

Because the revolt was at its core a military mutiny, the last and deepest reform was the army: a coup-proof design.

Teacher's board
  • Peel Commission background. (1) Lack of unity between armies: three regional armies (Bombay, Madras, Bengal).
  • (2) No caste-based unity in the new army.
  • (3) No very low ratio of British to Indians.
  • (4) Reward those who stayed loyal: Punjab, the princes, the zamindars.
  • (The "cont" from Madras is .)
  • The British understood that 1857 was, at its core, a military mutiny.
  • Preventing another one required a complete restructuring of the army, not minor adjustments.
  • The Crown appointed the Royal Commission on the Reorganisation of the Indian Army in July 1858.
  • It submitted its report in March 1859.
  • It is popularly known as the Peel Commission, after its head Major-General Jonathan Peel, who also served as Secretary of State for War (1858-59) in the British Cabinet.
  • (Jonathan Peel was the younger brother of Sir Robert Peel, the British Prime Minister who created the modern London police force.
  • The Peel family had a hand in restructuring both Indian policing and the Indian army after 1857.)
  • The Commission's goal: a "coup-proof" colonial army, one in which no future Bengal Army-style mutiny would ever be possible.
  • Its recommendations were implemented through a combination of legislation and executive military orders over the following years.

Recall sheet#

Read the question, answer in your head, then tap to check.

What formula defines 1857?Sepoy Mutiny + Civil Rebellion (teacher: common people led by traditional elites); a shared fear that religion was under threat.
Why did the revolt begin in the Bengal Army?Largest army; mostly upper-caste (Awadh and Bihar), so caste unity; lowest British-to-Indian ratio. Greased cartridge was only the trigger.
Opening shots of 1857?29 Mar Mangal Pandey fires (34th BNI, Barrackpur); 8 Apr hanged; 22 Apr Ishwari Prasad hanged and 34th BNI disbanded; 10 May Meerut; 11 May Delhi (Zafar 37-57 Emperor).
What was historic about 1857's early months?For the first time in a century the British monopoly over organised violence in India was broken; Delhi held 11 May to 20 Sep 1857.
Racism grievances of the sepoys?Lower pay and pension; "Suar"; highest rank Subedar at 60-70 rupees a month.
What happened in 1856 to sepoy pay?Salaries cut and the allowance for serving outside home region abolished: an economic blow and a loss-of-caste worry.
What was the cartridge rumour?Enfield rifle cartridges (replaced Brown Bess) greased with cow and pig fat and bitten before loading; also cow and pig bone dust in flour.
Why was Awadh's annexation a betrayal?Single largest source of Bengal Army recruits; nearly every family had a soldier; annexed 1856 (teacher: maladministration).
Awadh's two bites?1801: Doab, Gorakhpur, Rohilkhand (Mahalwari 1822; Ceded Districts, NWP 1836). 1856: rest (Summary Settlement, no field survey). Later names: 1877 NWP and Oudh, 1902 UP of Agra and Oudh, 1937 United Provinces.
Who is the "villain of 1857" on the teacher's board?The moneylender (Rs lender) beside the British, for the peasants.
Leaders and centres?Nana Sahib (Kanpur), Hazrat Mahal (Lucknow), Khan Bahadur Khan (Bareilly), Lakshmi Bai (Jhansi), Ahmadullah Shah (Faizabad), Kunwar Singh (Jagdishpur, Bihar), Zafar 37-57 (Delhi), Tantia Tope (central India).
Who was Nana Sahib?Adopted son of Baji Rao II 96-18, denied pension; Kanpur; Tantia Tope was his deputy.
Who was Kunwar Singh?Zamindar of Jagdishpur, Bihar; lost lands under the sunset clause of PS 1793; brother Amar Singh; guerrilla warfare. Prelims 2005: Bihar.
Tantia Tope's end?Nana Sahib's ablest commander, greatest guerrilla fighter; betrayed by Man Singh; hanged April 1859 (Prelims 2006).
What happened to Bahadur Shah Zafar 37-57?Canning 56-62 demoted the Mughals to "Princes" (1856); after Delhi fell: captured, tried, exiled to Rangoon; died 1862.
Where did Rani Lakshmi Bai die?In battle at Gwalior on 17-18 June 1858; Jhansi was annexed on the Doctrine of Lapse (teacher: 1854).
Why did taluqdars join?Lost estates (Mahalwari), forts and private armies, and social status (equality before law): economic, military, social.
Religion-threat list?Missionaries since 1813; Sati Abolition 1829; Lex Loci 1850; Widows' Remarriage 1856; tax on revenue-free lands; Pundits and Maulvis spread the fear.
What is the Lex Loci Act?Removal of Caste and Religious Disabilities Act 1850: "law of the place"; inheritance rights for those who converted.
Why did the British win?No money, weapons or command for rebels; British leadership, bureaucracy, army, telegraph; Delhi mistake; localised revolt; Bombay and Madras quiet, Punjabis and Gurkhas helped; north-central only.
The teacher's Delhi logic?Sepoy = military of revolt; all to Delhi; defeat of Delhi = defeat of sepoy mutiny = defeat of the revolt.
Three positions on 1857?Colonial: sepoy mutiny; Savarkar 1909 and Marx: war of independence (Swadharma and Swaraj); mainstream: more than a mutiny, but no modern nationalism.
Modern-nationalism test?Nation = sum of people who are one, equal, supreme (Fraternity, Equality, Liberty); democracy not monarchy. 1857 falls short.
Why Chittor not affected but Kota rebelled?Loyal Rajput princes (Chittor); Kota: Lala Jaidayal and Mehrab Khan took the city, held the King hostage six months, killed Major Charles Burton; retaken 1858.
Which groups stayed out and why?Ryotwari areas (Scam B: ryots became owners), Punjab (canals), Bengal (PS 1793), WEMC (jobs), loyal princes, taluqdars with land, the Bombay and Madras armies.
Why did WEMC oppose?Gained jobs and status; believed in British modernisation; teacher: opposed ideologically, not militarily (not neutral). Prelims 1998: opposed.
Evidence for "more than a mutiny"?Common enemy; sepoys spoke for all; Hindu-Muslim unity (Aitchison); out of all Hindustan; leaders communicated; federal vision; mass movement.
What did Aitchison's remark mean?Hindu-Muslim unity of purpose in 1857, so divide and rule became central after it (Prelims 2000: Revolt of 1857).
What did the GOI Act 1858 do?Ended EIC rule; Queen Victoria sovereign (paramountcy over princes); Secretary of State (Cabinet) replaces President of the Board of Control; Proclamation 1 Nov 1858.
What did the Proclamation promise?Religious tolerance and governance by India's traditions; reforms and Christianity promotion shelved; teacher: no more annexations; Br India 63% of the territory.
How did racism lead to the INM?Indians seen as inferior; WEMC shut out of senior roles; frustration became the seed of modern nationalism.
Police Act 1861 chain?1st Police Commission 1860 (Canning 56-62, M.H. Court); Act passed March 1861: Inspector General (to Governor/LG), Superintendent of Police (district), Sub-Inspector (thana); Police Raj.
Civil services after 1857?Exam (Charter Act 1853) not simultaneous in India; age cap 19-23; Statutory Civil Service 1870, implemented 1878 by Lytton 76-80 (slide says 1879): co-option not inclusion.
Peel Commission facts?Appointed July 1858; report March 1859; Major-General Jonathan Peel (also SoS for War 1858-59); goal: coup-proof army; legislation plus executive orders.
Peel structural measures?European Forces (India) Act 1860 (White Mutiny); ratios 1:2 Bengal, 1:3 Madras and Bombay; artillery barred; divide-and-rule regiments.
Peel control measures?Martial races (Jats, Rajputs, Sikhs, Gurkhas); namak ka haq; standing army; no senior Indians; civilian finance (checks and balance); deployed against INM.
Mains PYQ: "1857 was the culmination of recurrent local rebellions of the preceding hundred years. Elucidate."Intro: sepoy mutiny + civil rebellion; grievances not new. Body: agrarian wounds (LR, lender, auctions, courts) fed both; sepoy = peasant in uniform; loss of elite status; fear for religion; link to Chapter 20's pre-1857 revolts (pre-history of nationalism). Close: 1857 was the first convergence, led by traditional elites.
Mains PYQ: "1857 an important watershed in British policy towards colonial India. Explain."Intro: EIC ended, Crown in. Body: polity (GOI Act 1858, Proclamation, no annexations); ideology (racism, WEMC, INM seed); policing (1861); civil services (London exam, 19-23, Statutory CS); army (Peel Commission). Close: more efficient and repressive; the shut door seeded nationalism.
The teacher's prompt "Please tell impact of 1857 revolt" (five branches)?Ideology (racism, authoritarianism up); Polity (GOI Act 1858, Proclamation, Mughal empire ended legally); Civil services (London exam, age limit, Indianisation resisted, Police Act 1861); Army (Peel Commission); Society (reforms shelved, conversions down).
