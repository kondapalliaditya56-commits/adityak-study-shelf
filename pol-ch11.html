In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • Articles 153 to 167 (Part VI) deal with the State Executive: Governor, Chief Minister, Council of Ministers and Advocate-General.
  • The Governor is the nominal (constitutional) head of the State. The real power sits with the Council of Ministers led by the Chief Minister.
  • The office has a dual role: head of the State and agent of the Centre.
  • The Governor is appointed by the President by warrant under hand and seal (Art 155), not elected. The Constituent Assembly chose the Canadian model and dropped the American model of election.
  • He serves five years at the pleasure of the President. Only two written qualifications: citizen of India, 35 years of age.
  • His powers are executive, legislative, financial and judicial, plus some discretion that the President does not have.

Key terms

Warrant
The written order under hand and seal by which the President appoints the Governor (Art 155).
Pleasure of the President
The Governor holds office at the President's pleasure (Art 156); normal term is five years and he continues until his successor takes charge.
Raj Bhavan
The Governor's official residence; he also draws emoluments fixed by Parliament and they cannot be reduced during his term.
Suspensive veto
If the Legislature passes a returned ordinary bill again, the Governor must give assent. He can only delay, not kill it.
Referential veto
The Governor reserves a bill for the President's consideration (Art 200).
Ordinance (Art 213)
Law-making by the Governor when the Legislature is not in session; it lapses six weeks after the Legislature reassembles.
Situational discretion
Hidden discretion from the political situation, e.g. choosing a CM in a hung Assembly.
Art 361 immunity
No criminal case against the Governor during his term; civil case on personal acts only after two months' notice.

1 · Overview: the State Executive and the dual role#

  • Articles 153 to 167 (Part VI) deal with the State Executive: Governor, Chief Minister, Council of Ministers and Advocate-General. The Constitution sets up a parliamentary form in States, mirroring the Centre. The Governor is the constitutional head of a State, like the President at the Union level. Executive power is vested in him (Article 154), but he works largely as a nominal head
  • The real authority is the elected Council of Ministers under the Chief Minister
  • Dual role: The Governor is (1) head of the State and (2) link and agent of the Union Government.
  • Memory aid (Five heads of power): Executive, Legislative, Financial, Judicial, Discretionary. Sentence: "Every Lazy Fox Jumps Daily." (The handout adds a sixth heading, special responsibilities in certain States, under Discretion.)

How authority is split

  • Formal: Executive power vested in the Governor (Art. 154), exercised directly or through subordinate officers
  • Real: Council of Ministers headed by the CM aids and advises (Art. 163), responsible to the Assembly (Art. 164)
  • Agent role: Acts as the Union's link and agent

Also in the handout

  • The reasons for rejecting direct election were: - Nominal Head of State: Much like the President at the Union level, the Governor is only a constitutional (de-jure) head with no effective powers.

2 · Appointment of the Governor#

  • Article 155:
    • The Governor is appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal. He is not elected
    • The post is nominated by the Union executive
  • As per convention, the President consults the Chief Minister of the State before appointing, to keep Centre-State relations harmonious.
  • By convention the appointee should not belong to the same State, so he is impartial and free of local pressure.
  • One person may be Governor of two or more States (7th Amendment Act, 1956).
  • Constituent Assembly: It first considered direct election (the American model) but adopted the Canadian model of appointment by the Centre.

Constitution versus convention

  • Written rule:
    • Appointed by the President by warrant (Art. 155)
    • Same person can serve 2 or more States (7th Am. 1956)
  • Convention:
    • Outsider to the State
    • CM consulted. Both conventions have been violated in some cases

3 · Nominated versus elected Governor#

  • Outsider: unfamiliar with the local culture, language and ethos.
  • Friction with CMs: generally aligned to the ruling party at the Centre, so clashes with opposition-run States (the handout names West Bengal and Goa).
  • Erosion of federal principles: the Centre nominates the head of the State government.
  • Raj Bhavan as a "rehabilitation centre": retired politicians or election losers accommodated.
  • Mass removals when the Centre changes, harming security of tenure and neutrality.
  • Interference in State politics, including attempts to destabilise elected governments.
  • Memory aid (Six against nomination): Outsider, Friction, Erosion of federalism, Rehabilitation centre, Mass removals, Interference: "Old Foxes Enjoy Raj Bhavan Mostly Idle".

Arguments against an elected Governor

  • Nominal head: like the President, a de-jure head acting on ministerial advice, so an election would serve little purpose.
  • Potential friction with the CM: dual centres of authority, both with popular mandates.
  • Manifesto without authority: campaign promises he cannot implement.
  • Loss of central control:
    • The nominee serves as a channel of coordination and limited control
    • Seen as essential for unity and integrity amid separatist tendencies at framing
  • Second-rank candidate: sidelined persons may win, and the CM may claim a superior mandate.
  • Memory aid (Five against election): Nominal head, Potential friction, Manifesto without authority, Loss of central control, Second-rank candidate: "No Poll Means Less Strife".

Also in the handout

  • Their unfamiliarity with the local culture, language, and ethos limits their ability to contribute meaningfully to the state’s political and administrative affairs.
  • Raj Bhavan as a ‘Rehabilitation Centre’: The office of the Governor has often been misused to accommodate retired politicians or those who have lost elections, thereby diluting the prestige of the institution.
  • This has included attempts to destabilize duly elected state governments.
  • Hence, electing a Governor would not serve much purpose.
  • Both the Governor and the Chief Minister, having popular mandates, may clash and this could result in serious constitutional conflicts.
  • Possibility of a ‘Second-Rank’ Candidate: Elections could throw up situations where less capable or politically sidelined individuals win the governorship.

4 · Commissions on the Governor#

What each body said

BodyPoints
ARC-I (1966)Not burnt-out politicians with long partisan years; avoid lifelong loyalty to one party; no election losers; exclude persons of doubtful integrity (criminal records, corruption allegations); appointment needs approval of both Houses of Parliament; Leaders of Opposition in both Houses to be consulted

  • Retired service chiefs (Defence, Naval, Army)
  • Social activists (rare)

5 · Judicial interpretation on appointment#

  • Art. 155 gives the power to the President, but the Supreme Court set its limits and conventions.

Three cases

CaseHeld
B.P. Singhal v. Union of India (2010)President appoints and removes Governors but both must follow constitutional propriety and non-arbitrariness. "Pleasure of the President" (Art. 156) is not absolute. Dismissal cannot rest on political disagreement or a change of party at the Centre. The office is not an employment under the Union; it is a constitutional post needing independence and dignity
Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006)The Centre must ensure impartiality and constitutional conventions. Misusing the office to influence government formation or to impose President's Rule (Art. 356) violates the basic spirit of the Constitution
S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)Governors are crucial for the Centre-State balance. Cautioned against appointing politically affiliated persons or those lacking objectivity; partisan behaviour distorts federal governance
  • Memory aid (Case order): Bommai 1994, Rameshwar 2006, Singhal 2010 (oldest to newest). Sentence: "Big Red Stamp".

6 · Term, qualifications, conditions of office, oath and immunities#

Term and tenure (Article 156)

  • Holds office during the pleasure of the President.
  • Normal term five years, continuing until his successor assumes charge.
  • May resign any time by addressing the President.
  • May be transferred or reappointed to another State.
  • Memory aid (Tenure four): Pleasure, Five years, Successor takes charge, Resign to President: "Put Forward Some Resignation".

Qualifications (Article 156)

  • Only two: citizen of India and completed 35 years. Two conventions (outsider; CM consulted) have also developed, but both have been violated in some cases.

Conditions of office (Article 158)

  • Cannot be a member of Parliament or a State Legislature
  • If so, vacates the seat on taking office
  • Cannot hold any office of profit.
  • Entitled to an official residence (Raj Bhavan) and emoluments, allowances and privileges set by Parliament by law (Governor's Emoluments Act, 1982).
  • For two or more States, emoluments are shared as the President determines.
  • Emoluments and allowances cannot be reduced during his term.

Oath (Article 159)

  • Administered by the Chief Justice of the High Court (or in his absence the senior-most judge). He swears to faithfully execute the office
  • To preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law
  • And to devote himself to the service and well-being of the people of the State

Privileges and immunities (Article 361)

  • Personal immunity from legal liability for official acts.
  • During his term no criminal proceedings can be instituted against him, even for personal acts, and he cannot be arrested or imprisoned.
  • Civil proceedings for personal acts only after two months' notice.

7 · Constitutional position of the Governor#

  • Like the President, the Governor is the nominal executive head. His position rests on three Articles:

Articles 154, 163, 164

ArticleRule
154Executive power of the State is vested in the Governor, exercised directly or through subordinate officers; makes him the formal head
163A Council of Ministers headed by the CM aids and advises the Governor, except where he must act in his discretion; the principle of ministerial responsibility
164The Council is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly; real power sits with the elected government

Governor versus President: the position

Where the two differ

  • Governor:
    • Constitution explicitly allows discretion in certain matters
    • No amendment made for him, so he is not bound to accept ministerial advice in every situation, especially where discretion is permitted
  • President:
    • No discretion mentioned in the Constitution
    • After the 42nd Amendment (1976) he is bound by the Council's advice
  • Memory aid (Two differences): Discretion (Governor has it) and Binding advice (President is bound since 42nd Am.): "Different But similar".

Assent to Bills (Article 200)

Four options on a Bill passed by the State Legislature

  • 1. Assent: Bill becomes an Act
  • 2. Withhold: Bill fails
  • 3. Return (not a Money Bill): If passed again, with or without amendment, he must assent: only a suspensive veto

9 · Ordinance-making power (Article 213)#

Conditions

  • Only when both Houses (bicameral) or the Assembly (unicameral) are not in session.
  • Governor must be personally satisfied that circumstances need immediate action.
  • Only within the State's legislative competence
  • Beyond it, the Ordinance is void ab initio
Handout alert

Handout alert: The bullets say the Governor can issue an Ordinance only when both Houses are not in session. The comparison table says he can issue one when either House is not in session, "since a law requires approval of both Houses". The table's reasoning mirrors the President's rule; use the table version (either House not in session), and note the handout is inconsistent.

Prior Presidential instructions (three cases, proviso to Art. 213(1))

  • A Bill with the same provisions would have needed the President's previous sanction for introduction.
  • He would have thought it necessary to reserve such a Bill for the President.
  • An Act with similar provisions would be invalid unless reserved and assented by the President.
  • Memory aid (Three cases): Sanction, Reserve, Invalid without assent: "State Rules Instruct".

Duration and control

Life of an Ordinance

  • Promulgated: Governor, on the Council's advice, when the Legislature is not in session
  • Reassembly: Must be laid before the Legislature when it reassembles
  • Six weeks:
    • Ceases after six weeks from reassembly, unless earlier disapproved or replaced by State law
    • If Houses meet on different dates, count from the later date (Explanation to Art. 213(2))
  • Withdrawal: Governor may withdraw it any time
Life of an Ordinance

Scope, validity, repugnancy

  • Same force as an Act of the State Legislature, but cannot override constitutional limits.
  • Judicial review: can be struck down for mala fide intent or lack of immediate necessity (D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar, 1987).
  • Art. 213(3): a provision invalid in an ordinary State law is void in an Ordinance.
  • Art. 254 link: an Ordinance on a Concurrent List matter conflicting with a Parliamentary law is ordinarily void to the extent of repugnancy. But if promulgated with prior Presidential instructions, it is treated as a State law reserved for and assented to by the President and prevails in that State (proviso to Art. 213(3) with Art. 254(2)). Parliament can still override it later.

Two cases to remember

CaseHeld
D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987)Re-promulgation without placing before the Legislature violates constitutional morality and legislative supremacy
Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017)Governor's satisfaction under Art. 213 must be real, not mechanical; rights created by a lapsed Ordinance do not survive automatically
  • Shortcut: Mains hook. Re-promulgation without laying it before the House = Wadhwa (1987). "Real satisfaction" = Krishna Kumar Singh (2017).

Traps#

Trap 1 · Pardon of a death sentence The Governor can pardon for offences under State law, but not a death sentence. He can only suspend, remit or commute it. He also has no pardon power in court-martial cases. Rule: full pardon of death sentence and court-martial = President only.
Trap 2 · Conventions are not qualifications Only two qualifications are written: citizen and 35 years. Being an outsider to the State and consulting the CM are only conventions, and both have been violated. Rule: Constitution = 2 qualifications; outsider and CM consultation = conventions.
Trap 3 · Money Bill and the Governor On a Money Bill the Governor has three options: assent, withhold, reserve. He cannot return it. On an ordinary bill he has four, and a returned bill passed again must get his assent. Rule: ordinary bill = 4 options; Money Bill = 3 (no return).
Trap 4 · Who removes whom The Governor appoints the SPSC Chairman and members, but only the President can remove them. The State Election Commissioner is removed like a High Court judge. Rule: appointing authority is not always the removing authority.

Book edition: simple words and memory words#

In simple words

  • Articles 153 to 167 (Part VI) deal with the State Executive: Governor, Chief Minister, Council of Ministers and Advocate-General.
  • The Governor is the nominal (constitutional) head of the State. The real power sits with the Council of Ministers led by the Chief Minister.
  • The office has a dual role: head of the State and agent of the Centre.
  • The Governor is appointed by the President by warrant under hand and seal (Art 155), not elected. The Constituent Assembly chose the Canadian model and dropped the American model of election.
  • He serves five years at the pleasure of the President. Only two written qualifications: citizen of India, 35 years of age.
  • His powers are executive, legislative, financial and judicial, plus some discretion that the President does not have.

Key terms

Warrant
The written order under hand and seal by which the President appoints the Governor (Art 155).
Pleasure of the President
The Governor holds office at the President's pleasure (Art 156); normal term is five years and he continues until his successor takes charge.
Raj Bhavan
The Governor's official residence; he also draws emoluments fixed by Parliament and they cannot be reduced during his term.
Suspensive veto
If the Legislature passes a returned ordinary bill again, the Governor must give assent. He can only delay, not kill it.
Referential veto
The Governor reserves a bill for the President's consideration (Art 200).
Ordinance (Art 213)
Law-making by the Governor when the Legislature is not in session; it lapses six weeks after the Legislature reassembles.
Situational discretion
Hidden discretion from the political situation, e.g. choosing a CM in a hung Assembly.
Art 361 immunity
No criminal case against the Governor during his term; civil case on personal acts only after two months' notice.
