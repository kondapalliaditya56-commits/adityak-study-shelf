1 · Framework of Centre-State relations#

India has a parliamentary government inside a federal framework: the executive is responsible to the legislature (parliamentary principle), and the Union and States have separate spheres (federal principle). Because laws, administration and money touch people directly, the Constitution sets detailed rules.

Three dimensions of relations

Legislative
Who makes laws on what (Arts. 245-255)
Administrative
Who executes and how the Centre supervises (Arts. 256-263 and others)
Financial
Who taxes, shares and spends (Arts. 268-293)

The legislative scheme is the three-fold division (Union List, State List, Concurrent List) in the Seventh Schedule; Arts. 245-255, especially Art. 246, explain the powers and the rules where both legislate on one subject. Residuary subjects go to the Union.

Three relations
LAF

Legislative, Administrative, Financial: "Law, Administration, Funds".

245-255 legislative. 256-263 administrative. 268-293 financial.

2 · Legislative relations: territory and the Lists#

Part XI (Arts. 245-255) is the core, with other Articles making exceptions. The handout gives four headings: (a) territorial reach; (b) distribution of subjects; (c) Parliament in the State field; (d) central controls over State laws.

Territorial reach (Arts. 245-246)

  • Parliament may legislate for the whole or any part of India.
  • State legislatures may legislate for the whole or any part of their own State.
  • Parliament may make laws with extra-territorial effect, applying to Indian citizens and their property beyond India.

Exceptions to Parliament's reach

  • Art. 240: the President may make regulations for certain Union Territories, with the force of parliamentary enactments.
  • Fifth and Sixth Schedules (Art. 244): central laws may be adjusted or regulated in Scheduled Areas and tribal districts; the Governor or the President can make modifications or exceptions to protect local interests.
  • Art. 246(4): Parliament can make laws on any subject (even State List) for territories not part of any State (Union Territories, acquired territories). Example in the handout: Ladakh laws made by Parliament or under Art. 240 regulations.

The three Lists

Union, State and Concurrent Lists (Art. 246 and 246A with the Seventh Schedule)
ListWho legislatesCount in the handoutExamples
Union (List I)Parliament exclusively98 items (originally 97)Defence, foreign affairs, atomic energy, banking, currency, communication, inter-state trade and commerce
State (List II)State legislatures, normally59 items (earlier 66)Police, public health, agriculture, markets, local government, public order
Concurrent (List III)Both Parliament and States52 subjects (up from 47 originally)Criminal law, marriage and divorce, bankruptcy, education, labour welfare, electricity, forests, population control

Handout examples: nuclear energy under the Atomic Energy Act, 1962; defence forces under Art. 53; police and public order are State List Entries 1 and 2 (except when a national emergency lets Parliament legislate temporarily); education coordinated by NEP 2020 and implemented by States.

Handout alert

The Schedules chapter gives the counts as Union 97, State 61, Concurrent 52. This chapter gives 98, 59, 52 (with 97, 66, 47 as earlier figures). Quote 98, 59, 52 from this handout and check your class notes.

Handout alert

The 42nd Amendment (1976) is said here to have moved five subjects from the State to the Concurrent List: education, forests, weights and measures, protection of wild animals and birds, administration of justice (excluding the Supreme Court and High Courts). The Schedules chapter lists four (education, forests, wildlife, weights and measures). For this chapter use the five.

Hierarchy and conflict

  • The Union List prevails over both the State and Concurrent Lists; the Concurrent List prevails over the State List.
  • Art. 254(1): on a Concurrent subject, the Central law overrides the State law.
  • Art. 254(2): a State law that receives Presidential assent may prevail within that State; Parliament can still override it later.
  • Example in the handout: the Kerala Professional Colleges Act (2006), though approved by the President, was later superseded by Central NEET regulations under a Union law.

Special points

  • GST: the 101st Amendment (2016) inserted Art. 246A giving Parliament and States concurrent power on GST; Parliament is exclusive on GST in inter-state trade (Art. 269A); the GST Council (Art. 279A) is the joint forum.
  • Residuary powers: Parliament, Art. 248, includes law-making and taxation on unlisted subjects (digital currencies, space technology, cybersecurity named as examples).

Origin and comparison

  • The idea of lists comes from the Government of India Act, 1935: Federal, Provincial, Concurrent lists.
  • In 1935 residuary powers lay with the Governor-General; the Constitution gives them to Parliament, following the Canadian model.
  • The US has a single enumeration of federal powers with residuary power to the States; Australia is similar. India has double enumeration plus a concurrent field.
Lists
USC

Union = national uniformity (defence, currency), State = local diversity (police, agriculture), Concurrent = cooperation: "Union Serves Country".

List counts

Union 98, State 59, Concurrent 52; 42nd Amendment: five subjects to Concurrent; residuary with Parliament (Art. 248), Canadian model; lists from the 1935 Act (PYQ 2012).

3 · Parliament in the State field: five routes#

Normally Parliament cannot touch the State List. Five exceptions exist, each with its own trigger, scope and checks.

The five routes
#RouteTriggerEffect and duration
1Art. 249Rajya Sabha resolution by a special majority that Parliament should legislate on a State List subject in the national interestParliament can legislate for the whole or part of India. Resolution lasts one year, renewable yearly. Laws cease six months after the resolution lapses. States may still legislate, but Parliamentary law prevails on direct inconsistency
2Art. 250 with Art. 352National EmergencyParliament can legislate on any State List entry for the Emergency's duration. Laws end six months after the Emergency ceases, unless Parliament provides otherwise. States not barred; Central law overrides on repugnancy
3Art. 252Two or more State legislatures pass resolutions requesting itParliament legislates for the requesting States. Other States may adopt by resolution. Only Parliament can amend or repeal it, not the adopting States. Examples: Prize Competitions Act, 1955; Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972
4Art. 253To implement treaties, conventions or international agreementsParliament can legislate on State List matters. Examples: privileges of international organisations, Geneva Conventions laws, anti-hijacking law, environmental statutes
5Art. 356President's Rule in a StateParliament can legislate on any State List subject for that State. Laws continue after President's Rule ends; the State Legislature may later repeal, amend or replace them

Five routes
RNSTP

Rajya Sabha (249), National Emergency (250), States' request (252), Treaties (253), President's Rule (356): "Red New Sofa Tops Pillows".

Duration rule

249 and 250: laws end six months after the trigger ends. 356: laws continue until the State repeals them. 252: only Parliament can amend.

Constitutional logic

Each route is a compromise: the Union acts when uniform action is needed, but with checks (special majorities, State requests, a presidential proclamation). In practice these routes have enabled central laws on pollution control, wildlife protection, transplantation law, urban land regulation and implementation of international conventions.

Handout alert

The Art. 249 route is said to need a special majority in the text and two-thirds of Rajya Sabha members in the Articles table. Read them as the same rule: a two-thirds-type special majority of the Rajya Sabha.

4 · Central controls over State legislation#

Parliamentary oversight keeps laws consistent. The handout roots it in Arts. 200, 201, 304(b) and 360.

Four supervisory tools

Sarkaria Commission (1983-88): "the rule of federal supremacy is not a denial of federalism but a device to preserve unity amidst diversity."

Rationale

These checks aim to preserve cooperative federalism, with the Union guiding and harmonising State laws on nationally significant matters. 5 · Administrative relations: executive power and directions#

The administrative framework lies in Arts. 256-263 (Part XI). It gives the Centre power to guide, coordinate and supervise State administration.

Distribution of executive authority

  • Union: extends to the whole of India on matters where Parliament has exclusive legislative authority (Union List), and to implementing treaties and agreements (Art. 73).
  • State: confined to its own territory, extends to the State List.
  • Concurrent List: Art. 73(1)(a) says the State's executive power generally extends to these subjects unless the Constitution or a parliamentary law gives responsibility to the Union.
  • Example: Right to Education Act (2009) and Environmental Protection Act (1986) are carried out jointly by Union and States.

7 · All-India Services and Public Service Commissions#

All-India Services (Art. 312)

  • Three core services: IAS, IPS, IFS. Officers serve at both Union and State levels and rotate between them.
  • Recruitment, training and ultimate control with the Central Government; day-to-day supervision with the States.
  • Origins: Indian Civil Service (ICS) and Indian Police (IP) replaced by IAS and IPS after 1947; IFS established 1966.
  • Art. 312: Parliament can create additional All-India Services if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution (given as a two-thirds majority in one passage).
  • Members share common rights, uniform pay scales and equal status: a single integrated service.

Ambedkar in the Constituent Assembly: dual polity is followed in all federations by a dual service; some posts are strategic; States keep their own civil services but there shall be an All-India Service recruited on an all-India basis, with common qualifications, uniform pay, whose members alone could fill strategic posts. The services are seen as limiting State autonomy slightly in personnel management but justified for unity and standards.

8 · Integrated judiciary, emergencies and Union safeguards#

Integrated judicial system

  • A single hierarchy enforces both Union and State laws: Supreme Court (Arts. 124-147) with appellate, advisory and original jurisdiction; High Courts (Arts. 214-231).
  • Art. 231: Parliament may set up a common High Court for two or more States or Union Territories (Maharashtra and Goa; Punjab and Haryana).
  • High Court judges are appointed by the President (Art. 217) after consulting the Chief Justice of India and the Governor; the President also transfers or removes judges.
  • Aims: uniform interpretation, unitary judicial frame in a federal system, flexibility through common High Courts and transfers.

10 · Financial relations: tax powers and GST#

Part XII (Arts. 268-293) governs tax assignments, revenue sharing, grants-in-aid, fiscal institutions and public funds. Aim: strong Centre for national needs, adequate predictable resources for States. Five mechanisms: (a) division of taxes, (b) Finance Commission, (c) grants and transfers, (d) funds and borrowing rules, (e) audit and safeguards.

  • Tax division works through Art. 246 with the Seventh Schedule: Union List taxes belong to Parliament; State List taxes to State legislatures.
  • No tax entries in the Concurrent List: taxation is normally exclusive to Union or States, except GST.
  • Residuary taxing power: Parliament (Art. 248), historically used for wealth tax and other central levies.

Three powers

  • Levy (impose), collect, appropriate (keep proceeds)

Examples

  • Duties levied by the Union but collected and appropriated by States (Art. 268)
  • income tax (except agricultural) levied and collected by the Union, proceeds shared (Art. 270)

13 · Finance Commission, safeguards and tax immunities#

Finance Commission (Art. 280)

  • The President constitutes it every five years or earlier.
  • Composition: a Chairperson with public affairs experience; at least one member with finance or economics expertise; other members nominated by the President.
  • Independent; recommends on objective criteria.
Three functions
FunctionContent
Distribution of tax revenuesState share in net proceeds of Central taxes (income tax, GST compensation, customs duties, other levies)
Grants-in-aid (Art. 275)Statutory grants for welfare programmes, backward regions, Scheduled Areas
Fiscal consolidationAdvice on fiscal discipline, reducing deficits, prudent borrowing, revenue generation, expenditure rationalisation
  • 15th Finance Commission (2020-2025): recommended 41% devolution of Central taxes, considering population, income distance, area and fiscal performance.
  • Art. 281: the President lays the Commission's recommendations with an explanatory memorandum before Parliament; Art. 282: discretionary grants.

Safeguards for States' finances

  • Bills need the President's recommendation (Art. 274) before introduction in Parliament if they: impose or vary taxes in which States are interested; alter the definition of agricultural income; affect the principles of distribution of central revenues; impose a surcharge for the Centre.
  • "Tax or duty in which States are interested" = taxes whose net proceeds are wholly or partly assigned to a State, or on whose net proceeds sums are paid to a State out of the Consolidated Fund of India.
  • Net proceeds = total collected minus cost of collection; the CAG certifies it, and the certificate is final and binding.

Mutual tax immunity

Intergovernmental immunity
PointContent
Union property (Art. 285)Exempt from State and local taxes (municipalities, panchayats, district boards); "property" includes land, buildings, assets, shares, debts; exempt whether sovereign or commercial use; Parliament can remove or modify the exemption; Union corporations and companies are separate legal entities and not automatically immune
State property and income (Art. 289)Generally exempt from Union taxation, including commercial operations; Parliament may authorise taxing commercial activities of States; local authorities and State corporations are not immune
1963 advisory opinionState immunity does not extend to customs and excise duties; the Union may levy customs on imports or exports by a State and excise on goods produced by State entities

Financial effects of emergencies

  • Art. 352: the President may modify or withhold transfer of Central taxes and grants-in-aid; modifications last until the end of the financial year in which the Emergency ceases.
  • Art. 360: directions on financial propriety; salary and allowance reductions for State employees (including constitutional authorities and local body staff); State money bills reserved for the President.

14 · Evolving dynamics and sources of strain#

Evolution

  • First two decades: relations cooperative and stable because of Congress dominance at the Centre and in most States.
  • After the 1967 elections Congress lost power in nine States while keeping a fragile Centre majority; non-Congress States resisted centralisation and asserted autonomy.
  • Disputes: legislative competence, funds, Union's discretionary powers, President's Rule (Art. 356), control of services, State planning.
Factors shaping relations today
FactorContent
Political federalismRegional parties and coalitions since the late 1980s need negotiation; States with strong identities demand fiscal and administrative autonomy
Financial dependenceStates depend on the Centre for centrally sponsored schemes and projects despite Arts. 268-293, 275-282
Judicial interventionSupreme Court and High Courts mediate on resources, legislative competence, central directives
Institutional mechanismsInter-State Council (Art. 263), Zonal Councils, NITI Aayog
Multi-dimensional conflictsRegional disparity, linguistic and cultural identity, statehood demands

15 · ARC, Rajamannar, Anandpur Sahib and West Bengal#

ARC (1966)
PointContent
Formed1966, chaired by Morarji Desai, later K. Hanumanthayya; mandate included Centre-State relations
Study teamLed by M.C. Setalvad, first Attorney General of India; report 1969
RecommendationsInter-State Council (Art. 263); non-partisan, experienced Governors (spirit of Arts. 155-156); devolution of administrative powers; more financial resources to States; deploy Central armed forces mainly on State request (Art. 355)
StatusUnion took no significant action on most proposals
Rajamannar Committee
PointContent
Set up1969 by the DMK Tamil Nadu government; three members under Dr. P.V. Rajamannar; report 1971
Causes of centralisationConstitutional provisions (Arts. 356, 352, 360); one-party dominance; weak fiscal autonomy; centralised planning by the Planning Commission
RecommendationsInter-State Council now; permanent Finance Commission; replace Planning Commission with a statutory body; omit Arts. 356, 357, 365 and remove the Governor's power to dismiss a ministry at pleasure; shift subjects from Union and Concurrent Lists to State List; residuary powers to States; abolish IAS, IPS, IFS
StatusLargely ignored; none implemented

Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973, Akali Dal)

  • Punjab
  • Centre limited to defence, foreign affairs, communications, currency
  • residuary powers to States (Art. 248)
  • truly federal model with equal authority for States

West Bengal Memorandum (1977, Communist-led government)

  • Replace "Union" with "Federal"
  • Centre limited to defence, foreign affairs, currency, communications, economic coordination
  • residuary powers to States
  • repeal Arts. 356, 357, 360
  • State consent for new States or reorganisation
  • 75% of Centre's revenue to States
  • equal powers for Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha
  • abolish All-India Services
