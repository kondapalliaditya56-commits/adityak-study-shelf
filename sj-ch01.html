Meaning AND Dimensions OF Vulnerability

Key point

Vulnerability is the proneness of an individual or group to harm, exploitation or deprivation arising from external social, economic or structural forces. It is not a personal failing but a condition produced by society.

Four features

  • Group nature: vulnerability is collective and not individual.
  • Structural roots: embedded in caste hierarchies, patriarchy and economic inequality.
  • Multidimensional impact: social, economic, political, physical and psychological domains.
  • Self-reinforcing cycle: poverty leads to poor health and education, which in turn deepens poverty.

Classification of disadvantaged groups

  • The Government of India Act, 1935 classified disadvantaged groups into Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes.
  • Over the years the list has expanded to include women, children, the elderly, persons with disabilities, sexual minorities, denotified tribes and religious minorities.
  • The Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment is the nodal ministry for the empowerment of these groups.

Intersectionality OF Caste, Gender, Disability AND Location

Key point

A single person may belong to multiple vulnerable categories at once. A Dalit woman with disability living in a remote tribal area faces compounded disadvantages that go far beyond what any one category captures.

IdentityLayer of disadvantage
CasteSocial exclusion, denial of dignity, occupational segregation
GenderPatriarchy, unpaid care work, violence, low wages
DisabilityInaccessible infrastructure, stigma, employment barriers
LocationRural backwardness, poor service delivery, geographical isolation
ReligionIdentity-based discrimination, communal tensions
Way forward / key point

This intersectional reality is why welfare schemes that treat each category in isolation often miss the most marginalised within marginalised groups.

Concept OF Social Justice AND Social Empowerment

Key point

Social justice means fair distribution of resources, opportunities and dignity across all sections of society. It seeks to correct historical wrongs and create substantive equality, not merely formal equality before law.

Constitutional vision of social justice (three layers)

  • Preamble: secures justice (social, economic and political), equality of status and dignity for all citizens.
  • Fundamental Rights: Article 14 (equality before law), Article 15 (prohibition of discrimination), Article 17 (abolition of untouchability), Article 21 (right to life with dignity) and Article 23 (prohibition of forced labour).
  • Directive Principles: Articles 38, 39, 41, 46 and 47, which guide the State towards becoming a welfare State.
Note

Number card: FR articles 14, 15, 17, 21, 23 · DPSP articles 38, 39, 41, 46, 47.

Key point

Social empowerment is the practical expression of this vision. It means helping disadvantaged groups gain control over their lives through education, economic opportunity, political voice and barrier-free participation as equal citizens.

Inclusive Growth, Income Inequality AND Social Justice

Growth without inclusion

  • Economic growth without inclusion deepens inequality and weakens social justice. India has shown high growth but still has poor human development indicators, which makes the inclusive growth agenda central to social justice.
  • India's top 10% holds more than 57% of national income while the bottom 50% holds around 13% (World Inequality Report 2022).
  • Inclusive growth widens demand, builds human capital and reduces social tensions.
  • It requires investment in health, education, skill development and social safety nets.
Way forward / key point

The relationship between growth and justice is mutual: growth provides resources for welfare, while welfare ensures growth reaches everyone.

Social Infrastructure IN India

Key point

Social infrastructure is the network of public services and institutions that enable human development. It includes schools, hospitals, anganwadis, sanitation facilities, drinking water systems, social security networks and public housing.

Spending gaps

  • India spends only 1.8% of GDP on public health against the recommended 2.5% (Economic Survey 2025-26).
  • Combined Centre and State spending on education is around 4.6% of GDP, below the 6% target of NEP 2020.
  • Wide interstate variations remain, with Kerala and Tamil Nadu doing far better than Bihar, UP and Jharkhand.
Way forward / key point

Strong social infrastructure is the precondition for any welfare scheme to succeed. Without good schools, functioning health centres and accessible institutions even the best-designed schemes fail at the last mile.

2 · Design OF Welfare Schemes · Discrimination Debate · Centre-state · Last-mile GAPS · Social Exclusion#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2023 (welfare schemes discriminatory?), 2019 (awareness and involvement), 2014 (exclusion in urban business), 2013 (CSS restructuring) · Social Justice handout pp. 5-8. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Welfare schemes rest on the idea of a welfare State (Articles 38, 39, 46, 47). They are differential by design but not discriminatory in intent. Delivery is shared: the Centre designs and funds, States implement; CSS were consolidated into about 28 umbrella schemes. The biggest gap is the last mile: awareness deficit, leakages, ghost cards, digital exclusion. Protection must be balanced with promotion or it can exclude beneficiaries from urban business.

Conceptual AND Philosophical Basis OF Welfare Schemes

Key point

Welfare schemes flow from the idea of a welfare State where the government takes responsibility for the well-being of its citizens, especially those who cannot protect themselves through the market.

Constitutional basis

  • Article 38: State shall promote welfare of the people.
  • Article 39: equitable distribution of resources.
  • Article 46: special care of weaker sections.
  • Article 47: raising the standard of living and public health.

Multiple goals of welfare schemes

  • Reduction of poverty and inequality: MGNREGA provided employment to over 11 crore rural households.
  • Building human capital: PM-JAY offers health insurance to over 55 crore beneficiaries.
  • Provision of public goods: Samagra Shiksha expands school education.
  • Crisis resilience: PMGKAY and MGNREGA cushioned the poor during the COVID-19 pandemic.
  • Promoting equity: gives the disadvantaged a fair starting line.

Discriminatory Nature Debate OF Welfare Design PYQ 2023

Quote

PYQ 2023 (15 marks): Development and welfare schemes for the vulnerable, by its nature, are discriminatory in approach. Do you agree? Give reasons for your answer.

Arguments that welfare schemes are discriminatoryArguments that they are not discriminatory but corrective
They single out specific groups based on caste, gender or religion and may reinforce these identities rather than dissolve them.The Constitution itself permits positive discrimination under Articles 15(4), 15(5) and 16(4) to remedy historical injustices.
General category groups in genuine economic distress are often excluded.Equal treatment of unequals is itself unequal, as Aristotle and the Supreme Court have repeatedly held.
Scheme design can stigmatise beneficiaries, making them feel like permanent recipients of charity.Welfare schemes try to create a level playing field rather than tilt it against anyone.
Targeted schemes may create dependency rather than self-reliance.Indra Sawhney judgment (1992) upheld that affirmative action is a constitutional mandate, not discrimination.
Way forward / key point

The honest position: welfare schemes are differential by design but not discriminatory in intent. The challenge lies in ensuring they reach the most deprived within deprived groups, do not stigmatise beneficiaries and gradually build self-reliance.

Centre-state Dynamics IN Welfare Delivery

Key point

Welfare delivery in India is a shared responsibility: the Centre designs and funds while the States implement on the ground. This sharing is both a strength and a source of friction.

Centrally Sponsored Schemes AND State Flexibility PYQ 2013

Quote

PYQ 2013 (10 marks): The Central Government frequently complains on the poor performance of the State Governments in eradicating suffering of the vulnerable sections of the society. Restructuring of Centrally sponsored schemes across the sectors for ameliorating the cause of vulnerable sections of population aims at providing flexibility to the States in better implementation. Critically evaluate.

Key point

Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) are funded jointly by the Centre and States in agreed ratios. Major examples: MGNREGA, PM Awas Yojana, Samagra Shiksha, NHM, ICDS and POSHAN Abhiyaan.

FeatureEarlier patternRestructured pattern
Number of schemesOver 200 fragmented schemesConsolidated into around 28 umbrella schemes
Funding shareCentre had higher and rigid shareCentre-State share varies (60:40, 75:25, 90:10 for special category States)
State flexibilityLimited freedom in designGreater flexibility within broad guidelines
MonitoringCentralisedPerformance-based

Restructuring of welfare schemes

  • The B. K. Chaturvedi Committee (2011) and the NITI Aayog Sub-Group of Chief Ministers (2015) recommended rationalisation of CSS, to give States more flexibility, reduce overlaps and improve outcomes.

Strengths of restructuring

  • States can adapt schemes to local needs.
  • Reduced fragmentation makes delivery sharper.
  • States have skin in the game through cost sharing.

Limitations and tensions

  • States complain of inadequate central funding when their share rises.
  • Vertical devolution by the 15th Finance Commission at 41% is welcome but States still depend heavily on tied transfers.
  • Differential capacity across States means rich States gain more flexibility than poor ones.
  • Centre often blames States for poor outcomes while not increasing untied funds.
Way forward / key point

Cooperative federalism through forums like the NITI Aayog Governing Council and the Inter-State Council is essential to bridge these tensions.

Implementation AND Performance GAPS

Key point

The biggest gap in Indian welfare is not in design but in delivery. India spends large sums on welfare yet outcomes lag because schemes fail at the last mile.

ROLE OF Awareness AND Beneficiary Involvement PYQ 2019

Quote

PYQ 2019 (15 marks): Performance of welfare schemes that are implemented for vulnerable sections is not so effective due to absence of their awareness and active involvement at all stages of policy process. Discuss.

Welfare performance suffers when beneficiaries are kept passive

  • Awareness deficit: many genuine beneficiaries do not know which schemes apply to them.
  • Information asymmetry: lets middlemen exploit beneficiaries through fake fees and false promises.
  • Lack of voice in scheme design: programmes do not match real needs on the ground.
  • Weak grievance redressal: discourages people from reporting failures.
  • Low ownership: beneficiaries treat schemes as charity rather than rights.
Way forward / key point

Active involvement of communities through Gram Sabhas, Self Help Groups, ward sabhas and social audits transforms passive recipients into active stakeholders. MGNREGA social audits in Andhra Pradesh have shown how community involvement plugs leakages.

Last-mile Delivery Challenges

Structural problems that block the last mile

  • Inclusion and exclusion errors: India still has around 4.4 crore ghost ration cards even after Aadhaar seeding.
  • Leakages and corruption: a CAG report on Bihar PDS found 30 to 40% of foodgrains lost to leakage.
  • Bureaucratic apathy and frequent transfers of frontline staff.
  • Digital exclusion of the elderly, rural poor and those without smartphones.
  • Inter-state variations in administrative capacity.
  • Convergence failure between line departments.
Way forward / key point

Reforms such as Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT), JAM Trinity (Jan Dhan, Aadhaar, Mobile), One Nation One Ration Card and PM GatiShakti (for logistics) are improving delivery, but the human element of motivated frontline workers remains the weakest link.

Welfare Schemes AND Social Exclusion PYQ 2014

Quote

PYQ 2014 (12.5 marks): Do government's schemes for up lifting vulnerable and backward communities by protecting required social resources for them, lead to their exclusion in establishing businesses in urban economies.

Key point

Welfare schemes are meant to include the excluded, but sometimes their design or implementation produces fresh exclusion. The 2014 question asked whether protective measures for vulnerable groups may end up excluding them from urban business opportunities.

Exclusion in urban economic opportunities

  • Protective ring effect: reservation in jobs and education builds a comfort zone but does not push beneficiaries into competitive entrepreneurship.
  • Urban informal economy is largely outside the protective scope of caste-based schemes.
  • Capital and network barriers keep SC, ST and OBC entrepreneurs out of mainstream business.
  • Stand Up India, the Venture Capital Fund for SCs and PM Vishwakarma are steps to close this gap, yet only a small fraction of MSME credit reaches Dalit and Tribal entrepreneurs.
Way forward / key point

The lesson: protection must be balanced with promotion. Reservation alone does not create an entrepreneurial class. It must be combined with credit access, market linkages, mentorship and skill upgradation.

Handout only · Saarthi IAS

In simple words
  • Women have made strides yet remain vulnerable because of structural patriarchy, not nature. The numbers show it: MMR 97, sex ratio at birth 907, FLFPR 41.7%, 330 minutes of unpaid work a day. Schemes improve material conditions but attitudes need other tools (engaging men, media, curriculum, leaders, workplaces, laws, recognising unpaid work). The legal scaffolding is strong (Articles 14-16, 23, 39, 42, 51A(e), 243D, 243T; Dowry, PCPNDT, POSH, DV, Surrogacy Acts); implementation is the problem.

Status OF Women IN India

Key point

Women in India have made remarkable strides in education, politics and the workplace, yet they remain a vulnerable section due to deep-rooted patriarchy, unequal access to resources and persistent violence. The vulnerability is structural, not natural.

Key challenges faced by women (overlapping disadvantages across the life cycle, from birth to old age)

  • Health vulnerabilities: Maternal Mortality Ratio 97 per 1 lakh live births against SDG target of 70 (SRS 2018-20).
  • Son preference: Sex Ratio at Birth 907 girls per 1000 boys (CRS 2018-20).
  • Child marriage: 20.1% of women aged 20 to 24 were married before 18 (NFHS 6).
  • Economic exclusion: Female Labour Force Participation only 41.7% (PLFS 2023-24).
  • Wage gap: rural casual women earn Rs 286 per day against men's Rs 407 (PLFS 2024).
  • Violence: NCRB 2022 reported 4.45 lakh cases of crimes against women.
  • Unpaid care burden: women spend 330 minutes daily on unpaid domestic work against men's 101 minutes (Time Use Survey 2024).
  • Digital divide: only 1 in 3 women have ever used the internet against 57% of men (NFHS 5).
Note

Cross-check (from the Health chapter, NFHS 6): women aged 20-24 married before 18 fell to 20.1% (NFHS 6) from 23.3% (NFHS 5). The Children chapter quotes the NFHS 5 figure, so the two numbers are consecutive survey rounds, not a conflict. Quote the survey name with the figure.

Patriarchy AND Social Attitudes PYQ 2021

Quote

PYQ 2021 (15 marks): Though women in post Independent India have excelled in various fields, the social attitude towards women and feminist movement has been patriarchial. Apart from women education and women empowerment schemes, what interventions can help change this milieu.

Key point

Patriarchy operates through silent codes that no law can directly touch. It shapes who eats first at home, who inherits land, who steps outside and who carries the family honour. Schemes can improve material conditions but attitudes need different tools.

Interventions to change patriarchal attitudes

  • Engaging men and boys through programmes like HeForShe and the MARD initiative by Farhan Akhtar to shift masculinity norms.
  • Media and popular culture can break stereotypes through films, advertisements and digital content with positive women role models.
  • School curriculum reforms to integrate gender sensitivity, consent education and equal sharing of housework.
  • Religious and community leaders who can challenge patriarchal customs from within tradition.
  • Workplace cultural change through paternity leave, flexible hours and zero tolerance to harassment.
  • Legal reforms in personal laws and uniform inheritance rights to remove hidden discrimination.
  • Recognition of unpaid work in national income accounts to make women's contribution visible.

KEY LAWS Protecting Women

Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961

  • Prohibits giving, taking or demanding dowry directly or indirectly.
  • Punishment includes imprisonment up to 5 years and fine.
  • Section 498A of IPC (now BNS) deals with cruelty by husband or relatives for dowry.
  • Challenge: low conviction rates and social acceptance of dowry as gifts.

Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act, 1994 (PCPNDT)

  • Prohibits sex-selective abortion and pre-natal sex determination.
  • Bans advertisements relating to sex determination.
  • Mandates registration of all ultrasound clinics and genetic counselling centres.
  • Implementation suffers from low conviction rates and clandestine clinics.

Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 (POSH Act)

  • Based on the Vishaka Guidelines of the Supreme Court in 1997.
  • Mandates an Internal Complaints Committee in every workplace with 10 or more employees.
  • Local Complaints Committees for informal sector workers.
  • NCRB 2022 recorded only 419 cases of workplace sexual harassment, indicating heavy under-reporting.
  • Justice Hema Committee Report on the Malayalam film industry exposed deep structural harassment.

Surrogacy (Regulation) Act, 2021

  • Allows only altruistic surrogacy and prohibits commercial surrogacy.
  • Eligibility limited to married Indian couples with infertility.
  • Establishes National and State Surrogacy Boards.
  • Concerns: exclusion of single, LGBTQIA+ and live-in couples, and the burden placed on surrogate mothers.

Domestic Violence Act, 2005

  • Provides civil remedies including protection orders, residence orders and monetary relief.
  • Recognises physical, emotional, sexual and economic abuse.
  • Implementation suffers from shortage of Protection Officers.

Other key laws

  • Maternity Benefit (Amendment) Act, 2017: extending paid leave to 26 weeks.
  • Equal Remuneration Act, 1976.
  • Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006.
  • Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986.

Key point

The Indian State has launched a vast architecture of schemes to translate constitutional promises into reality.

Women-led Development AND Lakhpati DIDI

Women-led development marks a shift from women as beneficiaries to women as drivers of change. The PM addressed this concept at the G20 Delhi Declaration, 2023.

Lakhpati Didi Initiative

  • Target of 3 crore Lakhpati Didis, with 11 lakh felicitated by the PM at Jalgaon.
  • Builds on the SHG ecosystem of DAY-NRLM.

Contemporary Issues Concerning Women

Key point

Beyond traditional welfare, women face new-age challenges that demand fresh policy responses.

Violence against women and cybercrime

  • Violence is the most visible expression of patriarchy and the most persistent failure of Indian society.
  • NCRB 2022 recorded 4.45 lakh cases of crimes against women
  • Domestic violence accounts for over 30% of these
  • Cybercrime against women is rising fast: online stalking, deepfakes, image abuse and trolling.
  • Digital Shakti initiative by NCW spreads awareness on online safety.
  • IT Act 2000 and Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita sections 75 to 79 cover online harassment.

Marital rape debate

  • India is one of the few countries where marital rape is not a criminal offence. Exception 2 to Section 375 of IPC (now Section 63 of BNS) exempts husbands from rape charges.

Arguments for criminalisation

  • Bodily autonomy is a constitutional right under Article 21, affirmed in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India.
  • International obligations under CEDAW.
  • Delhi HC delivered a split verdict in 2022 and the matter is pending in the Supreme Court.

Arguments against

  • Misuse against husbands.
  • Risk to the institution of marriage.
  • Issue should be left to the legislature.

Sexual harassment at workplace

  • Despite the POSH Act 2013, workplace harassment remains widespread.
  • Only 419 cases formally reported in 2022 (NCRB) against an iceberg of unreported incidents.
  • Local Complaints Committees for informal sector workers exist only on paper in most districts.
  • Justice Hema Committee Report revealed systemic abuse in the Malayalam film industry.
  • Need for stronger ICCs, awareness drives and protection of complainants.

Women in STEM and green jobs

  • Women form only 27% of the STEM workforce in India (AISHE 2021-22).
  • Gender gap in green jobs is significant despite climate jobs growing fast.
  • WISE-KIRAN (Women in Science and Engineering) scheme by DST supports women scientists.
  • GATI programme promotes gender equity in research institutions.

Care economy and purple economy

  • The care economy is the paid and unpaid work of caring for children, the elderly, the sick and the disabled. It is also called the Purple Economy.
  • Women perform over 75% of total unpaid care work globally (ILO).
  • If valued at minimum wage, women's unpaid work would add 15 to 17% to India's GDP (Oxfam India).
  • Budget 2026-27 announced training of 1.5 lakh multi-skilled caregivers under NSQF.
  • Need to invest in childcare, paid family leave and elderly care infrastructure.

Low female labour force participation

  • India has one of the lowest FLFPR in the world despite rising female education.
  • Marriage and motherhood penalty: women are 55.2% more likely than men to take career breaks (Global Gender Gap Report 2025).
  • Lack of safe transport and workplaces.
  • Unpaid care burden at home.
  • Workplace discrimination and gender pay gap.
  • Withdrawal of educated women from the workforce, known as the U-shaped curve.

Way forward

  • Affordable childcare, safe transport, flexible work.
  • Skill training aligned with market demand.
  • Workplace inclusion and pay parity.
  • Recognition and redistribution of unpaid care work.

Rising manosphere and gender equality

  • The manosphere refers to online communities promoting aggressive masculinity and anti-feminist content. UN Women has warned it is a serious threat to gender equality.
  • Promotes ideologies such as AWALT (All Women Are Like That).
  • Spreads through algorithm-driven social media platforms.
  • Normalises violence and misogyny among young men.
  • Need for legal action, media literacy and positive male role models.
  • EU's Digital Services Act prohibits misogynistic content. India can learn from the UK Online Safety Act to regulate digital platforms.

SELF HELP Groups AND Economic Empowerment PYQ 2025

Quote

PYQ 2025 (10 marks): Women's social capital complements in advancing empowerment and gender equity. Explain.

Significance of women-led SHGs

  • SHGs have mobilised over 9 crore rural poor women under DAY-NRLM.
  • 65% of rural SHG members improved their incomes between FY19 and FY24 (SBI Report).
  • SHG members as Banking Correspondent Sakhis helped lower gross NPAs to 1.6%.
  • SHGs build pressure groups that highlight alcoholism, open defecation and women's health.
  • Project eShakti by NABARD digitises SHG records.

Challenges

  • Socio-cultural rigidities of caste, patriarchy and illiteracy.
  • Regional disparity: 68.56% of SHGs are concentrated in South India (NABARD).
  • Elite capture by better-off members.
  • Lack of digital skills and access to markets.

Social Capital AND Gender Equity PYQ 2025

Key point

Social capital refers to networks, trust and norms that enable collective action. Women's social capital includes SHGs, mahila mandals, neighbourhood groups and online sisterhoods.

How social capital advances empowerment

  • Builds collective bargaining power as SHGs negotiate better prices and rights.
  • Creates information networks that spread awareness of schemes and rights.
  • Provides emotional and economic support in crises.
  • Enables political voice, as seen in women panchayat leaders.
  • Breaks isolation that traps women within four walls.
Way forward / key point

The Kudumbashree movement in Kerala is a global model of how women's social capital transforms lives. Such social capital complements formal schemes by ensuring last-mile reach and dignity.

Gender Equality, Gender Equity AND Women Empowerment: Concepts

ConceptMeaning
Gender EqualitySame rights, opportunities and treatment for all genders
Gender EquityFairness through different treatment based on different needs to achieve equal outcomes
Women EmpowermentProcess of gaining power, agency and control over one's own life
Way forward / key point

Equity recognises that giving the same to unequally placed people will reproduce inequality. Maternity leave for women is gender equity, not inequality. Empowerment is the goal, equality is the destination and equity is the path.
