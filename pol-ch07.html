In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) are in Part IV (Articles 36 to 51). They are ideals that guide the State in making laws and policies.
  • They came from the Irish Constitution (1937) and aim at a welfare state with social and economic justice.
  • They are non-justiciable: courts cannot enforce them. But Article 37 says they are fundamental in governance, and their real force is public opinion and elections.
  • They fall in three groups: Socialistic, Gandhian and Liberal-Intellectual. Amendments (42nd, 44th, 86th, 97th) added or changed some of them.
  • Fundamental Rights prevail over DPSPs today, but the Court (Minerva Mills) calls them two wheels of a chariot that must be balanced.

Key terms

Part IV
Articles 36 to 51 of the Constitution, containing the Directive Principles.
Non-justiciable
Cannot be enforced through a court; no legal remedy if the State does not follow them.
Article 37
Says the principles are not enforceable by any court yet are fundamental in governance; State must apply them in making laws.
Instrument of Instructions
Directions given to Governors under the Government of India Act, 1935; Ambedkar compared DPSPs to it.
Welfare state
State that works for people's welfare, in contrast to the colonial police state.
Article 31C
Added by the 25th Amendment (1971); protects laws that implement Articles 39(b) and 39(c).
Two wheels of a chariot
Minerva Mills (1980) image for Fundamental Rights and DPSPs: both essential, neither absolute.
Directives outside Part IV
Article 335 (SC/ST claims in services), Article 350-A (mother tongue at primary level), Article 351 (Hindi).

Non-justiciable but fundamental in governance (Article 37): sanction is public opinion, not courts.

1 · Source and the Articles of Part IV#

  • The DPSP sit in Part IV, Articles 36 to 51. They are the Constitution's philosophy of social and economic justice and guide the State in making laws and policies.
  • Main source: the Irish Constitution of 1937, which itself took such principles from the Spanish Constitution.
  • Other influences: Baldwin's ideas of the welfare state and Gandhiji's vision of socio-economic justice.

2 · Nature of the Directive Principles#

  • Five ideas define what the DPSP are.

1. Guiding ideals for the State

  • They are constitutional instructions or recommendations to the legislative, executive and administrative organs. By Art. 36, "State" means the same as in Part III: Central and State governments, legislatures, local bodies and public authorities.

2. Resemblance to the Instrument of Instructions

  • They resemble the Instrument of Instructions of the Government of India Act, 1935.
  • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar compared them to those colonial instructions, but in independent India they are addressed to the legislature and executive.
  • The Governor-General once received directions from the British government
  • DPSP now give democratic directions to Indian legislatures

3. Comprehensive socio-economic programme

  • A holistic economic, social and political programme for a modern democratic state.
  • Aim: secure the Preamble's justice, liberty, equality and fraternity.
  • Vision: a welfare state, not the colonial police state. Equitable distribution of wealth and protection of workers show this.

4. Non-justiciable but fundamental

  • Courts cannot enforce them.
  • Yet Art. 37 declares them fundamental to governance and makes it the State's duty to apply them in policy-making.

5. Role in constitutional interpretation

  • Courts use them to judge the reasonableness and constitutionality of laws.
  • A law that furthers a DPSP may be upheld even if challenged under Fundamental Rights.
  • Example in the handout: in land reform cases the Supreme Court upheld laws because they gave effect to Directive Principles promoting social justice.

Instrument of Instructions: then and now

  • 1935 Act: Directions from the British government to the Governor-General
  • DPSP: Democratic directions to the legislature and executive of independent India

Also in the handout

  • This directive ensures affirmative action while safeguarding the efficiency of governance.

3 · Classification of the Directive Principles#

  • The Constitution does not classify them. On content and orientation they fall into three groups: Socialistic, Gandhian, Liberal-Intellectual.

Socialistic (social and economic equality, welfare state)

  • Art. 38 welfare state:
    • Secure a social order based on justice
    • Minimise inequalities
  • Art. 39 equitable livelihood and distribution: adequate means of livelihood, no concentration of wealth, equal pay for equal work, protection of workers and children.
  • Art. 39A equal justice and free legal aid, so economic barriers do not deny justice.
  • Art. 41 right to work, education, assistance: social security in unemployment, sickness, old age, disability.
  • Art. 42 humane work conditions and maternity relief.
  • Art. 43 living wage, decent standard of life, cultural opportunities.
  • Art. 43A workers' participation in management.
  • Art. 47 nutrition, living standards, public health.

4 · Evolution through amendments#

  • Amendments have expanded and modified the Directives, showing the Constitution adapts to national needs.

42nd Amendment Act, 1976

  • The most comprehensive enlargement
  • Four changes for social justice, workers' rights and environment
  • Memory aid (Four 42nd changes): Children (Art. 39 widened: healthy development, physical and moral well-being), Legal aid (new 39A), Workers in management (new 43A), Environment (new 48A): "Clever Lawyers Win Elections."
  • Art. 48A brought ecological concerns into the Constitution for the first time.
  • 39A: access to justice must not depend on economic capacity.
  • 43A: constitutional backing to industrial democracy.

Art. 45 over time

  • Original Art. 45: Free and compulsory education up to 14 years
  • 86th Amendment 2002: Education up to 14 made a Fundamental Right under Art. 21A
  • New Art. 45: State to provide early childhood care and education for all children until six years

Also in the handout

  • 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 The 42nd Amendment is often regarded as the most comprehensive in terms of enlarging the scope of DPSPs.
  • Initially concerned with minimising income inequalities, the amended Article directed the State to also reduce disparities in status, facilities, and opportunities among individuals and groups. 5 · Constitutional status of the Directive Principles#

  • They are guiding values, not enforceable rights. The split between justiciable and non-justiciable rights was recommended by Sir B.N. Rau (Constitutional Advisor) and accepted by the Drafting Committee. FRs (Part III) are enforceable
  • DPSP (Part IV) are not
  • Article 37: "The provisions contained in this Part shall not be enforceable by any court, but the principles therein laid down are nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country, and it shall be the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws." Not legally binding, but a moral and political obligation.

Constituent Assembly voices

  • Alladi Krishna Swamy Ayyar: no responsible ministry could "light-heartedly" ignore the DPSP, since public opinion and political accountability would compel them.
  • Dr. Ambedkar: a government that ignores them answers to the people at elections.

Why the framers made them non-justiciable

Three pragmatic reasons

  • 1: At Independence the country lacked the financial resources for the extensive programme
  • 2: Socio-economic diversity and regional backwardness made uniform enforcement hard
  • 3: The new State, burdened with integration, poverty and development, needed flexibility on order, timing and manner, free of litigation
  • The real sanction is not courts but public opinion, democratic processes and political responsibility. The DPSP are a moral compass towards the Preamble's ideals.
  • Shortcut:
    • Sanction one-liner. Court cannot enforce
    • The voter can. Money, diversity, flexibility = the three reasons

Also in the handout

  • Constituent Assembly debates reinforced this position.
  • Ambedkar highlighted that any government ignoring the DPSPs would be held answerable to the people during elections, thus placing their sanction in popular sovereignty and democratic accountability.
  • First, at the time of Independence, the country lacked sufficient financial resources to implement the extensive social and economic programme envisaged. 24.Second, India’s vast socio-economic diversity and regional backwardness would have made uniform enforcement extremely difficult. 25.Third, the newly independent State, burdened with challenges of integration, poverty, and development, required flexibility to decide the order, timing, and manner of implementing the principles without being constrained by litigation.
  • These situations highlight the risk of friction when ideals clash with institutional roles.
  • These efforts span various sectors and address economic, social, environmental, and political objectives of the Constitution.
  • This directive promotes cultural diversity, linguistic rights, and educational inclusivity, in line with India’s pluralistic ethos.
  • This directive aims to strengthen Hindi while respecting the linguistic diversity of the nation, fostering national integration through language policy.

6 · Significance of the Directive Principles#

  • Fifteen points in the handout, grouped for recall.

Moral and ideological frame

  • Fundamental to governance: the moral and ideological framework within which the State operates.
  • Life-giving provisions (L.M. Singhvi)
  • M.C. Chagla: full implementation would make India a true welfare state
  • Conscience of the Constitution (Granville Austin)
  • Instrument of social revolution
  • Moral and educative value (B.N. Rau): a reminder to governments.
  • Instrument of Instructions (M.C. Setalvad): guidance to build a just social and economic order.
  • Reinforce the Preamble: justice, liberty, equality, fraternity.

Economic democracy

  • Ambedkar: the foundation of economic democracy alongside political democracy, so liberty and equality are substantive, not formal.
  • They balance political democracy with economic justice, so democracy touches everyday life.
  • Supplement to FRs:
    • FRs give political freedoms
    • DPSP recognise social and economic rights for human dignity

Law, policy and politics

  • Guidance for judiciary in judging reasonableness and constitutionality of laws.
  • Background for legislation and policy.
  • Stability and continuity: commitment stays constant when governments change.
  • Accountability tool for the Opposition.
  • Standard for evaluating government performance: citizens can use them as benchmarks.
  • Common political manifesto: a constitutional guide, philosopher and friend for any government.
  • Memory aid (Who said what): Singhvi = life-giving, Chagla = true welfare state, Austin = conscience, Rau = moral and educative (Setalvad = instrument of instructions, Ambedkar = economic democracy): "Some Critics Adore Rights."

Also in the handout

  • Accountability Tool for Opposition: They empower the Opposition to question ruling parties for neglecting social and economic commitments enshrined in the Constitution.

8 · Fundamental Rights versus Directive Principles#

  • Justiciable FRs and non-justiciable DPSP have clashed since 1950. Art. 37 makes DPSP morally binding, but only FRs can be enforced.

The case chain

  • Champakam Dorairajan 1951:
    • Court: FRs prevail over DPSP
    • DPSP must conform and be subordinate. Parliament may amend FRs to give effect to DPSP
  • Parliament acts: First Amendment (1951), Fourth (1955), Seventeenth (1964) operationalise socio-economic directives
  • Golaknath 1967: Parliament cannot amend or abridge FRs, which are sacrosanct. Deadlock: DPSP cannot be implemented if they encroach on FRs
  • 24th and 25th Amendments 1971: 24th restores Parliament's power to amend FRs. 25th inserts Art. 31C
  • Kesavananda Bharati 1973: Strikes down the second part of 31C (judicial review is a basic feature). Upholds the first part: limited primacy of Art. 39(b) and (c) over Arts. 14, 19 and 31
  • 42nd Amendment 1976: Extends the first clause of 31C to laws for all DPSP
  • Minerva Mills 1980: Strikes that expansion down. DPSP again subordinate to FRs, though Arts. 14 and 19 are subject to Art. 39(b) and (c)
The case chain
  • Art. 31C as inserted by the 25th Amendment: (i) A law to implement the socialist DPSP in Art. 39(b) and 39(c) is not invalid for contravening Arts. 14, 19 or 31. (ii) No court can question such a law on the ground that it does not conform to that policy.
  • Memory aid (Case chain): Champakam, Golaknath, Amendments 24th-25th, Kesavananda, Forty-second, Minerva Mills: "Cats Get Angry, Kick Fat Mice."
  • The 44th Amendment (1978) abolished Art. 31, removing the constitutional right to property.
  • Minerva Mills called FRs and DPSP "two wheels of a chariot": the Constitution rests on a delicate balance, neither gets absolute precedence, and DPSP goals must be pursued without undermining FRs.
  • Today: FRs hold precedence, but Parliament may amend FRs to give effect to DPSP, provided the basic structure is preserved.

  • Shortcut: Restrain versus push. FR = restrictive, DP = prescriptive. FR: court, legal sanction, self-operating. DP: voter, moral sanction, needs a law.

From Lakshmikanth · Indian Polity, 8th ed. Ch 9, Directive Principles of State Policy
  • Ambedkar called the Directive Principles 'novel features' of the Constitution. Together with Fundamental Rights they contain the philosophy of the Constitution.
  • A 'police state' only looks after law and order and defence
  • It rests on the 19th century idea of laissez-faire individualism
  • Strictly, Directive Principles are in Articles 38 to 51
  • Article 36 defines State and Article 37 gives their nature
  • Article 39 has six aims: livelihood, fair distribution of resources, no concentration of wealth, equal pay for men and women, health of workers and children, healthy development of children. Article 39(b): ownership and control of resources shared to serve the common good. Article 39(c): economic system must not concentrate wealth.
  • Article 41 covers public assistance in unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement. Article 49 protects monuments of artistic or historic interest of national importance.
  • A 'living wage' is more than a 'minimum wage' (food, shelter, clothing)
  • It adds education, health, insurance. A 'fair wage' lies between them
  • FR versus DPSP:
    • FRs are negative, personal and individualistic and aim at political democracy
    • DPSPs are positive, societarian and socialistic and aim at social and economic democracy
  • K. Santhanam's conflict example:
    • The Centre can direct states to follow DPSPs and may dismiss a non-complying state government
    • The President may reject a bill that ignores them
  • Lok Adalat has the status of a civil court
  • Its award is binding and final with no appeal. The Criminal Procedure Code (1973) separated judiciary from executive (Article 50)
  • Why implementation is incomplete: inadequate finances, unfavourable socio-economic conditions, population explosion and strained Centre-state relations. Other steps: 73rd Amendment (1992) for panchayats, 103rd Amendment (2019) for 10% EWS reservation, 102nd Amendment (2018) for constitutional status to the Backward Classes Commission.

Recall sheet#

Cover the right column and answer. Same questions as the flashcards.

Where are the DPSP placed and from which Constitution were they drawn?
Part IV, Articles 36-51; the Irish Constitution of 1937, which took them from the Spanish Constitution.
What does Art. 36 do?
Defines "State" for Part IV (same as in Part III).
What does Art. 37 say?
DPSP are not enforceable by any court but are fundamental in governance; the State must apply them in making laws.
Which Amendment added Art. 39A, and why?
42nd Amendment, 1976: equal justice and free legal aid for the poor and weaker sections.
Which four changes did the 42nd Amendment make to DPSP?
Art. 39 widened for children; new 39A, 43A (workers in management) and 48A (environment).
What did the 44th Amendment do to Art. 38?
Widened it from income inequalities to also status, facilities and opportunities.
What did the 86th Amendment do to Art. 45?
Replaced free and compulsory education up to 14 (now Art. 21A) with early childhood care and education until six years.
What did the 97th Amendment add?
Art. 43B: cooperative societies.
Name the three groups of DPSP.
Socialistic, Gandhian, Liberal-Intellectual.
Which Articles are Gandhian in the handout?
40 (panchayats), 43 (cottage industries), 43B (cooperatives), 46 (weaker sections), 47 (prohibition), 48 (cow protection).
Which Articles are Liberal-Intellectual?
44, 45, 48 (modern agriculture), 48A, 49, 50, 51.
Which Articles are Socialistic?
38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 43A, 47.
Why are DPSP called an "Instrument of Instructions"?
They resemble the 1935 Act's instructions to the Governor-General; Ambedkar noted they are now addressed to the legislature and executive.
Who recommended the justiciable and non-justiciable split?
Sir B.N. Rau, accepted by the Drafting Committee.
Three reasons for non-justiciability?
Lack of resources; socio-economic diversity; need for flexibility without litigation.
What is the real sanction behind DPSP?
Public opinion, democratic processes and political responsibility.
Who called DPSP the conscience of the Constitution?
Granville Austin.
Who called them life-giving provisions?
L.M. Singhvi.
Name the five criticisms of DPSP.
No legal force; illogical arrangement; no coherent philosophy; conservative Fabian outlook; potential for constitutional conflict.
What did Champakam Dorairajan (1951) hold?
FRs prevail over DPSP; DPSP must conform; Parliament may amend FRs to implement DPSP.
What did Golaknath (1967) hold?
Parliament cannot amend or abridge FRs.
What did the 24th and 25th Amendments (1971) do?
24th restored Parliament's power to amend FRs; 25th inserted Art. 31C.
What does Art. 31C protect?
Laws implementing Art. 39(b) and (c) from invalidity under Arts. 14, 19 and 31, with no court questioning them (second part later struck down).
Kesavananda on 31C?
Second part struck down (judicial review is basic feature); first part upheld.
Minerva Mills (1980)?
Struck down the 42nd Amendment's extension of Art. 31C; FRs and DPSP are two wheels of a chariot.
FR versus DPSP in one line?
FR restrictive, enforceable, legal sanction; DPSP prescriptive, non-enforceable, moral and political sanction.
When were the Planning Commission and NITI Aayog set up?
Planning Commission 1950; NITI Aayog replaced it in 2015.
Which Acts of 1961 and 1976 serve women's welfare?
Maternity Benefit Act (1961); Equal Remuneration Act (1976).
Which directives lie outside Part IV?
Art. 335 (SC/ST in services), Art. 350-A (mother tongue), Art. 351 (Hindi).
What did the Legal Services Authorities Act (1987) do?
Framework for free and competent legal aid; Lok Adalats with binding awards.
Mains 2015: Discuss the possible factors that inhibit India from enacting for its citizens a uniform civil code
Intro: The Uniform Civil Code is a directive in Art. 44, listed among the Liberal-Intellectual principles of Part IV Body: Art. 44 is a Directive Principle and DPSP are non-justiciable so courts cannot compel Parliament to enact it; DPSP need enabling legislation or policy measures to be realised; implementation depends on the political will of the State; the framers left the State flexibility over the order and timing of implementing the principles; India's vast socio-economic diversity makes uniform enforcement difficult; the real sanction is public opinion and electoral accountability; Art. 37 makes it the duty of the State to apply the principle in making laws. Close: Art. 44 is a duty without a court behind it: the push must come from public opinion and political will.

Traps#

Trap 1 · Same article, different group Article 47 appears in Socialistic (nutrition and public health) and in Gandhian (prohibition of intoxicants). Article 43 appears in Socialistic (living wage) and Gandhian (cottage industries). Article 48 appears in Gandhian (cow protection) and Liberal-Intellectual (modern agriculture). Rule: judge by the part of the article being described, not the number.
Trap 2 · Article 45 changed meaning Originally Article 45 asked for free and compulsory education up to age 14. The 86th Amendment (2002) made elementary education a Fundamental Right (Article 21A) and changed Article 45 to early childhood care and education until six years. Rule: 6 to 14 = right (21A); below 6 = directive (Article 45).
Trap 3 · Two halves of Article 31C Kesavananda (1973) struck the second part (no court can question the law) and kept the first part for Articles 39(b) and (c). The 42nd Amendment extended protection to all DPSPs; Minerva Mills (1980) struck that extension. Rule: protection stays only for Article 39(b) and 39(c); judicial review stays.
Trap 4 · Who wins: FR or DPSP Champakam Dorairajan (1951) said FRs prevail. Golaknath (1967) said FRs cannot be amended at all. Today FRs have precedence, yet Parliament may amend FRs to implement DPSPs if the basic structure is respected. Rule: FR supreme, but not absolute over DPSP.
