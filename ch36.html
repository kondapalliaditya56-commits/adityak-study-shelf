Gandhi's ideology was a moral politics: a stateless ideal, a village economy and Satyagraha, held together by truth, non-violence and an inclusive Swaraj.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Ideology comes first: Gandhi's cultural nationalism and moral critique of the West give him a stateless, village-based ideal, and Satyagraha (rising tempo, control, S-CW-S, an undefined Swaraj) is the method that carries ordinary people towards it.

  1. Why start with ideology. Modern politics has goals, plans, a programme, leadership, voice and money, and the root of all of them is ideology. Gandhi is the Father of the Nation because he brought the masses (religions, castes, genders, regions, ethnicities) into a movement to establish a Nation State, though his was the maximum contribution, not the only one.
    → So where do we read his mind? In one book.
  2. Hind Swaraj and a cultural nationalist. Hind Swaraj (1909) was written on a ten-day sea voyage as a dialogue between a Reader and an Editor. Gandhi stood in the tradition of Dayanand's Arya Samaj (1875), Vivekananda's Ramakrishna Mission (1897) and Bankim, but he took it further: he dressed and spoke like the masses, used Ram Rajya, Sita and Draupadi, and used religion to mobilise.
    → Was he then against everything modern?
  3. Not anti-modern, but moral. Gandhi criticised Western modernity, built on industrial capitalism, the profit motive and the coercive State, not reason or science. Western civilisation was Godless, and Indians enslaved themselves when doctors, lawyers and railways became instruments of an alien system looting India. He called an act immoral (adharma), not merely illegal.
    → If the State is coercive, what should replace it?
  4. A stateless ideal. Gandhi's end-goal, like Marx's, was a stateless society, but through Enlightened Anarchism: every individual his own master, morality in place of law. He wanted direct democracy and Village Republics, with the Gram Sabha (Article 243) as the living legacy, and he accepted parliamentary democracy with a weak State only as a transition. Ambedkar replied that a strong State with constitutional law protects the oppressed.
    → And the economy under such a polity?
  5. The village economy. He wanted a village-based, self-sufficient economy modelled on ancient India, rejecting the Industrial Revolution that uproots villagers into factories. Almost everyone else was pro-IR; Mao turned against it in 1958 for a different reason. Land should come by voluntary gift: Vinoba Bhave's Bhoodan (Telangana, 1951).
    → But what of capitalists?
  6. Trustee and arbitrator. He did not want capitalists abolished: under Trusteeship the owner holds the enterprise for the welfare of workers and community, so ownership stays and purpose changes. Disputes go to Arbitration: reveal the truth of both sides, then a middle path. The same method united Moderates and Extremists in 1920.
    → The method for politics is Satyagraha.
  7. Satyagraha and its two principles. Satyagraha, truth-force, is Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience, Constructive Work, Non-Violence and Morality, with a Satyagrahi who is truthful, fearless, non-violent, ready to suffer and morally superior. He would not gain from the evil doer's weakness (he backed Britain in both wars) and hated breach of promise (Montague 1917, Rowlatt Act 1919).
    → How did he run a movement?
  8. Tempo, control, truce. He began with lighter forms and escalated; he insisted on a controlled mass movement and withdrew the NCM after Chauri Chaura (22 policemen, February 1922). The truce was never idle: Constructive Work filled it, so S-T-S was really S-CW-S, a lineage from the Ramakrishna Mission to Sabarmati.
    → Why did it work?
  9. Why it worked, and an undefined Swaraj. Satyagraha united Moderates and Extremists (Surat 1907), let common men and women join, and made repression a propaganda disaster. Gandhi never defined Swaraj, so a peasant, worker, woman and nationalist could each find their own meaning; Jinnah and Tagore were uneasy about this.
    → Where did the ideas come from?
  10. Five influences. Ruskin's Unto This Last (1862) gave Sarvodaya and the Phoenix Settlement (1904); Tolstoy (1894) gave non-resistance and the Tolstoy Farm (1910); Thoreau (1849) gave civil disobedience; the Gita gave Nishkama Karma; the Sermon on the Mount gave love of the enemy. The mix made Satyagraha a universal method.
    → And the record of his life?
  11. Fasts, writings, jails. Nine fasts run from Ahmedabad 1918 to Delhi 1948, including the Epic Fast 1932 (Poona Pact, 71 to 147) and the 1933 temple-entry fast. His papers run from Indian Opinion 1903 and Hind Swaraj 1909 to Young India 1919 and Harijan 1933. Ten arrests run from 1906 to Quit India 1942, with the 1922 trial as the only major trial in India.

In simple words#

  • Gandhi's ideology (in Hind Swaraj, 1909) is the root of his movements: a cultural nationalism that spoke to the masses in their own symbols, with religion as the strongest social bond.
  • He criticised Western modernity, not science:
    • Its profit motive, industrial capitalism and the coercive State
    • His own ideal was a stateless society (Enlightened Anarchism) with Village Republics and direct democracy
  • His economy was village self-sufficiency, with Trusteeship for capitalists and Arbitration for disputes, and voluntary land gifts (Bhoodan) instead of State force.
  • Satyagraha (NC + CD + CW + Non-Violence + Morality) worked through a rising tempo, a controlled mass movement and S-CW-S
  • It united Moderates and Extremists and let ordinary people join
  • He drew on Ruskin, Tolstoy, Thoreau, the Gita and the Sermon on the Mount
  • His fasts, publications and arrests are the record that exam questions test

1 · Hind Swaraj (1909): the most authentic source#

Story link

Because ideology is the root, we need the source of his mind. Ask: what kind of book is Hind Swaraj, and why is it called authentic?

  • Before the mass movements (Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience, Quit India), understand what Gandhi believed and what kind of India he wanted.
  • Hind Swaraj (1909) is the most authentic source of his ideology.
  • It was written during a ten-day sea voyage and is structured as a dialogue between a "Reader" and an "Editor".
  • In it Gandhi takes positions that were unconventional then, and remain so today.

2 · What kind of nationalist was Gandhi?#

Story link

Because the book shows a nationalist of culture, not just politics. Ask: whose tradition did he stand in, and how did he go further than Tilak?

  • Gandhi was a Cultural Nationalist, in a tradition built over decades before he arrived.
Teacher's board

Nationalism has three branches: culture, politics, economics.

2.1 The tradition before him

  • British racism (Indian civilisation "primitive and inferior") stimulated cultural nationalism: Indians answered that their civilisation was equally worthy of pride.
  • Contributors: Swami Dayanand Saraswati and the Arya Samaj (1875), Swami Vivekananda and the Ramakrishna Mission (1897) and Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay.
Teacher's board
  • Cultural nationalism = identity rooted in civilisation, culture and shared heritage, not race, language or territory alone
  • Different from political nationalism (the demand for self-governance)
  • Culture = art, music, paintings, religion, folk lore, literature, historical heroes, festivals, spiritual questions and philosophies, all expressing the values they represent, the way of life.
  • Values help resolve ethical dilemmas in economy, society and geopolitics.
  • The way of life covers economic, political and cultural life
  • Culture covers social relations, art, religion.
  • Point 3: "India is a civilization".

2.2 How Gandhi took it further

  • He dressed like the masses, spoke their language and invoked symbols they understood.
  • Ram Rajya was not a sectarian claim: it was a shared civilisational vocabulary across class and caste.
  • Sita and Draupadi as icons of suffering and resilience drew women, till then spectators, into the national movement.
  • He used religion to mobilise, because in India religion is a stronger social bond than class.
  • The Moderates, with petitions aimed at the Western-educated middle class, never grasped this.
  • He took cultural nationalism into a concrete economic and political programme: this made him unique among cultural nationalists.
Teacher's board
  • Ram Rajya = a society with no oppression: no gender-based, caste-based or religion-based oppression.
  • Religion used to mobilise masses: similar to the Extremists, different from the Moderates.
Teacher's board

Contrast with linguistic communalism: MG = Gujarati, yet the 1920 Nagpur Session of the INC said use Hindi as far as possible (board tag: "utilitarianism").

Teacher's board
  • Extremists:
    • Tilak = Ganapati festival 1893
    • Bankim = Bharat Mata (an ideologue, not a politician)
  • They believed in a secular use of religion.
Teacher's board
  • A society can be organised in multiple ways, like seating a school class in rows.
  • "isms" are chashmas (spectacles)
  • Our ism = nationalism.
  • The rows: communalism (value neutral: H, M, S, C, Bu, Atheist; racism sits under it), linguistic nationalism (L1 to L7), casteism, genderism (feminism, patriarchal, LGBTQ), class-ism (landed, capitalist, ryot, factory worker, artisan), regionalism.
  • In India the religion row binds the most people.

2.3 Gandhi, Tilak and Tagore

  • Tilak used religion too: Ganesh Chaturthi (1893) and Shivaji Festival (1896).
  • Difference:
    • Tilak's mobilisation built a relatively combative nationalism
    • Gandhi's aimed at universal inclusion across all communities.
  • Tagore shared the love of Indian civilisation but feared mass religious nationalism could slide into dangerous intolerance.
  • Gandhi: you cannot raise consciousness before you first mobilise the people.
Teacher's board
  • Tilak's festivals = "CULTURE (not RELIGION)"
  • Shivaji Festival = the day of his coronation
  • Gandhi's own hymn line: "Ishwar Allah tero naam".
  • Board tag beside Tagore's fear: "[UTILITARIANIST]".

3 · Was Gandhi anti-modern?#

Story link

Because a cultural nationalist might seem to reject everything new, the chapter clears the doubt. Ask: what exactly did he criticise?

  • He was not anti-modern. He criticised Western modernity: industrial capitalism, the profit motive as sole purpose and the coercive State.
  • His critique was of its content, not of reason, science or human progress.
Teacher's board

This is cultural nationalism at work: the coercive State = strong centralised State; Western civilisation is "Godless" = morality-less.

3.1 The moral critique

  • Modern Western civilisations are Godless: their only motive is profit, with no concern for morality.
  • Gandhi's persistent language of morality links to the pre-1857 and 1857 resistance movements, framed in moral and religious terms (British rule as polluter of the Indian moral order).
  • He updated this resistance for modern politics.
  • Self-criticism: Indians are responsible for their own enslavement: Indian minds were enslaved by capitalism, and doctors, lawyers and railways became instruments of an alien economic system looting India.

3.2 Dayanand and the nation

  • Swami Dayanand made the argument decades earlier: Swarajya = Indians enslaved themselves by abandoning their heritage.

4 · Stateless society and Enlightened Anarchism#

Story link

Because the West's State is coercive, he wanted none at all. Ask: how does his stateless society differ from Marx's, and why did Ambedkar object?

  • Gandhi believed in a stateless society, reaching the same end-goal as Marx by a different path.
Same destination, different journeys (Prelims 2020)
  • (Marx: the State withers away after the communist revolution; Gandhi: when individuals reach perfect moral self-regulation.)
Teacher's board
  • UPSC PYQ marked here (Prelims 2020, below).
  • Same end-goal as Marx but different rationale and methods.
  • MG's Swaraj = Enlightened Anarchism:
    • Self-rule of each individual
    • His favourite Fundamental Right = individual liberty.
  • Marx wanted the Dictatorship of the Proletariat (working class).
  • Morality, not State law, guides behaviour
  • With perfect self-regulation even multi-party democracy (MPD) is unnecessary
  • Swaraj = not just independence from Britain but every individual his own master. The word was taken literally.
  • Gandhi wanted self-rule of each individual, not the home rule of the Extremists, the indirect democracy of the Moderates, or the class rule of Marx.
Teacher's board
  • MG: rule of each individual. Marx: rule of the working class.
  • So MG is more democratic and wants greater decentralisation.
Teacher's board

Extremists, Moderates, Bose, Nehru, Patel: Swaraj = self-rule = indirect (representative) democracy, which is India today: (1) people elect their representatives; (2) representatives rule (legislature and executive). MG wanted direct democracy.

4.1 Village Republics and the critique of parliamentary democracy

  • In place of the strong State-led democracy of the West, Gandhi wanted Village Republics built on direct democracy and mutual cooperation.
  • In the Gram Sabha each villager is effectively an MP.
  • Article 243 (Gram Sabha as foundation of Panchayati Raj) is Gandhi's most concrete institutional legacy in the Constitution.
  • His critique: multi-party democracy leads to the will of political parties, not of the people.
  • The voter hands over power every few years and is forgotten.
Teacher's board
  • Direct democracy = every person is MLA/MP and minister.
  • That is why MG wanted Village Republics
  • In the Gram Sabha MLA/MP = villager.
  • Aim: maximum decentralisation of political power to the level of the individual.
Teacher's board

Madhya marg: use technology (blockchain, internet) for referendums on certain matters and to seek public opinion, combining direct democracy with MPD.

Teacher's board
  • Voting once in 5 years = a power of attorney to the MP: rule of the political party, not of the people.
  • Example:
    • An MP from a constituency of traders
    • A Bill taxes traders and the money helps the poor.
  • As MP, how do you vote: Yes or No?
Handout alert
  • Today's relevance. The handout links the critique to the anti-defection law, party whips overriding an MP's conscience, and the weakening of Parliament as a deliberative body.
  • Board: "to rule of parties and not of MPs".

4.2 Gandhi among the leaders

  • Tilak and Nehru wanted a strong central State to replace the British State
  • Patel built one
  • Bose wanted temporary authoritarian rule to remove poverty fast
  • Gandhi alone feared State power itself, not only British power.
  • Ambedkar's sharpest disagreement:
    • A strong State with constitutional law was the only protection of the oppressed
    • A village republic would be local caste dominance with a romantic name.
  • Transitional concession:
    • Gandhi accepted parliamentary democracy with a State as weak as possible until the higher morality for a stateless society arrived
    • Then the State should wither away.
  • A concession to reality, not an endorsement.

5 · The economic vision: village self-sufficiency#

Story link

Because the State was to wither, the economy had to be of villages. Ask: why was he nearly alone against the Industrial Revolution?

  • Gandhi wanted a village-based, self-sufficient economy modelled on ancient India.
  • The Industrial Revolution uproots rural masses (forced migration) into urban factories where they are dependent, exploited and cut off from their roots.
Teacher's board
  • A big factory = magnet of jobs.
  • Villages around it send people in: village, town, city, with increasing population and homes packed around the centre. Urbanisation is linked to IR.
  • On the IR Gandhi stood almost alone: Marx, Moderates, Extremists, Nehru and Ambedkar were all pro-IR.
  • Mao turned anti-IR with the Great Leap Forward (1958) to protect communist power from the bourgeoisie that industry creates.
  • Gandhi rejected it to protect the moral self-sufficiency of village life: same conclusion, different reasoning.
  • Land: Gandhi opposed forceful redistribution (State coercion against morality over law) and favoured voluntary donation by landlords.
  • This inspired the Bhoodan Movement of Vinoba Bhave, Telangana, 1951, walking village to village asking landlords to donate land to the landless.

6 · Trusteeship and Arbitration: reforming capitalism without destroying it#

Story link

Because he kept capitalists but changed their purpose. Ask: how does the Trust differ from the Company, and how does Arbitration work?

  • Gandhi did not demand abolition or expropriation of capitalists.
  • Trusteeship: a capitalist holds his enterprise as a trustee for the welfare of his workers and the community.
  • The ownership stays
  • The purpose of ownership changes.
Company against Trust: where does the profit go?
Teacher's board
  • MG opposed the Marxist end of the capitalist class.
  • Company/factory:
    • Decision maker = capitalist
    • Motive = profit.
  • Trust:
    • Decision maker = trustee (the factory owner)
    • Motive = welfare of the beneficiary (= workers).
  • Profit example, Rs 200: Company re-invests for 2 more factories plus shareholders, no rise in wages.
  • Trust: Rs 100 to workers (wages, pension, bonus) and Rs 100 for 1 more factory plus shareholders.
  • Three answers to inequality:
    • Marx called capitalists parasites to be expropriated by force
    • Ambedkar wanted the State to control major industries
    • Gandhi asked capitalists to reform themselves morally.
Teacher's board

Those who wanted State control of industries: Ambedkar and also Bhagat Singh, Bose, Nehru (board list; first name partly unclear).

6.1 Arbitration

  • Disputes between workers and capitalists: Arbitration, a transparent inquiry into both sides' accounts (the capitalist's balance sheet, the worker's cost of living) for a just middle path, not strikes or lockouts that harden conflict and disrupt the economy.
Teacher's board
  • Step 1: reveal the truth of both sides (workers bring household expenses; capitalist shows balance sheet and books of accounts).
  • Step 2: a mid-path with the help of a neutral arbitrator: win-win, not lose-lose.
  • A lockout = the capitalist locks the factory.
Teacher's board

Dispute resolution = arbitration in three steps: (1) inquiry; (2) reveal the truth of both sides; (3) madhyamarg (middle path).

6.2 Arbitration in political form

  • In 1920 Gandhi united the Moderates and Extremists, split since Surat (1907), through Satyagraha: Moderates accepted NC and CD
  • Extremists gave up violence.
Teacher's board
  • Moderates = only petition (pen) + non-violence (NV).
  • Extremists = pen + NC + CD + CW + violence if repressed.
  • Satyagraha = pen and NV, plus NC, CD, CW: each side gave up something, both gained a mass movement.

7 · Universal disarmament: not abstract pacifism#

Story link

Because moral politics rejects selective force. Ask: what was the Arms Act 1878 asymmetry he challenged?

  • Gandhi favoured universal disarmament, but with a precise Indian context: the Arms Act of 1878 under Viceroy Lytton 76-80 disarmed Indian civilians while exempting Anglo-Indians and Eurasians.
  • The colonial State armed itself while disarming the colonised.
  • Disarmament is just only when universal, not as a colonial tool of repression.

12 · The inclusive approach: why Swaraj was deliberately never defined#

Story link

Because India is plural, the goal was left open. Ask: why did Jinnah and Tagore worry about it?

  • Gandhi knew India's plurality (caste, religion, language, class) and never precisely defined the goal of Swaraj: this was deliberate political architecture, not vagueness. Interests at times collided.
  • Swaraj meant end of high rents to a peasant, better wages to a worker, dignity and agency to a woman, self-governance to a nationalist intellectual.
  • Leaving it open maximised the support base across social plurality.
  • His simple attire, Hindi, Ram Rajya, Sita and Draupadi let masses, who would never attend a constitutional conference, feel the movement was personally theirs.
  • Jinnah's sharpest criticism: "Swaraj" was a Hindu cultural concept dressed as a national one and Muslim interests could not be safe inside its undefined borders.
  • Gandhi's counter: its openness made it inclusive of all.
  • Tagore's worry, the mirror image: undefined mass emotion, even well-intentioned, could become narrow and intolerant.
  • Gandhi:
    • Mobilise first, refine after
    • You cannot teach philosophy to an empty stomach.

13.1 John Ruskin, Unto This Last (1862)

  • Gandhi said it "transformed my life." Message:
    • The good of the individual lies in the good of all

13.2 Leo Tolstoy, The Kingdom of God is Within You (1894)

  • The State, military and Church rest on organised violence
  • A morally self-regulated individual needs none of them
  • Non-resistance to evil by force and moral self-regulation struck Gandhi strongly.
  • He corresponded with Tolstoy and named the Tolstoy Farm (1910) near Johannesburg after him: a Satyagraha training commune, the prototype of his Indian ashrams.
Teacher's board

13.3 Henry David Thoreau, Civil Disobedience (1849)

  • When a law is unjust the citizen has a moral duty to disobey
  • Obeying an unjust State is itself a moral failure
  • The term "civil disobedience" entered Gandhi's vocabulary through Thoreau, one of the two pillars of Satyagraha with non-cooperation.

13.4 Bhagavad Gita: Nishkama Karma

  • Action as duty without attachment to its result: his moral compass, letting him act decisively in politics while inwardly detached from power, fame and gain.
  • He called the Gita his "spiritual dictionary" and read it daily.

13.5 Sermon on the Mount (New Testament)

  • Christ's non-violence (turn the other cheek, love the enemy, overcome evil with good) confirmed that non-violence is the highest moral courage.
  • With the Gita, the twin spiritual pillars of Ahimsa.
  • The eclecticism (Hindu, Christian, Western secular) was deliberate: it made Satyagraha a universal moral method, not a Hindu religious one, and credible from South Africa to London to rural India.

Recall sheet#

Read the question, answer in your head, then tap to check.

What is Hind Swaraj?1909 book, written on a ten-day sea voyage, a dialogue of Reader and Editor: the most authentic source of Gandhi's ideology.
What is cultural nationalism?Identity rooted in civilisation, culture and shared heritage, not race, language or territory alone; distinct from political nationalism.
Who built cultural pride before Gandhi?Dayanand (Arya Samaj 1875), Vivekananda (Ramakrishna Mission 1897), Bankim.
How did Gandhi take it further?Dressed and spoke like the masses, Ram Rajya, Sita and Draupadi, religion used to mobilise; plus an economic and political programme.
Tilak vs Gandhi on religion?Tilak's festivals (1893, 1896) built a combative nationalism; Gandhi aimed at universal inclusion.
Was Gandhi anti-modern?No: he criticised Western modernity (industrial capitalism, profit, coercive State), not reason or science.
What did he say about doctors, lawyers, railways?They were instruments of an alien, profit-driven system looting India; Indians enslaved themselves by accepting it.
What is Enlightened Anarchism?True self-rule: each individual his own master, morality over law, no need even for representative democracy.
Gandhi vs Marx on the State?Both end in a stateless society (Prelims 2020); Gandhi by moral self-regulation, Marx after the Dictatorship of the Proletariat.
What did Gandhi want in place of Western democracy?Village Republics: direct democracy and mutual cooperation; Gram Sabha (Article 243).
What was his critique of MPD?It gives the will of parties, not of the people; the voter is forgotten between elections.
Why was Ambedkar against it?A strong State with constitutional law protects the oppressed; the village republic would be local caste dominance.
What was the transitional concession?Parliamentary democracy with a weak State until the higher morality for a stateless society arrives.
What was Gandhi's economic vision?Village-based self-sufficient economy; against the IR (forced migration); voluntary land donation (Bhoodan 1951).
What is Trusteeship?The capitalist holds his enterprise as a trustee for workers and community; ownership stays, purpose changes.
How does Arbitration work?Reveal the truth of both sides, then a mid-path with a neutral arbitrator; no strikes or lockouts.
Why universal disarmament?The Arms Act 1878 (Lytton 76-80) disarmed Indians and exempted Anglo-Indians and Eurasians; disarm British police and army first.
What is Satyagraha?Truth-force: NC + CD + CW + NV + Morality, an extra-constitutional non-violent mass struggle.
Qualities of a Satyagrahi?Truthful, fearless, non-violent, ready to suffer and love the evil doer, morally superior (SOULS).
Gandhi's two principles?Do not benefit from the evil doer's weakness; hate breach of promise (Rowlatt 1919 after Montague 1917).
Why Gandhi refused to join the INM in WWI?He would not use Britain's weakness; justice was on Britain's side as victim of aggression.
What is rising tempo?Start with lighter forms (pen, NC) and escalate to CD; NCM plan: one year NC, then CD.
Why was Chauri Chaura decisive?22 policemen were killed (Feb 1922); Gandhi withdrew the NCM at its peak to keep non-violence.
What is S-CW-S?Struggle, Constructive Work, Struggle: the truce is preparation (atmashakti, training, organisation, local leaders).
Why was Satyagraha effective?It united Moderates and Extremists, let common people participate, and made repression a propaganda disaster.
Why was Swaraj left undefined?To include everyone: peasant (rents), worker (wages), woman (dignity), intellectual (self-governance).
Jinnah's and Tagore's objections?Jinnah: a Hindu concept in national dress. Tagore: undefined mass emotion can become intolerant. Gandhi: mobilise first.
Five influences on Gandhi?Ruskin, Tolstoy, Thoreau, the Gita, the Sermon on the Mount.
Ruskin's message and the Phoenix Settlement?Good of the individual is in the good of all; Phoenix (1904) near Durban.
Tolstoy and Thoreau?Tolstoy: non-resistance, Tolstoy Farm 1910 near Johannesburg. Thoreau: civil disobedience as a moral duty (1849).
Which two fasts must not be confused?1932 Epic Fast (Communal Award, Poona Pact, 71 to 147) and 1933 fast (temple entry).
Fasts in order?1918 Ahmedabad, 1922 NCM, 1924 unity, 1932 Epic, 1933 purification, 1939 Rajkot, 1943 Quit India, 1947 Calcutta, 1948 Delhi.
Key publications?Indian Opinion 1903, Hind Swaraj 1909, Young India and Navajivan 1919, Harijan 1933.
The only major trial of Gandhi in India?1922, before Judge C.N. Broomfield, Ahmedabad, six years; other detentions had no trial.
Where was he held in 1942?Aga Khan Palace, Pune (August 1942 to May 1944); Kasturba and Mahadev Desai died there.
Mains skeleton: Gandhi vs Bose?Intro: same goal, different means. Body: non-violence, no exploiting weakness vs "Give me blood", Axis alliance; fear of State vs temporary authoritarian rule; village republics vs heavy industrialisation. Close: never settled.
Mains skeleton: Gandhi vs Tagore?Intro: love of Indian civilisation. Body: Tagore feared intolerance, educate first; Gandhi mobilise first; Nai Talim vs Shantiniketan. Close: inclusion with care.
