1 · How did Sikhism begin, and what were its core beliefs?#

  • Guru Nanak Dev (1469-1539) was a contemporary of Babur 26-30. Sikhism rose from 1500 onwards, built on four core principles:
  • One supreme and formless God.
  • Direct access to God without rituals or priests.
  • Rejection of the caste system.
  • Emphasis on honest living and selfless service.
  • Guru Nanak undertook four major spiritual journeys (Udasis) over 30 years, covering about 30,000 km across India, South Asia, Tibet and Arabia. He finally settled in Kartarpur (present-day Pakistan), where he established the Langar, the free communal kitchen designed to promote equality.
Memory hook

Nanak's four principles: ODRH, "One Direct Road Home".

Walk One Direct Road Home.

Decode: One supreme formless God; Direct access to God without rituals or priests; Rejection of the caste system; Honest living and selfless service. Founder Guru Nanak Dev (1469-1539), contemporary of Babur 26-30; rose from 1500 onwards.

Why it sticks: the road image gives the order of the four ideas.

Memory hook

Udasis: the three-zero pair.

4 journeys, 30 years, 30,000 km: "four trips, thirty twice."

Decode: 4 Udasis; 30 years; about 30,000 km across India, South Asia, Tibet and Arabia. Ending: settled at Kartarpur (present-day Pakistan) and set up the Langar, the free communal kitchen for equality.

Why it sticks: 30 and 30,000 are the same digits, so you only memorise one number plus the 4.

2 · When did conflict begin between the Sikhs and the Mughal State?#

  • Initially there was no conflict. But as the Sikh population grew and Sikhs began demanding a Sikh State in and around Anandpur (west of Shimla and south of Chamba), an area under the Shivalik Hill Chiefs who were Mughal allies, religious and political tensions built up.
  • First flashpoint, 1606: the 5th Guru, Arjan Dev, was executed by Jahangir 05-27. A turning point: a peaceful spiritual movement began its transformation into a martial community.
  • Second flashpoint, 1675: the 9th Guru, Teg Bahadur, was executed in Delhi by Aurangzeb 58-07.
Memory hook

Two flashpoints: 5th in 06, 9th in 75.

Five-oh-six, nine-seven-five: Jahangir 05-27's Guru is the 5th, Aurangzeb 58-07's Guru is the 9th.

Decode: 1606, 5th Guru Arjan Dev executed by Jahangir 05-27 (first flashpoint; a peaceful movement turns martial). 1675, 9th Guru Teg Bahadur executed in Delhi by Aurangzeb 58-07 (second flashpoint).

Why it sticks: the Guru's number matches the order of the rulers, early ruler with the earlier Guru.

Memory hook

Anandpur: W of Shimla, S of Chamba.

W-Shimla, S-Chamba: the compass letter comes before the town.

Decode: Anandpur lies west of Shimla and south of Chamba, in the Shivalik foothills, an area under Shivalik hill chiefs who were Mughal allies; the Sikh demand for a State here started the tension.

Why it sticks: "W then S" is the order of the two clues in the sentence.

3 · The Khalsa (1699)#

  • In 1699, at Anandpur, the 10th Guru, Gobind Singh 76-08, established the Khalsa, a Sikh military organisation created to defend the Sikh Panth and struggle for a Sikh State. The handout compares it to an NSG/STF: its creation marked the formal militarisation of the Sikh community's political ambitions.
Memory hook

Guru numbers 5, 9, 10 = years 06, 75, 99.

Fifth in 1606, ninth in 1675, tenth in 1699: "6, 75, 99."

Decode: 5th Guru Arjan Dev, 1606, executed by Jahangir 05-27; 9th Guru Teg Bahadur, 1675, executed by Aurangzeb 58-07; 10th Guru Gobind Singh 76-08, 1699, founds the Khalsa at Anandpur (a military body like NSG/STF, to defend the Panth and struggle for a Sikh State).

Why it sticks: the last two digits rise 06, 75, 99; the 10th Guru answers the two executions with an army.

4 · December 1704 to October 1708: the Sahibzadas and the Guru's death#

  • December 1704, Battle of Chamkaur: the Guru's two elder sons, Sahibzada Ajit Singh (aged 18) and Sahibzada Jujhar Singh (aged 14), died in battle. The same month his two younger sons, Sahibzada Zorawar Singh (aged 9) and Sahibzada Fateh Singh (aged 6), were buried alive in a wall on the orders of Wazir Khan, the Mughal Faujdar of Sirhind. (A Faujdar = a district military commander, comparable in local authority to a Superintendent of Police today. Since 2022, 26 December is observed as Veer Bal Divas in memory of the younger Sahibzadas.)
  • 1707: the new emperor Bahadur Shah (1707-12), following the pacifist policy (diplomacy more than war), granted the Guru a high Mansab and promised a Sikh State in and around Anandpur. The promise was never fulfilled.
  • 7 October 1708: Wazir Khan arranged assassins to attack the Guru at Abchal Nagar Sahib Gurdwara in Nanded, Maharashtra, where he had accompanied Bahadur Shah. The Guru killed the assassins himself but later succumbed to his injuries, dying at the age of 42. No action was taken against Wazir Khan; Anandpur was never given to the Sikhs.
Memory hook

Four Sahibzadas: AJ fought, ZF fell.

Ages fall 18, 14, 9, 6 as the names go A, J, Z, F.

Decode: Dec 1704, Battle of Chamkaur: Ajit Singh (18) and Jujhar Singh (14) died in battle. Same month Zorawar Singh (9) and Fateh Singh (6) were buried alive in a wall on the orders of Wazir Khan, Mughal Faujdar of Sirhind (a Faujdar is like a Superintendent of Police). 26 December = Veer Bal Divas since 2022.

Why it sticks: elders 2 in battle, youngers 2 in the wall; the two pairs are 18-14 and 9-6.

Teacher's board

1707: Aurangzeb 58-07 dies, and the Mughal pacifist policy follows: "better relations with rivals." Implementation for the Guru: a very high Mansab [Zat] and Anandpur promised (an Article 370-like status, like Rohilkhand, Jats, Farrukhabad, the Marathas under Shahu 19-49, Mysore, Rajputs). 1708: in Nanded the Guru is killed by men sent by Wazir Khan [Faujdar of Sirhind], who was also responsible for the death of the Guru's four sons. Wazir Khan was not punished by the Mughals; Anandpur was not given. (Compare the same pacifist-policy argument in Chapter 4, section 5.)

Memory hook

1707 promise, 1708 plot.

Seven: a Promise. Eight: a Plot. And the Pledge is never kept.

Decode: 1707 Bahadur Shah (pacifist policy after Aurangzeb 58-07's death) gives Guru Gobind Singh 76-08 a high Mansab and promises Anandpur; 7 Oct 1708 Wazir Khan's assassins strike at Abchal Nagar Sahib, Nanded; the Guru kills them but dies of his injuries aged 42. Wazir Khan is not punished; Anandpur is not given.

Why it sticks: P-P-P: Promise, Plot, Pledge broken, one letter, one year each.

5 · Why the Guru declared the Guru Granth Sahib the final Guru#

  • A few days before his death, at Abchal Nagar Sahib, Guru Gobind Singh 76-08 declared that he would be the last living Guru and that all spiritual authority would henceforth be vested in the Guru Granth Sahib Ji, the holy book he had compiled.
  • The decision was deliberate: to prevent community tensions between the Jat community, which dominated the Khalsa, and the Khatri community of traders, to which all ten Gurus had belonged. By vesting authority in the holy book rather than a human successor, the question of community dominance was permanently closed.
  • The Guru Granth Sahib Ji was installed at the Akal Takht in the Golden Temple complex, Amritsar. From the Akal Takht the Gurus issued Hukumnamas (formal orders) and Sikh warriors took blessings before going into battle against the Mughals.
  • The 19th-century sects of Nirankaris and Namdharis believe in a living Guru, which has historically led to serious conflicts within the community.
Memory hook

Jat = Jang, Khatri = Khazana: why the Book became Guru.

Jats fought, Khatris funded; the Gurus were Khatris, so the Guru gave the crown to a Book.

Decode: Jats dominated the Khalsa (the army); Khatris were traders and moneylenders, and all ten Gurus were Khatris. Guru Gobind Singh 76-08 vested authority in the Guru Granth Sahib Ji (installed at the Akal Takht, where Hukumnamas were issued) to end the rivalry for good. Exception: 19th-century Nirankaris and Namdharis believe in a living Guru.

Why it sticks: J-J (Jat, Jang) versus K-K (Khatri, Khazana); a Book cannot be a Jat or a Khatri.

Teacher's board

The Guru Granth Sahib has two aspects: the Holy Book and the Guru itself. Disrespect = Beadbi (very sensitive today also). Jats dominated the Khalsa; Khatris were the men of money (merchants, moneylenders, ijaradars) ([*Jahandar Shah 12-13 introduced Ijaradari in Punjab and Bengal]); all the Gurus were Khatris. The teacher's ink places Banda Bahadur 08-15's small state "in and around Patiala", which takes revenge by executing Wazir Khan.

6 · Banda Bahadur 08-15: the first Sikh State, and why it collapsed#

  • From 1708 the rebellion was carried forward by Banda Bahadur 08-15. He defeated and killed Wazir Khan at the Battle of Chappar Chiri on 10 May 1710, which also led to the conquest of Sirhind.
  • He established a Sikh State over territories between the River Ravi and the River Jamuna, with its own coins, its own officials and its own seal for administrative orders (the teacher's ink: full independence).
  • Farrukhsiyar 13-19 appointed Abdus Samad Khan as Governor of Lahore specifically to suppress this Sikh State. By 1715 Banda Bahadur 08-15 was defeated; captured and executed in Delhi in 1716 by Farrukhsiyar 13-19.
Memory hook

Banda Bahadur 08-15's State: Ravi to Jamuna, COS.

Banda Bahadur 08-15's State kept the COS: Coins, Officials, Seal.

Decode: From 1708 Banda Bahadur 08-15 carried on; 10 May 1710 Chappar Chiri, kills Wazir Khan, conquers Sirhind; State between the Ravi and the Jamuna with its own Coins, Officials and Seal (full independence). Farrukhsiyar 13-19 sends Abdus Samad Khan as Governor of Lahore; 1715 defeated, 1716 executed. Chapter ruling on territory: the handout says Ravi to Jamuna (the answer-writing wording); the teacher's ink says "in and around Patiala".

Why it sticks: COS = the three marks of a real State.

Memory hook

Wazir Khan's circle: 04, 08, 10.

Oh-four he walled the boys; oh-eight he sent the knives; ten, the circle closed.

Decode: Dec 1704 buries the two younger Sahibzadas alive; 7 Oct 1708 arranges assassins against Guru Gobind Singh 76-08; 10 May 1710 killed by Banda Bahadur 08-15 at Chappar Chiri, in revenge.

Why it sticks: 04, 08, 10 steps by 4 then 2, and the third event is the revenge.

Note

Flag on the territory: the handout places Banda's State between the Ravi and the Jamuna; the teacher's ink on the board says "a small state in and around Patiala." Both appear in the class material; the handout's wording is the answer-writing one.

Why his State fell (three reasons):

  1. Khatri traders turned against him. Constant warfare hurt trade, and the commercial class needs decisive victors and political stability to function.
  2. Divide and rule. Jahandar Shah 12-13 introduced Ijaradari (the revenue-farming system) in Punjab specifically to co-opt Khatri traders with business opportunities. Both Jahandar Shah 12-13 and Farrukhsiyar 13-19 offered Mansabs to Khatri traders, who switched sides. (Note: this further increased the number of Mansabdars, adding to the Jagirdari Crisis; see Chapter 2.)
  3. Hindu Jat zamindars switched sides. Churaman 95-21 Jat (1695-1721), for instance, went over to the Mughals after they recognised his Jat Kingdom, granting him conditional dynastic autonomy in exchange for loyalty.
Memory hook

Why Banda Bahadur 08-15 fell: the K-D-J DJ.

The K-D-J DJ changed the tune three times and the crowd walked off the floor.

Decode: K = Khatri traders turned (constant war hurt trade; commerce wants a decisive winner); D = Divide and rule: Jahandar Shah 12-13 brought Ijaradari to Punjab to co-opt Khatris, and both he and Farrukhsiyar 13-19 offered them Mansabs; J = Jat zamindars switched, e.g. Churaman 95-21 went over after the Mughals recognised his Jat Kingdom. Also: Banda Bahadur 08-15 was not a Guru.

Why it sticks: three letters, three reasons, in the chapter's own order (1, 2, 3).

Teacher's board

Khatris stopped supporting him (money); Churaman 95-21 Jat likewise (guns / military), co-opted by accepting a Jat kingdom within the Mughal empire. The Khatris wanted peace because trade was hurt by constant wars, so they chose the stronger side. Banda Bahadur 08-15 was not a Guru, so he lacked the Gurus' authority. Jahandar Shah 12-13 introduced Ijaradari in Punjab and Bengal (a business opportunity), and Jahandar Shah 12-13 and Farrukhsiyar 13-19 made Khatris Mansabdars. The general rule (double-starred): when a personality weakens, power leaves (downward or sideways), and factionalism rises (Khatris against Banda Bahadur 08-15).

7 · What kept the Sikh State alive: the Misl confederacy#

  • The defeat of Banda Bahadur 08-15 did not end the Sikh State. The Mughals were too weak to fully reassert control over Punjab, and power became decentralised among Misl Chiefs.
  • Misls are kinship-based clans. They united during wars, particularly against Afghan invasions, but otherwise operated independently. Whenever a Misl conquered territory, the lands were distributed among its members in proportion to their military and monetary contribution. Even the lowest-ranking soldier received a share of land.
  • By 1770 there were 12 major Misls in Punjab, with the Dal Khalsa at the centre under a leader chosen by the Misl chiefs: a confederacy structure with a weak centre and strong constituent units.
  • Takeaway (handout): compare it to the Maratha Confederacy under the Peshwas, where a nominal centre held together largely autonomous regional chiefs. Decentralisation was both a source of resilience and, ultimately, a vulnerability.
Teacher's board

The death of Banda Bahadur 08-15 left no strong centre, so power decentralised to the misl chiefs. Polity structure: a Confederacy (of misls); fragmented. The board's sketch shows one big circle (Punjab) with about a dozen circles inside (misls), "roughly 40-60 misls" (the handout's formal count is 12 major Misls by 1770). The Dal Khalsa = the assembly of misl chiefs: e.g. on Abdali's 1748 invasion the Dal Khalsa will meet and prepare a joint defence, e.g. Manupur 1748, where Abdali was defeated (see Chapter 4).

Memory hook

12 Misls in one basket; the teacher's 40-60 is only a rough sketch.

Decode: Misls = kinship clans; united in wars (esp. against Afghan invasions), otherwise independent; conquered land shared by military and monetary contribution (even the lowest soldier got a share). By 1770: 12 major Misls, with the Dal Khalsa (assembly of Misl chiefs) at the centre. Chapter ruling: the handout's formal count is 12 by 1770; the board's "roughly 40-60" is the sketch. Manupur 1748: Dal Khalsa meets on Abdali 47-72's invasion, Abdali 47-72 defeated. Same shape as the Maratha Confederacy under the Peshwas.

Why it sticks: 12 = a dozen, a basket with a weak centre.

8 · Ranjit Singh 01-39 unifies the Misls#

  • Ranjit Singh 01-39 of the Sukerchakia Misl played a crucial role in defeating Zaman Shah's third Afghan invasion of 1799 (the slide reads: he rose during Zaman Shah's Afghan invasions of 1796-1799). This gave him the platform to establish dominance over the other Misls, and he was coronated King (Maharaja) of the Sikh State in 1801. The other Misl chiefs were reduced to tribute-paying vassals, retaining conditional control of their lands in return for loyalty and tribute to the King (the teacher's ink: lands held as Jagirs, not Watan).
Teacher's board

Zaman Shah (Abdali's successor) invaded: "crisis = opportunity." The Sukerchakia misl of Ranjit Singh 01-39 emerged the most powerful. 1801: coronation. 1809: the Treaty of Amritsar made the Sutlej the border with the Company State (British India) and brought a military alliance with the EIC. The Misl chiefs' lands: Jagirs, not Watan.

Memory hook

Jagir, not Watan: the chiefs held on conditions.

Ranjit Singh 01-39's chiefs got a Jagir with strings; the Watan Jagir of Chapter 5's rebels was a different bargain.

Decode: after the 1801 coronation (Ranjit Singh 01-39), Misl chiefs became tribute-paying vassals with conditional control of land in return for loyalty and tribute: Jagirs, not Watan.

Why it sticks: "Loyalty and tribute, or no land."

  2. 1808: conquered Jammu from the Dogras (the handout adds: in 1819 he returned Jammu to the Dogras but within the Sikh Empire: the Dogras became vassals; the slide adds: Jammu later granted as a jagir to Kishore Singh in 1820, and his son Gulab Singh formally made Raja of Jammu in 1822).
  3. 1809: the handout lists "conquered the whole of Punjab" as the third step; the slide reads: "signed the Treaty of Amritsar ... secured his eastern frontier."

9 · The Treaty of Amritsar (1809)#

  • In 1809 Ranjit Singh 01-39 signed the Treaty of Amritsar with the EIC, represented by Charles Metcalfe, the EIC's Resident in the Delhi court. (Recall that the Scindias had been removed from Delhi in 1803 and the EIC had set up indirect rule there through a British Resident.) Four outcomes:
  • The EIC recognised Ranjit Singh 01-39 as the sovereign ruler of Punjab.
  • It led to a military alliance (later link to the EIC's Policy of Ring Fence).
  • The Sutlej River was fixed as the border between the Sikh State and the Company State.
  • Ranjit Singh 01-39 was free to expand on his side of the Sutlej without British interference.
Memory hook

Treaty of Amritsar 1809: R-M-S-F, "Ranjit Singh 01-39 Met Metcalfe, Shook hands, Freed".

Recognised, Military, Sutlej, Free.

Decode: 1809, Ranjit Singh 01-39 and the EIC (Charles Metcalfe, Resident at Delhi): Recognised as sovereign of Punjab; Military alliance (link to Ring Fence); Sutlej fixed as border; Free expansion on his side. Map: north of the Sutlej = RJ 01-39, south = EIC.

Why it sticks: Metcalfe's name echoes "Met", and R-M-S-F is the order of the four outcomes.

Teacher's board

On the Punjab map: north of the Sutlej = Ranjit Singh 01-39 (RJ 01-39); south of the Sutlej = the EIC.

10 · How far did Ranjit Singh 01-39 expand after 1809?#

  • With his eastern border secured, Ranjit Singh 01-39 turned west and north:
  • 1818: removed the Afghans from Multan.
  • 1819: conquered Kashmir. (Recall that Ahmad Shah Abdali 47-72 captured Punjab and Kashmir; Punjab was taken back by the Marathas in 1758 but not Kashmir; Ranjit Singh 01-39 completed what the Marathas left unfinished.)
  • 1834: conquered Peshawar (present-day Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, bordering Afghanistan along the Durand Line). Peshawar was conquered by Hari Singh Nalwa, whose reputation was so fearsome that Afghan mothers used his name to frighten unruly children: "If you don't stop crying, Haria Ragle will turn up."
Memory hook

West and north: Multan, Kashmir, then the long wait for Peshawar.

18, 19 back to back; then 34 and a legend: Haria Ragle.

Decode: after 1809, east secured, Ranjit Singh 01-39 turns west and north: 1818 Multan (Afghans removed); 1819 Kashmir (the Marathas retook Punjab in 1758 but not Kashmir; he completed it); 1834 Peshawar, conquered by Hari Singh Nalwa (present-day Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Durand Line), whose name Afghan mothers used to quiet children.

Why it sticks: Multan and Kashmir are consecutive years; Peshawar needs the one name, Nalwa.

10.1 The 1838 Tripartite Treaty

  • In 1838 Ranjit Singh 01-39 signed the Tripartite Treaty between the Sikh State, the EIC and Shah Shuja of Afghanistan. The design: to establish British indirect rule in Afghanistan through Shah Shuja as a puppet ruler. The aim: to create a buffer of Afghanistan and Punjab against the feared Russian invasion of British India from the north-west.
Teacher's board

British fear of Russia: the chain drawn on the board is Russia, then Afghanistan, then the Khyber Pass, then the Sikh State, then the Company State (British India). Afghanistan and Punjab are the buffer.

Memory hook

Tripartite 1838: Russia Always Knocks; Sikhs Cushion.

R-A-K-S-C: the chain from Russia down to the Company.

Decode: Russia → Afghanistan → Khyber Pass → Sikh State → Company State. Three parties (Tri): the Sikh State, the EIC and Shah Shuja of Afghanistan; Shah Shuja is the puppet for British indirect rule; Afghanistan and Punjab are the buffer against the feared Russian invasion.

Why it sticks: the sentence is the board's chain, top to bottom.

Ranjit Singh 01-39's four-legged charpai.

The Maharaja sits on a charpai with four legs; kick one out and he wobbles.

Memory hook

Kardar = the four C's.

Collector, Court, Cop, Customs: one man in the Taluqa.

Decode: Kardar, chief administrative officer of a Taluqa (district), is the revenue Collector, local magistrate (Court), police chief (Cop) and Customs officer. He often acted as an Ijaradar, but the ryot was not to be oppressed by very high LR.

Secular cabinet: Fakir for Foreign, Dewan for Dhan.
