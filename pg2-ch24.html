1 · Elections · Election Commission OF India · Appointment · Tenure · Removal · Functions · Plenary Powers#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Elections module pp. 4-7. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The Constitution lays down the machinery of elections in Articles 324-329. The Election Commission of India supervises elections to Parliament, State Legislatures, President and Vice-President (not local bodies). It became a three-member body in 1993, appointments now follow the 2023 Act with a Prime Minister-led Selection Committee, and Article 324 gives it plenary powers to ensure free and fair elections where the law is silent.

Constitutional Provisions ON Elections

Elections: where the rules come from

  • The Constitution primarily provides detailed procedures for the election of the President and Vice-President.
  • Details regarding elections to Parliament and State Legislatures are largely determined by Parliament through legislation under Article 327.
ArticleMajor provisions
324Superintendence, direction and control of elections vested in the Election Commission of India (ECI).
325No separate electoral rolls or exclusion from electoral rolls on grounds only of religion, race, caste or sex.
326Elections to Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies on the basis of Universal Adult Suffrage.
327Parliament can make laws relating to elections, including electoral rolls and delimitation of constituencies.
328State Legislatures can make election laws where Parliament has not legislated, subject to the Constitution.
329Restricts judicial interference in electoral matters. Election disputes are challenged through election petitions.

Election Commission OF India

Jurisdiction

  • Article 324 provides that the superintendence, direction and control of electoral rolls and elections shall be vested in the ECI.
  • Its jurisdiction covers:
    • 1. Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha elections
    • 2. State Legislative Assembly and Council elections
    • 3. Presidential and Vice-Presidential elections
  • Important: elections to Panchayats and Municipalities are conducted by the State Election Commissions under Articles 243K and 243ZA, respectively.
Key point

Composition and evolution: a multi-member body

YearDevelopment
1950-1989ECI functioned as a single-member body, consisting of the Chief Election Commissioner (CEC).
October 1989Two additional Election Commissioners (ECs) were appointed, making it a three-member body.
January 1990The earlier expansion was revoked, restoring its single-member status.
1991The Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Conditions of Service) Act regulated tenure and service conditions.
October 1993On 1 October 1993, two ECs were reappointed under Article 324(2). An Ordinance amended the 1991 Act, later replaced by the 1993 Amendment Act (Act 4 of 1994), establishing the statutory framework for the three-member ECI.
2023The CEC and Other Election Commissioners Act, 2023 introduced a statutory appointment procedure and replaced the 1991 law.

Decision-making

  • The CEC acts as Chairman of the Election Commission.
  • All three members have equal decision-making powers.
  • Decisions should preferably be taken by consensus. In the absence of consensus, decisions are taken by majority (2:1).
  • T. N. Seshan v. Union of India (1995): the Supreme Court upheld the multi-member structure and the equal decision-making powers of the Election Commissioners.

Appointment OF CEC AND Election Commissioners

Earlier system

  • Before the 2023 legislation, appointments were made by the President on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers.
  • There was no separate Parliamentary law laying down a selection committee for these appointments.
  • Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India (2023): the Supreme Court directed that, until Parliament enacted a law, the CEC and ECs would be selected by a committee comprising the Prime Minister, the Leader of Opposition in Lok Sabha and the Chief Justice of India.

CEC and Other Election Commissioners Act, 2023

  • The Act provides a statutory framework for the appointment, qualifications, tenure, service conditions and removal of the CEC and ECs.
Selection Committee memberRole
Prime MinisterChairperson
Leader of Opposition in Lok SabhaMember
Union Cabinet Minister nominated by PMMember

Rest of the process

  • If no recognised Leader of Opposition exists, the leader of the single largest opposition party in the Lok Sabha serves on the Committee.
  • Search Committee: headed by the Union Minister of Law and Justice, with two members of Secretary rank. It prepares a panel of five candidates. The Selection Committee may also consider persons outside this panel.
  • Qualifications: persons of integrity who hold or have held a post equivalent to Secretary to the Government of India, with knowledge and experience in election management and conduct.
  • Appointment: the President appoints the CEC and ECs on the recommendation of the Selection Committee.

Major criticisms

  • 1. Executive Dominance: the PM and the nominated Cabinet Minister give the Government a 2:1 majority in the Selection Committee.
  • 2. Exclusion of CJI: the Chief Justice of India was replaced by a Cabinet Minister, removing the judicial member envisaged in the interim arrangement under Anoop Baranwal.
  • 3. Executive-Controlled Shortlisting: the Search Committee consists of executive officials, raising concerns about institutional independence.
  • 4. Restricted Eligibility: the Secretary-rank requirement excludes several other categories of experienced professionals.
  • 5. Unequal Removal Protection: the Act retains stronger constitutional protection for the CEC than for other Election Commissioners.
  • Counterargument: Article 324(2) expressly permits Parliament to make a law on appointments, and the 2023 Act establishes a formal statutory selection process.

Functions OF Election Commission

Administrative functions

  • 1. Preparation of Electoral Rolls: supervises the preparation, revision and correction of electoral rolls.
  • 2. Conduct of Elections: to Parliament, State Legislatures and the offices of President and Vice-President.
  • 3. Model Code of Conduct (MCC): prepares and enforces the MCC to ensure free and fair elections.
  • 4. Election Expenditure: monitors expenditure, examines candidates' expenditure accounts and enforces prescribed limits.
  • 5. Political Parties: registers political parties and determines their recognition as National and State Parties, according to prescribed criteria.
  • 6. Election Symbols:
    • Allots symbols and adjudicates disputes under the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968. A National Party's reserved symbol is protected throughout India
    • A State Party's reserved symbol is primarily protected in the State where it is recognised, subject to the Symbols Order
  • 7. Election Management Training: through the India International Institute of Democracy and Election Management (IIIDEM), established in 2011.

Advisory and quasi-judicial functions

  • 1. Disqualification of Legislators: advises the President and Governors on post-election disqualification of MPs and State legislators under Articles 103 and 192.
  • 2. Party Recognition and Symbols: determines party recognition and resolves disputes relating to recognised political parties and their symbols.
  • 3. Election Accounts: examines violations concerning election expenditure statements and may order disqualification under Section 10A of RPA, 1951.

Plenary Powers OF ECI

Mohinder Singh Gill v. Chief Election Commissioner (1978)

  • The Supreme Court held that Article 324 gives the Election Commission plenary powers to ensure free and fair elections.
  • The ECI can exercise powers where election laws are silent or insufficient.
  • However, these powers cannot be exercised contrary to an existing law.
  • Example: Vellore Lok Sabha Election (2019): the election was rescinded following reports of large-scale inducement of voters through money.
Way forward / key point

Significance: Article 324 enables the Commission to deal with unforeseen electoral circumstances, while remaining subject to the Constitution and Parliamentary laws.

2 · Representation OF THE People ACT 1950 · RPA 1951: Disqualifications · LILY Thomas · Sections 8, 8A, 9, 9A, 10, 10A, 11A, 11B#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Elections module pp. 7-10. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • RPA 1950 governs electors and rolls (CEO, DEO, ERO, residence, overseas electors). RPA 1951 sets the statutory disqualifications: conviction (Section 8), corrupt practices (8A), dismissal (9), government contracts (9A), government company posts (10) and unfiled expenditure accounts (10A). Lily Thomas (2013) struck down the three-month protection for sitting legislators.

Representation OF THE People ACT, 1950

The RPA, 1950 primarily deals with electors, electoral rolls, allocation of seats, constituencies and voter registration. It provides the legal framework for preparing and maintaining electoral rolls under the supervision of the ECI.

Key point

The RPA, 1951 provides the statutory framework for the conduct of elections, qualifications and disqualifications of candidates, corrupt practices, electoral offences and election disputes. Its provisions on disqualification seek to ensure the integrity of elected representatives.

Section 8: Disqualification ON Conviction

Key point

Section 8 provides for disqualification based on criminal conviction, not merely registration of an FIR or filing of a chargesheet.

Section 8(1): conviction for specified offences

  • Promoting enmity between communities, electoral bribery and undue influence.
  • Rape and offences against women, as specified in the Act.
  • Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 (untouchability).
  • Customs Act, 1962, for specified offences.
  • Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967.
  • Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985.
  • Religious Institutions (Prevention of Misuse) Act, 1988.
  • Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971.
  • Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act, 1987.
  • Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988.
  • Specified electoral offences under RPA, 1951, including promoting enmity and booth capturing.
  • Some offences listed refer to legislation that has subsequently been repealed, including the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act, 1987 and the Prevention of Terrorism Act, 2002.
  • The Act also retains references to certain IPC provisions, which must be read along with the transition and savings provisions following the introduction of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023.
SentenceDisqualification (Section 8(1))
Only fineSix years from the date of conviction
ImprisonmentFrom the date of conviction and for six years after release
Value addition

Important: for offences covered by Section 8(1), the specific statutory offence is decisive. A sentence of two years is not required in every case.

Section 8(2): other specified offences

  • Offences relating to hoarding and profiteering
  • Adulteration of food or drugs
  • Specified offences under the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961
  • Condition: imprisonment for not less than six months.
  • Disqualification: from the date of conviction and for six years after release.

Section 8(3): other criminal offences

  • A person convicted of an offence not covered by 8(1) or 8(2), and sentenced to imprisonment for not less than two years, is disqualified.
  • Disqualification period: from the date of conviction and for six years after release.
  • Key distinction: the two-year imprisonment requirement applies to Section 8(3) and should not be generalised to all convictions under Section 8.
Sub-sectionOffencesSentence condition
8(1)Specified serious offences / lawsSpecific offence decisive; fine = 6 years from conviction
8(2)Hoarding, profiteering, adulteration, Dowry Prohibition ActNot less than 6 months
8(3)Any other offenceNot less than 2 years

Section 8(4) AND LILY Thomas (2013)

Earlier protection for sitting legislators

  • Section 8(4) provided special protection to sitting MPs and MLAs. Their disqualification would not take effect for three months after conviction.
  • If an appeal or revision application was filed within that period, disqualification could remain suspended until its disposal.

Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013)

  • The Supreme Court declared Section 8(4) unconstitutional.
  • 1. No Special Protection: sitting legislators cannot enjoy protection unavailable to other candidates.
  • 2. Immediate Disqualification: a legislator convicted of an offence attracting Section 8 becomes disqualified from the date of conviction.
  • 3. Filing an Appeal Is Not Sufficient: merely filing an appeal does not automatically suspend disqualification.
  • Significance: ended the special statutory protection enjoyed by sitting legislators and strengthened political accountability.

Remedies against disqualification on conviction

  • 1. Appeal against conviction: before the appropriate appellate court. However, filing an appeal does not automatically remove disqualification.
  • 2. Stay of conviction: the appellate court may, in exceptional circumstances, stay the conviction itself.
  • Lok Prahari v. Election Commission of India (2018): the Supreme Court clarified that when an appellate court stays the conviction, the disqualification under Section 8 ceases to operate while the stay remains effective.

Disqualification FROM Voting: Sections 11A AND 11B

Section 11A: disqualification from voting

  • 1. Specified Electoral Offences: conviction for certain offences such as bribery, undue influence, personation or other specified electoral offences attracts disqualification for six years.
  • 2. Corrupt Practices: a person disqualified under Section 8A may also be disqualified from voting for the corresponding period.

Section 11B: removal of disqualification

  • The Election Commission may remove certain disqualifications arising under Section 11A, subject to statutory provisions.
  • Important: the restrictions on voting by persons in prison or police custody are separately governed by Section 62(5).

Overall Significance AND Issues

Significance and issues

  • 1. Probity in Public Life: prevents persons convicted of specified offences from occupying legislative positions.
  • 2. Conflict of Interest: restrictions on government contracts and specified offices seek to protect independent legislative functioning.
  • 3. Electoral Integrity: penalties for corrupt practices and false expenditure accounts strengthen accountability.
  • 4. Delay in Criminal Trials: candidates facing serious criminal charges may continue to contest because mere pendency of a criminal case does not ordinarily attract Section 8.
  • 5. Misuse of Criminal Proceedings: disqualification before conviction may be misused through politically motivated criminal cases.

Way forward

  • 1. Serious Criminal Charges: consider the Law Commission's 244th Report (2014) recommendation for disqualification at the stage of framing of charges in serious offences, with safeguards against political misuse.
  • 2. Speedy Trials: prioritise criminal trials involving MPs and MLAs through special courts and time-bound proceedings.
  • 3. Strengthen Expenditure Accountability: improve scrutiny of election expenditure accounts and penalties for deliberate non-compliance.
  • 4. Balance Fairness and Presumption of Innocence: prevent criminalisation without allowing false or politically motivated accusations to become instruments of exclusion.

In simple words
  • RPA 1951 lays out who notifies elections, who runs them (CEO, DEO, Returning Officer), the nomination timetable and deposits, polling rules, VVPAT matching, tie-breaking by lots, expenses, and how elections are challenged only through election petitions in the High Court within 45 days, with appeals to the Supreme Court within 30 days.

4 · Corrupt Practices · Electoral Offences · Criminalisation OF Politics · Vohra Committee#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Elections module pp. 13-18. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Section 123 lists corrupt practices (bribery, undue influence, communal appeals, false statements, excess expenditure, booth capturing and more) which are tried through election petitions, while Sections 125-136 list electoral offences punished by criminal courts. Criminalisation of politics has risen (46% of Lok Sabha 2024 MPs declared criminal cases) because of winnability, money power and weak party regulation; the Supreme Court has built a disclosure regime and special courts for MPs and MLAs.

Corrupt Practices Under RPA 1951: Section 123

Key point

Section 123 of RPA, 1951 defines corrupt practices that undermine the fairness of elections.

SectionCorrupt practiceMeaning
123(1)BriberyOffering or accepting gratification to influence voting, candidature or withdrawal from an election.
123(2)Undue InfluenceDirect or indirect interference with the free exercise of electoral rights, including threats and intimidation.
123(3)Appeal on Religious or Caste GroundsSeeking votes on grounds of religion, race, caste, community or language, or through prohibited appeals to religious or national symbols.
123(3A)Promotion of EnmityPromoting hatred or enmity between communities on grounds of religion, race, caste, community or language.
123(3B)Glorification of SatiPromoting or glorifying the practice of Sati for electoral advantage.
123(4)False StatementsPublishing knowingly false statements of fact about another candidate's personal character, conduct or candidature to damage electoral prospects.
123(5)Illegal Conveyance of VotersHiring or procuring vehicles or vessels for the free transportation of voters to or from polling stations, subject to statutory exceptions.
123(6)Excess Election ExpenditureIncurring or authorising expenditure in contravention of Section 77.
123(7)Assistance from Government ServantsObtaining prohibited assistance from specified government officials for electoral advantage.
123(8)Booth CapturingBooth capturing by a candidate, agent or another person under the circumstances specified in the Act.
Value addition

Government servants under Section 123(7) include Gazetted Officers, Judges, Magistrates, Armed Forces Personnel, Police Officers, Excise Officers and specified Revenue Officers.

Important judicial developments

  • 1. S. Subramaniam Balaji v. Government of Tamil Nadu (2013): promises of freebies in election manifestos do not, by themselves, constitute bribery under Section 123. However, such promises raise concerns about free and fair elections.
  • 2. Abhiram Singh v. C. D. Commachen (2017): adopted a broad interpretation of Section 123(3), prohibiting electoral appeals based on the religion, caste, community or language of candidates, voters or other relevant persons.
  • 3. Lok Prahari v. Union of India (2018): strengthened disclosure requirements concerning candidates' sources of income and financial interests.
  • Important: mere possession of disproportionate assets does not automatically establish a corrupt practice. Its legal consequences depend on disclosure requirements, material facts and the applicable statutory provisions.

Key point

Important provisions

  • 1. Promoting Enmity (Section 125): imprisonment up to three years, a fine or both.
  • 2. False Affidavit (Section 125A): imprisonment up to six months, a fine or both.
  • 3. Silence Period (Section 126): prohibits specified election-related activities during the 48 hours ending with the hour fixed for conclusion of polling. Often described as a Peace Period, allowing voters to make their decisions without last-minute campaign pressure.
  • 4. Booth Capturing (Section 135A):
    • Ordinary offenders one to three years plus fine
    • Government servants three to five years plus fine
  • 5. Paid Holiday (Section 135B): eligible employees must receive a paid holiday on polling day. Violation may attract a fine of up to Rs 500.
  • 6. Dry Period (Section 135C): restricts the sale or distribution of liquor during the prescribed 48-hour period before the conclusion of polling.

Criminalisation OF Politics

Two linked processes

  • Criminalisation of Politics: when politicians, whether or not they have a criminal background, use criminal methods or anti-social elements to increase their chances of winning elections.
  • Politicisation of Criminals: when criminals or anti-social elements themselves contest elections and participate in politics.
  • Both processes weaken free and fair elections and democratic governance.
Key point

Important findings from Lok Sabha 2024

  • 251 of 543 MPs (46%) declared criminal cases
  • 170 MPs (31%) declared serious criminal cases
  • 27 elected MPs declared previous convictions
  • The number of MPs with declared criminal cases increased by 55% since 2009
  • With serious criminal cases by 124%

  • Important: declaration of a criminal case is not equivalent to conviction. The cases may be pending, contested or subsequently dismissed. (verify figures)

Factors responsible

  • 1. Winnability: parties select candidates with criminal backgrounds based on their ability to win rather than ethical considerations.
  • 2. Centralised Ticket Allocation: local party units lack sufficient influence over ticket distribution.
  • 3. Increasing Money Power: high election expenditure encourages black money, illegal financing and criminal networks.
  • 4. Decline in Ethical Norms: became more visible after the Fourth General Election (1967).
  • 5. Inadequacies of Criminal Justice System: delays, manipulation of investigations and prolonged trials.
  • 6. Absence of Party Regulation: inadequate enforceable norms on composition, internal democracy and functioning of parties.
  • 7. Politician-Criminal Nexus: dependence on criminal networks for funding, intimidation and mobilisation of voters.
  • 8. Identity and Patronage Politics: caste, community loyalties and personal networks encourage support despite serious criminal allegations.

Effects

  • 1. Against Free and Fair Elections: money and muscle power restrict the free exercise of electoral choice.
  • 2. Reduces Society's Faith in Democracy: criminal politicians may focus on protecting themselves from legal proceedings rather than public service.
  • 3. Affects Good Governance: corruption, patronage and diversion of public resources.
  • 4. Affects Integrity of Civil Servants: honest officials may face political pressure, intimidation and victimisation.
  • 5. Causes Social Disharmony: a culture of violence, weakened social trust and harmful examples for youth.
  • 6. Weakens Rule of Law: political influence may interfere with police investigations, prosecution and enforcement.
  • 7. Reduces Legislative Accountability: political competition shifts from policy issues to money, identity and personal influence.

Vohra Committee (1993) on criminal-political nexus

  • The N. N. Vohra Committee (1993) examined the nexus between criminals, politicians and government functionaries.
  • 1. Criminal-Political Nexus: criminal gangs and mafia organisations had developed links with political leaders and government officials.
  • 2. Parallel Government: these networks were practically running a parallel government in certain areas.
  • 3. Institutional Penetration: criminal networks had acquired influence over administration, law enforcement and political processes.
  • Assessment:
    • Brought the problem into the open, described as the "Cat out of the Bag" moment
    • Criticised for limited concrete institutional reforms, sometimes described as a "Damp Squib"

Steps to address criminalisation

  • 1. Strict Enforcement of Laws: relating to taxation, real estate, industry, money laundering and black money.
  • 2. Protect Honest Bureaucrats: against victimisation when acting against criminal politicians.
  • 3. Regulate Political Parties: enforceable rules on composition, internal elections, functioning and accountability.
  • 4. Check Criminality in Youth Wings: they often supply future political leadership.
  • 5. Voter Awareness: voter literacy and disclosure of criminal antecedents.
  • 6. Amend Section 8(3) of RPA: consider reducing the two-year imprisonment threshold, with safeguards.
  • 7. Disqualification at Charge-Framing Stage: recommended by the ECI and Law Commission, subject to safeguards against fabricated cases.
  • 8. Transparency in Political Funding: strengthen disclosure of fundraising and expenditure by parties.
  • 9. Paid News: consider making Paid News an Electoral Offence.
  • 10. Strengthen Electoral Offences: increase penalties where appropriate and establish special courts for speedy disposal.
  • 11. Voters' Councils: local mechanisms to report criminality and election irregularities, particularly on polling day.
  • 12. Videography: of election proceedings, particularly in sensitive constituencies.
  • 13. Quick and Exemplary Action: prompt prosecution and punishment of convicted criminal politicians.

5 · Electoral Systems AND Their Aberrations · NOTA · Right TO Reject · Right TO Recall#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Elections module pp. 18-20. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • NOTA (PUCL 2013) lets voters express dissatisfaction secretly but carries no power to reject: the highest candidate still wins. The Right to Reject would force a fresh election; the Right to Recall would remove a representative mid-term. Both raise serious practical problems, and the Law Commission (255th Report, 2015) recommended neither.

NONE OF THE Above (NOTA)

What NOTA is

  • NOTA allows voters to express their unwillingness to vote for any contesting candidate while maintaining the secrecy of voting.
  • PUCL v. Union of India (2013): the Supreme Court directed the Election Commission to introduce NOTA on EVMs and ballot papers.
  • Earlier system: Rule 49-O of the Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961 allowed an elector to refuse to vote after identification. However, the process did not adequately protect the secrecy of the elector's choice. The rule was subsequently omitted following the introduction of NOTA.

Nature and limitations of NOTA

  • 1. Neutral Vote: NOTA records dissatisfaction but does not invalidate the election.
  • 2. No Right to Reject: even if NOTA receives more votes than every contesting candidate, the candidate securing the highest number of candidate votes is declared elected.
  • 3. Limited Electoral Impact: NOTA has not produced substantial changes in electoral outcomes.
  • 4. Democratic Expression: it enables voters to express dissatisfaction without supporting an unsuitable candidate.

Way forward / key point

Assessment: NOTA strengthens electoral expression but does not provide an effective mechanism for rejecting candidates.

Right TO Reject

Meaning

  • Voters can reject all contesting candidates, resulting in a fresh election when the prescribed threshold is satisfied.
  • Unlike NOTA, it gives a binding consequence to electoral rejection.

Issues

  • 1. Re-contesting: whether rejected candidates should be allowed to contest again from the same or another constituency.
  • 2. Disqualification Period: difficulty in deciding how long rejected candidates should remain ineligible.
  • 3. Endless Elections: repeated rejection may prevent the completion of the electoral process.
  • 4. Administrative Burden: election officials and security personnel may remain occupied with repeated elections.
  • 5. Financial Cost: multiple elections would be costly, particularly in difficult terrains.
  • 6. Prolonged MCC: repeated elections may prolong the Model Code of Conduct, affecting government decisions and policy announcements.
Value addition

Law Commission (255th Report, 2015): did not recommend introducing the Right to Reject in India.

Right TO Recall

Meaning

  • Allows voters to remove an elected representative before the completion of the representative's tenure through a prescribed procedure.
  • The grounds may include apathy, non-performance, corruption or misconduct, depending on the applicable legal framework.

Arguments in favour

  • 1. Deterrence: discourages apathy, corruption and underperformance.
  • 2. Public Trust: makes elected representatives answerable between elections.
  • 3. Continuous Accountability: citizens can monitor performance throughout the tenure.
  • 4. Election Expenditure: the threat of recall may discourage excessive electoral expenditure and irresponsible conduct.

Issues

  • 1. Signature Requirement: difficulty in deciding the minimum number of signatures and verifying their authenticity.
  • 2. Re-contesting: whether recalled representatives should be allowed to contest again.
  • 3. Eligibility: whether persons who did not vote in the original election should participate in recall proceedings.
  • 4. Timing: difficulty in deciding how soon after an election recall may be initiated.
  • 5. Vague Grounds: criteria such as non-performance and apathy are subjective and difficult to measure.
  • 6. Legislative Independence: representatives may avoid decisions that are beneficial in the long term but unpopular in the short term.
  • 7. Localism: legislators may focus disproportionately on local demands rather than broader legislative responsibilities.
  • 8. Frequent Elections: repeated recall proceedings increase electoral expenditure and administrative burden.
  • 9. Money and Muscle Power: recall may encourage vote-buying, intimidation and political instability.
  • 10. Dominant Groups: powerful sections may misuse recall to threaten representatives from weaker communities.
  • 11. FPTP Problem: under First-Past-The-Post, representatives often win with less than 50% of votes. Previously divided opposition voters could combine to recall an elected representative, creating instability.
  • The Right to Recall may consequently become an example of an Excess of Democracy.
CountryRecall system
USASeveral States allow recall of Governors and other elected officials. Successful gubernatorial recalls include North Dakota (1921) and California (2003).

United Kingdom: Recall of MPs Act, 2015

  • Recall is triggered by specified events, including certain criminal convictions, serious parliamentary suspensions or misconduct involving allowances.
  • At least 10% of eligible electors must sign the petition within six weeks.
  • A successful petition leads to a by-election. The recalled MP is permitted to re-contest.
  • Important: the UK does not allow recall merely because voters are dissatisfied with an MP's performance.
Value addition

Law Commission (255th Report, 2015): did not recommend the Right to Recall for Parliamentary and Assembly elections in India.

6 · Simultaneous Polls · Kovind Committee · 129TH Amendment BILL · Compulsory Voting · Sveep#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Elections module pp. 20-23. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • One Nation, One Election means holding Lok Sabha and State Assembly polls together, and later local polls within 100 days. The Kovind Committee (report 14 March 2024) proposed two-phase synchronisation, a common electoral roll and elections for the unexpired term after early dissolution. The 129th Amendment Bill (December 2024) is with a Joint Parliamentary Committee. Compulsory voting has been rejected in India; the ECI prefers voluntary participation and SVEEP.

Simultaneous Polls

What it means

  • Simultaneous Elections, also called One Nation, One Election (ONOE), refer to conducting elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies together.
  • The broader proposal also seeks to align Panchayat and Municipal elections with this electoral cycle.

Arguments in favour

  • 1. Frequent Application of MCC: as elections are conducted repeatedly, the MCC remains applicable in different parts of the country. This may delay new policy announcements and development decisions in election-bound areas.
  • 2. Administrative Efficiency: government officials and security forces are regularly engaged in election duties, distracting them from their primary responsibilities.
  • 3. Economies of Scale: may reduce repeated expenditure on polling arrangements, transport, security and election administration. However, savings must be assessed against the initial cost of additional EVMs, VVPATs and infrastructure.
  • 4. Time for Social Work: political leaders and party workers may devote more time to public service and constituency development, rather than continuous campaigning.
  • 5. Policy Continuity: fewer interruptions may improve consistency and continuity of government policies.
  • 6. Reduced Electoral Populism: a common cycle may reduce repeated short-term promises and politically motivated expenditure before State elections.
  • 7. Higher Participation: combined elections may encourage voter participation by reducing repeated voting requirements.

Arguments against

  • 1. Presidential System through the Back Door: fixed electoral cycles may weaken the flexibility of the Parliamentary System, where a Government can fall before completing its tenure.
  • 2. National Narrative May Subsume State Narrative: national issues and leaders may dominate campaigns, reducing attention to State-specific concerns.
  • 3. Continuous Elections Should Be Celebrated:
    • Frequent elections are symptomatic of the rise of a healthy multiparty democracy and federalism
    • They provide recurring opportunities to express preferences
  • 4. Economies of Scale May Not Materialise: substantial investment in additional voting machines, personnel and storage facilities.
  • 5. Hung Assembly Problem: parties may be compelled to form unstable coalitions to avoid disrupting the common cycle. If government formation becomes impossible, fresh elections may be necessary.
  • 6. Reduced Accountability:
    • Staggered elections allow voters to hold governments accountable at different intervals
    • E.g. voters can express dissatisfaction with the party governing a State during Lok Sabha elections, and vice versa
  • 7. Federal Diversity:
    • Different States face different social, economic and political issues
    • A uniform cycle may reduce their distinct electoral significance
  • 8. Constitutional and Legal Changes: amendments to the Constitution, the RPA and related electoral laws are required.

Kovind Committee ON Simultaneous Elections (2024)

The Committee

  • The Union Government constituted a High-Level Committee, chaired by former President Ram Nath Kovind, in September 2023. The Committee submitted its report on 14 March 2024.

Major recommendations

  • 1. Two-Phase Synchronisation: First phase: conduct Lok Sabha and State Assembly elections simultaneously. Second phase: conduct Panchayat and Municipal elections within 100 days of the general elections.
  • 2. Common Electoral Roll: a single electoral roll and Electoral Photo Identity Card (EPIC) for elections to all three tiers, through the prescribed institutional framework.
  • 3. Premature Dissolution: if the Lok Sabha or an Assembly is dissolved early, fresh elections should ordinarily be held only for the remaining period of the common electoral cycle.
  • 4. Constitutional Amendments: modify the framework governing legislative terms and elections.
  • 5. Administrative Preparedness: adequate EVMs, VVPATs, security personnel and election infrastructure.
  • The Union Cabinet accepted the recommendations on 18 September 2024.

International comparison: United Kingdom

  • The Fixed-term Parliaments Act, 2011 sought to establish fixed Parliamentary terms of five years. Under the earlier law, early elections were permitted through specified Parliamentary procedures, including a two-thirds majority vote.
  • However, the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act, 2022 repealed the 2011 Act and restored the earlier framework for Parliamentary dissolution.
  • Lesson for India: a fixed electoral cycle must be assessed in the context of Parliamentary accountability, government stability and the power of dissolution.

Compulsory Voting

Position in India

  • The idea was first considered by Parliament during the enactment of the Representation of the People Acts, but was rejected because of practical difficulties.
  • Also opposed by:
    • Tarkunde Committee (1974)
    • Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)
    • National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (2002)
    • Law Commission 255th Report (2015)
  • Legal position: Section 79(d) of RPA, 1951 defines an electoral right, including the right to vote or refrain from voting. PUCL v. Union of India (2013) recognised the importance of voter choice and secrecy, including the freedom not to support any candidate.

Arguments in favour

  • 1. Increase in Voter Turnout: the extent depends on the severity of penalties and strictness of enforcement.
  • 2. Political Awareness: greater participation may encourage people to learn about parties, candidates and public issues.
  • 3. Voice to Weaker Sections:
    • Universal participation may increase the political influence of marginalised communities. However, this assumes lower turnout among disadvantaged voters
    • In India, poorer sections often participate more actively than urban middle and upper-middle classes
  • 4. Greater Legitimacy: higher participation may strengthen the perceived legitimacy of the elected Government.
  • 5. Wider Representation: outcomes may reflect the preferences of a broader section of the population.

Arguments against

  • 1. Logistical Nightmare: enforcement would be administratively difficult due to the large electorate and geographical diversity.
  • 2. Burden on Weaker Sections: monetary penalties may disproportionately affect poor and marginalised voters.
  • 3. Donkey Voting: as observed in discussions of Australia's electoral system, some voters may mark ballots merely to fulfil the legal requirement without considering candidates.
  • 4. Reduced Quality of Participation: a rise in turnout does not necessarily improve political awareness or informed voting.
  • 5. Voter Registration Problems: may discourage some eligible citizens from registering to avoid legal obligations.
  • 6. High Opportunity Cost: resources could be used for voter education and improving polling accessibility instead.
  • 7. Democracy and Coercion: compelling citizens to vote raises questions about the voluntary character of democratic participation.
  • 8. Incentivising Voting: tax breaks, cash prizes or other financial incentives may introduce monetary considerations into electoral choice.

Election Commission's approach and SVEEP

  • The ECI has preferred voluntary electoral participation over compulsory voting.
  • Systematic Voters' Education and Electoral Participation (SVEEP) seeks to: increase voter awareness and registration
  • Encourage ethical and informed voting
  • Address urban voter apathy
  • Improve participation of women, persons with disabilities, young voters and marginalised communities
  • Assessment: instead of forcing people to vote, India should address the reasons for low participation, including lack of awareness, migration, accessibility barriers and distrust of political institutions.
