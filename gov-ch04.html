1 · Civil Society & NGOs#

Handout only · CS pp. 3-9
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2025 (anti-State?) · 2023 · 2021 · 2016 · 2015 (NGOs and environment). Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • When the state and the market both fail, people organise on their own: civil society, the third sector. NGOs are its main face. Know the features, the four generations, the roles and the problems.
Key point

Governance has two aspects: process/institutions (state, market, NGOs, legal frameworks) and substantive content (how it addresses health, education etc.). In a welfare state like India the state is the principal problem-solving forum and its legitimacy lay in its capacity to address public issues, defining India's model until the 1991 reforms. Over time structural inefficiencies showed it could not tackle complex challenges single-handedly; post-1990s reforms shifted to market delivery; three decades on the market's exploitative tendencies and limits are evident: so civil society emerged as the third forum (inclusivity, transparency, participatory decisions).

Why the State falls short (1st sector)

  • overcapitalisation: too much money and reliance on the state yet inadequate benefit to people
  • good politics, bad economics: decisions not matched to actual needs
  • nanny state: over-dependence on the state
  • too much money to one forum: corruption, embezzlement, stiffening of funds, linkages
  • rule-driven bureaucracy:
    • Adherence to rules became the goal
    • Whether ultimate goals are met was a limited concern
ForumStrengthWeakness
State (1st sector)welfare, policy, legitimacy till 1991overcapitalised; nanny state; rule-driven; corruption
Market (2nd)alternative delivery of goods and servicesprofit-driven; user-pay; exclusionary; merit goods (health, education) neglected for those who cannot pay
Civil society (3rd)voluntary, collective, autonomousno universal definition
Value addition

Civil society = people organising themselves outside state and market (NGOs may get donations from state or market but are created by people and largely outside them): an intermediate space; the "fifth estate" (after legislature, executive, judiciary and media). Voluntary (organised on the principle of voluntarism) · collective interest · autonomous · largely self-supporting · bound by shared values · citizens acting collectively in the public sphere · does not seek formal power. Political parties and media are kept out. No universally accepted definition: a mafia satisfies all the characteristics yet is not civil society. It is not society in general but organised social life: people organising around common beliefs, values, interests (NGOs, interest, advocacy, philanthropic, social-activism groups).

Why civil society grew

  • developmental failure of state and market
  • societal learning (skill, will to organise)
  • rise of the governance paradigm
  • societal learning: people acquire skill, will and ability to organise outside state and market
  • rejection of the instrumentalist state:
    • State not at the centre of everything or of public-goods delivery
    • Move from state-centric to people-centric development
  • globalisation and information age
  • colour revolutions (democratic revolutions of the 1990s in many ex-Soviet republics to topple dictators and restore democracy), Arab Spring

Components AND Significance

Components

  • people-serving (general interest: Human Rights Watch, ADR) vs member-serving (pressure groups focus on their own members)
  • traditional associations:
    • Caste, religion, ethnicity, language
    • Representative: trade, student, peasant unions
  • interest groups:
    • SHGs, pressure groups
    • Philanthropic (Ramakrishna Mission), advocacy (PETA)
  • Socio-cultural (sports, recreational clubs)
  • Professional, typically member-serving (BCI, IMA)
  • Social movements (India Against Corruption)
  • NGOs:
    • Private, not-for-profit, voluntary, self-governing entities
    • CBOs: voluntary grassroots entities serving local community need

Significance

  • supplements, complements, questions, confronts, substitutes (where government is not active) the state/market
  • Advocates social change
  • Pro-active defence of civil liberties: HRW, Amnesty, PUCL
  • strengthens transparency and accountability:
    • ADR, Transparency International
    • A vigilant civil society ensures government does not deviate from its promise
  • makes de-administered, decentralised development workable
  • Evaluates impact of government policies (social audits)
  • Training ground for future political leaders
  • technical support to government: CPR, IDSA, FICCI

Own anchor for significance: Supplement · Advocacy · Freedoms defended · Evaluate (audit) · Support (technical) · Control of state power · Accountability · New leaders. (own mnemonic)

Anti-state OR Non-state? PYQ 2025

Way forward / key point

Non-State, not anti-State: they operate outside the government machinery, which does not make them anti-State. Criticism is not opposition: in a functioning democracy dissent and watchdog activity strengthen the State by improving accountability. Treating all civil society as suspect weakens democracy: blanket suspicion silences the legitimately voiceless alongside the genuinely problematic actor.

Note

The concern is not baseless, only overextended: a small number may misuse funds or work against public interest, so proportionate, transparent regulation, the kind the 2nd ARC envisaged for FCRA, is legitimate. The failure is using that concern to justify blanket restriction.

Key point

Way forward: treat non-state actors as governance partners, not adversaries (service delivery, advocacy, innovation) · reform FCRA toward proportionality, decentralising administration as 2nd ARC suggested, distinguishing genuine financial misconduct from politically inconvenient advocacy · deepen SHG 2.0, addressing rising NPAs and credit weaknesses so SHGs evolve from a subsistence tool into economic empowerment infrastructure · modernise cooperative governance (PACS computerisation, Model Cooperative Societies Act, Ministry of Cooperation) · formal consultative mechanisms for pressure groups, not street mobilisation and informal lobbying alone · calibrate regulation to risk: target genuine wrongdoing without a chilling effect on legitimate civic work.

Social Capital AND Legal BASE

Value addition

Social capital = power of the collective. Two pillars: trust and reciprocity. vs financial capital: both are productive; social capital is everywhere whereas financial capital is confined to financial institutions; social capital is unlimited and infinite, financial is finite.

NGO features

  • Voluntary
  • Initiated and governed by own members, ideally on democratic principles
  • Autonomous from state and market
  • legal entities: trusts, cooperatives, charities, trade unions, not-for-profit companies
  • Own written constitution
  • Definite aims and objectives
  • Proper organisational structure
  • Ability to mobilise resources locally or outside
  • Well-organised accounting/auditing
  • Continuous evaluation of performance
  • government funding:
    • Certainty of finance
    • Saves time and energy spent raising funds
    • Expands scope and outreach
    • Regular state-NGO interaction is mutually beneficial

Roles AND Generations

Role of NGOs in development

  • mediatory:
    • Link between citizens and government
    • Brings to government circles people's problems that escaped notice
  • mobilising:
    • Organise Gram Sabha meetings
    • Many SHGs sponsored and developed by NGOs
  • consultative:
    • Think-tanks give technical inputs
    • Leaders consulted, put on ad-hoc advisory bodies
  • educational:
    • Vocational training for poor to create self-employment
    • E.g. SEWA (early 1970s, Ela Bhatt: poor women)
  • supplementary/complementary:
    • Core developmental areas
    • Last-mile connectivity
    • Address people's trust deficit with the state through a sensitive approach
  • leadership: principal change agents in certain areas, eyes and ears of government (e.g. issues of sex workers)
  • innovation:
    • Imaginative interpretation of rules to serve public welfare
    • Experiment until best practice, then replicated across departments
  • efficiency:
    • Bureaucracy is rule-driven, NGOs are goal-driven
    • Adequate funding raises efficiency of government spending
  • also:
    • Highlight loopholes in legislative and institutional framework
    • Enforce standards of accountability and transparency
    • Voice of informed dissent through research and surveys

Problems OF NGOs PYQ 2021PYQ 2015

Eight Frictions

  • NGO PROBLEMS:
    • Few resources
    • Uneven presence
    • No coordination
    • Few trained staff
    • State interference
    • Corruption (Jholawalas)
    • Elite, donor control
    • Weak accountability

Problems in detail

  • deficiency of resources:
    • Pre-1991 closed economy and public-sector dominance limited private donations
    • NGOs with radical agendas became dependent on government funds, so bureaucratic interference
    • Post-liberalisation corporate donations rose but scarcity persists
  • uneven presence:
    • Limited networks, absent in large parts of rural India
    • Certain regions attract excess attention (Niyamgiri-Vedanta agitation), others neglected
  • lack of coordination:
    • NGOs in similar sectors (health, education, skills) compete for donor recognition
    • Reluctance to pool resources for fear of losing credit: duplication and wastage
  • Limited outreach/networks reinforce uneven presence
  • Shortage of trained and dedicated workers (technical and managerial expertise) hurts field effectiveness
  • state interference:
    • Government-funded NGOs face bureaucratic oversight
    • Internal operations become bureaucratised and rule-driven, undermining independence
  • corruption:
    • Some misuse funds, money laundering, misappropriation
    • Ex-CVC N. Vittal named "Jholawalas" (corrupt NGOs) alongside Netas, Babus, Lalas and Dadas
  • elite domination: reliance on corporate donors, rich elites, landlords gives donor-driven agendas, compromising grassroots autonomy
  • accountability:
    • Government-funded NGOs submit progress reports and audits
    • Those raising funds from public donations or informal channels lack structured accountability, so monitoring is difficult
  • no common governance norms:
    • Registered under Trusts, Societies, Charitable Institutions, Not-for-Profit Companies: fragmented oversight
    • RWAs and SHGs not formally registered
  • government crackdown:
    • In democracies with weak institutions governments see NGOs as threats for dissent and activism
    • Regulatory scrutiny and funding restrictions on those advocating public causes

2 · FCRA · Regulating NGOs · Donor Agencies#

Handout only · CS pp. 9-12
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2015 (FCRA) · 2024 (charitable trusts) · 2022 (donor agencies). Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The state has tightened control of foreign funding step by step: FCRA 1976 → FCRA 2010 → Amendment Act 2020 → an Amendment Bill 2026 that is still only a Bill. Know which are Acts and which is a Bill.
  1. 1976
    FCRA 1976 enacted by Indira Gandhi amid declared suspicion that foreign interests were trying to influence or destabilise India
  2. 1982
    Kudal Commission appointed to investigate activities and foreign funding of certain voluntary organisations; report not placed in public domain
  3. 2010
    FCRA, 2010 main framework (MHA)
  4. 2013
    Lokpal Act 2013: NGOs receiving substantial foreign contributions fall within Lokpal's jurisdiction, strengthening accountability over foreign funds
  5. 2014
    media reports cited a leaked Intelligence Bureau report alleging some foreign-funded NGOs mobilised opposition to projects on nuclear energy, mining, GM crops and industrial development: Kudankulam, uranium mining, POSCO, Vedanta
  6. 2020
    FCRA (Amendment) Act, 2020
  7. 2022
    Noel Harper v UoI upholds 2020 provisions
  8. 2026
    FCRA Amendment Bill, 2026: still a Bill, with a JPC
Note

Act vs Bill: FCRA 1976, 2010 and the 2020 Amendment are Acts. The 2026 measure is a Bill referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee, so it is not law yet (handout wording).

FCRA, 2010

FCRA 2010 (MHA)

  • Regulates receipt and utilisation of foreign contribution by individuals, associations, NGOs, trusts and other entities
  • Administered by MHA
  • two routes: Registration (fixed period, subject to renewal) and Prior Permission (specific purpose and specific foreign source, generally for organisations without regular FCRA registration)
  • allowed fields: cultural, economic, educational, religious, social
  • barred recipients: political parties, election candidates, public servants, organisations of a political nature
  • MHA can suspend or cancel
  • Annual returns and accounts
Value addition

Concerns: complex registration and renewal · vague "political nature" · suspension and cancellation · compliance load · admin spend cut by 2020. FCRA Rules: office-bearers declare on religious conversion, sedition, violence; foreign hospitality covered.

2020 changes: Public servants barred · Transfer barred · Admin cap 20% · Suspension 360 days · Aadhaar · Designated SBI account. (own mnemonic)

Way forward / key point

Noel Harper v Union of India (2022): SC upheld the major 2020 provisions; receiving foreign contribution is not an absolute right.

FCRA AMENDMENT BILL, 2026 (referred to JPC)

What the Bill proposes

  • certificate ceases if not renewed, no application made, or renewal rejected
  • Designated Authority for vesting, supervision, management and disposal of foreign contributions and assets created from them after registration ends
  • provisional vesting:
    • Assets temporarily in custody, restored if registration revived
    • Permanent vesting: if not restored, assets may vest permanently in the Authority, be used for public purposes, disposal proceeds credited to Consolidated Fund of India
  • Prior-permission funds must be received and utilised within the prescribed period
  • Designated Authority must preserve religious character of places of worship, cannot convert or repurpose them
  • Maximum imprisonment for specified offences reduced to one year from five
  • Prior approval of the Central Government before initiating investigation for offences under the Act
Way forward / key point

Significance: closes a legal gap (framework for assets when certificate expires or is not renewed) · prevents regulatory avoidance (cannot escape scrutiny by letting registration lapse) · protects foreign-funded assets for public purpose · transparency and anti-money-laundering compliance · decriminalisation (reduced imprisonment shows a trust-based regulatory approach).

Value addition

Concerns: disproportionate consequence: even procedural lapses may cost assets · retrospective impact: earlier assets may stay subject to vesting even if the organisation now functions on domestic funds · differential treatment of registered vs prior-permission organisations · due process: no clear hearing or appeal against denial of renewal, more executive discretion · centralisation: prior Central approval for investigation raises institutional-independence concerns.

Key point

Balance and way forward: regulate to stop laundering, foreign political influence, diversion and security threats, but keep space for health, education, environment, relief. Give a statutory appeal · judicial safeguards before permanent vesting · clean exit from FCRA without forfeiture of legitimate assets · real-time digital disclosure on the FCRA portal · proportionate treatment of technical lapses. Conclusion: stronger executive power needs due process, proportionality and transparency.

CAN Civil Society Replace THE State? PYQ 2016

State functions not to outsource

  • Defence
  • Public order
  • External affairs
  • larger societal problems neither civil society nor market can handle: population-control policy, comprehensive national policies, broad problems like communalism and casteism
  • symbolic functions (regularly celebrating Republic Day, Independence Day)
  • framing monetary, fiscal, EXIM, industrial policies
  • Verdict: civil society can be one alternative problem-solving forum, but not a complete substitute for the state or the market

Donor Agencies PYQ 2022

Key point

Donor agencies are now significant players in financing India's development projects; resources and expertise are valuable, but excessive reliance may undermine community ownership and grassroots participation in setting priorities.

Against dependence

  • reduced community ownership: projects look externally driven, alienate locals (Narmada Dam protests)
  • top-down imposition: global narratives over local realities (World Bank ICDS support faced local adaptability challenges)
  • bureaucratic complications: donor procedural norms, audits, reporting burden local agencies (MNREGA reporting delays from donor-linked processes)
  • weakened accountability: external steering bypasses national oversight, less transparency to locals and democratic institutions

For involvement

  • capacity building: training and technical inputs (UNDP water management, rural India)
  • participatory planning: community engagement in design and monitoring (ADB Urban Climate Fund)
  • local knowledge: partnerships amplify indigenous experience (Sweden-SEWA boosted women's livelihoods)
  • global best practice: benchmarks in transparency, gender inclusivity, environmental safeguards

3 · Self-help Groups (SHGs)#

Handout only · CS pp. 12-17
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2021 · 2020 · 2017 · 2015 · 2014 · 2013. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • An SHG is a small informal savings group, mostly rural women, that lends from its own corpus and later links to a bank. Social capital replaces collateral. NABARD started the bank-linkage programme in 1992.
Value addition

SHG: principally a rural women's movement (not only women-specific; some include men); small, informal associations, not legally registered, not legal entities; largely poor women, preferably of similar socio-economic background; ideal size 10-20. Each must save regularly (weekly); a weekly loan corpus is created and lent to members for income-generating ventures, vulnerabilities, productive needs. A savings-led microfinance model to improve earnings and savings and build an economic base to escape poverty, on self-help and collective responsibility: loans carry no security (poor rural women lack the collateral financial institutions demand); repaid because of collective responsibility and peer pressure.

Key point

Bank link: when an SHG matures it is linked to a bank: it deposits its collective corpus, earns interest, and can avail a larger loan. Banks normally follow a 1:4 deposit-to-credit ratio (SHG depositing Rs 10,000 a month as corpus is eligible for four times that as loan).

TypeHow it works
ROSCA (Rotating Savings and Credit Association)like the urban kitty/committee: each month or week savings pooled; corpus given to one member (name picked by draw of lots); rotates till the last member is selected, then the cycle repeats
ASCA (Accumulated Savings and Credit Association)no rotation; accumulated corpus from regular periodic savings; loans to those who need, on collective decision and joint approval

Shg-bank Linkage (shg-blp)

Shg-blp

  • NABARD, 1992 started the SHG Bank Linkage Programme
  • Principal self-help promoting agency in the country
  • Mature SHGs get linked to banks
  • Microfinance SHGs: set up by financial institutions
  • Microfinance Plus SHGs: set up by government agencies and NGOs, with wider development aims
  • SHPIs: NABARD, RRBs, SCBs, NGOs, government agencies
  • 100+ million households covered
  • 84%+ SHGs are women's: movement largely driven by rural poor women
Key point

Panchsutras (Panchsheel): if SHGs follow these they are of very good quality and banks are willing to lend: regular savings · regular internal lending · regular repayment of loans · regular meetings · regular books of accounts.

Memory aid · S-I-R-M-B

Teacher's Panchsutra (five regulars): Savings · Internal lending · Repayment · Meetings · Books of account. (initials are my aid; the five points are the handout's) (own mnemonic)

NABARD tools

  • Joint Liability Group:
    • The whole group collectively takes responsibility for repaying the loan even if only a small part was lent to one member
    • Introduced by NABARD 2005-06
    • Best done by organising women of similar skills into affinity SHGs and developing enterprises for all (each like a shareholder; bank loan goes to the whole enterprise)
  • NABFINS (NABARD Financial Services Ltd): to evolve into an ideal MFI, setting governance standards among MFIs, a model for how to approach SHGs especially loaning
  • microfinance development programmes:
    • Skill development if members lack skills
    • Improve skills or optimise production if they have them
  • Livelihood and Enterprise Development:
    • Livelihood promotion in clusters of 15-30 SHGs in contiguous villages (one SHG's product can be input for another)
    • Handholding 3-6 months (representatives explain the value chain)
    • Affinity groups
    • End-to-end support
  • DAY-NRLM:
    • Mission aims to bring at least one woman from each identified poor rural household into SHGs
    • Special emphasis on the most vulnerable: manual scavengers, disabled, PVTGs, bonded labour, victims of sexual offences

Benefits AND Impact PYQ 2021PYQ 2020

Benefits

  • financial inclusion: perhaps the best model for extending microfinance: savings, credit, insurance
  • physical capital replaced by social capital:
    • Poor lack physical collateral
    • SHGs need none
  • capability approach (not dole, due or rights): strengthens capacity and autonomy to make the choices people want in life
  • social and economic transformation of women:
    • Income meets life-cycle and production needs of family
    • Social status rises
    • Gives economic reforms a human face
    • Declared component of GoI poverty-alleviation strategy
    • Positive effect on demographic and social indicators
  • political empowerment:
    • Community platform
    • Rural women attend Gram Sabha regularly, take part in panchayat elections
Note

Dole vs Due: dole = something government gives out of generosity or political will; addresses a shortage of goods at a point in time, never on a sustaining basis. Due = government converts benefits (employment, food security: RTS, FSA) into legally enforceable rights, the Rights-Based Approach. SHG is neither: it strengthens people's capacity and so their autonomy of choice, the capability approach.

ImpactFact

Creditcollateral-free loans of Rs 87,098 crore to 5 million SHGs (by March 2019)

Issues PYQ 2014PYQ 2013

Issues

  • outreach:
    • More successful in South India (undivided Andhra led SHG formation; Kerala got lots of government support; maybe linked to higher education)
    • Poorest of the poor left out
  • restrictive policies of FIs and banks:
    • Banks compel SHGs to work as suits the banks
    • SHGs wound up, members return to moneylenders (NABARD now gives 100% refinancing against SHG loans)
  • quality of management and governance:
    • Weekly meetings not held, poor attendance
    • Book-keeping weak
    • Membership too large, unmanageable
    • Only leaders benefit more from loans
    • Leadership should change periodically but does not
    • Securing autonomy from government interference
  • Inability to take up new productive ventures
  • Unhealthy competition among states
  • Inadequate micro and macro studies
  • Capacity and skill development
  • rising NPAs (new concern):
    • Outstanding SHG loans Rs 423 crore (2007-08), Rs 452 crore (2018-19)
    • Expected to rise due to COVID
  • inequality: reduces inequality based on social gaps but may create inequality by education: better-educated women benefit more
Way forward / key point

Measures: government as facilitator, not controller (Grameen Bank) · targeted expansion in credit-deficient regions (MP, Rajasthan, North-East, to bridge regional disparity; Kenya's rural SHGs, Chamas, cut financial exclusion among women) · financial infrastructure and digital connectivity: integrate with banking networks, ICT, capacity building (AP SERP model: dedicated monitoring cells and ICT tracking performance, credit linkages, transaction histories) · urban and peri-urban SHGs for slum dwellers and urban micro-entrepreneurship (Brazil's Bolsa Familia included urban SHGs). Government role example: Bangladesh's Grameen Bank showed proactive government support fosters a microfinance ecosystem.

4 · Pressure Groups & Lobbying#

Handout only · CS pp. 17-26
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2026 · 2025 · 2021 · 2019 · 2017 · 2013. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • A pressure group influences government policy without trying to become the government. India has many types: social, professional, institutional, ad hoc and anomie. Learn techniques, problems and a few recent examples.
Value addition

Pressure group: hard to define (parties are sometimes included, sometimes excluded); the common definition excludes political parties and covers groups wishing to influence government policies and activities while remaining apart from government. An association of people with common interests who try to achieve them by influencing government; helps social integration, political articulation and acts as a catalyst for change. Definition: "an organised aggregate which seeks to influence the context of governmental decisions without attempting to place its members in formal governmental capacities". Alan R. Ball: firmly part of the political process; reinforce or change the direction of policy but do not wish to become the government. Henry Turner: non-partisan; do not draft party programmes or nominate candidates. Finer: "anonymous empire". R.D. Lambert: "unofficial government". Truman (group theory): a group, on the basis of shared attitudes (the interest), makes claims on other groups for the establishment, maintenance or enhancement of behaviour implied in those attitudes.

Genesis

  • In existence ever since governmental machinery could deliver benefits to individuals or groups
  • Took concrete form after the industrial revolution and rise of market economies
  • trusts and monopolies, struggle over tariffs
  • technology and agricultural skills: new problems, desires, needs, new groups
  • state took on welfare functions besides regulation: more power and discretion in state apparatus, so need to pressure it grew
  • Dominant sections need State help for economic activity
  • Weak and deprived need it for basic requirements
  • freedom of association in democracies: the basic factor
  • welfare state, socialism, positivism: pressure groups as a shield against misuse of government power
  • consequence of inadequacies of political parties in articulating demands of deprived and dominant interests

Features AND Interest Groups

Features

  • Organised on social, political, economic, religious, vocational interests
  • Try to dominate political structure and suppress clashing groups
  • Represent interests of different parts of society
  • Vary in size and structure
  • Vitality = ability to influence policy makers, law makers, implementers
  • Influence varies (trade unions, business, professionals strong; a small ad hoc local committee can force quick policy change by mass marches)
  • modern means without renouncing old: financing parties, sponsoring close-associate candidates, getting friendly persons into executive jobs
  • Change allegiance quickly
  • Appear and disappear with conditions
  • individuals have lobbying rights but a collective approach exerts stronger pressure and consolidates similar ideas

Pressure vs interest group

  • interest group: formed by people with shared interests, no interest in pressurising government for favourable decisions
  • pressure group: organised interest group with regular interface with government, questions or connects with it to win favourable policy
  • A group may start as an interest group and morph into a pressure group as it grows with wider objectives
  • Terms often used interchangeably
  • Inevitable in a democracy
  • Their study is intrinsic to understanding dynamics of politics and of policy making
Note

Pressure groups vs political parties: pressure groups do not participate in elections, parties do; pressure groups have a smaller social base, parties a larger one.

Western vs Indian

Differences

  • Western groups regarded as the "fourth organ" of government
  • Indian groups not yet able to play a significant role in politics
  • India and Britain:
    • Main lobbying targets are the cabinet and civil service, not Parliament
    • USA: Congress and its committees, rather than the President
  • Indian groups based on caste, religion, region are more powerful than business organisations
  • American groups take interest in foreign policy
  • Indian groups do not

Significance

Significance

  • channel of communication between citizens and government
  • broaden political participation: people join because of deep distrust between people and political parties
  • political socialisation: process by which people become politically active and acquire political vision, approach, values
  • data, inputs, information for policy (FICCI, ASSOCHAM, CII)
  • prevent a dominant force: countervailing groups demand opposite things
  • informal face of politics: mobilise and shape public opinion, question and confront government, agitation and propaganda: keep democracy alive between elections
  • support parties and candidates, give donations
  • "legislatures behind legislatures": legislators get cues on what to discuss from groups that raise issues
  • reservoirs of political leadership (Kejriwal from the Lokpal movement)
  • Check arbitrary use of state power (farmers vs three farm laws)
  • Improve governance (human-rights groups)
  • educate people by campaigns, propaganda, reports, media debates (environmentalists against growth harmful to environment)
  • safety valve for grievances, preventing spontaneous outbursts
  • consensus building to present a united face to government (FICCI; Samyukt Kisan Morcha)

Eight WAYS TO Pressure

Techniques in detail (with handout examples)

  • lobbying and direct negotiation with policymakers, bureaucrats, legislators (CII, FICCI on tax reforms and industrial policy)
  • PILs for judicial intervention (CSE for air-pollution control)
  • media campaigns and opinion building (RTI activists exposing administrative opacity)
  • mass mobilisation: rallies, dharnas, petitions, signature drives (farmers' protests 2020-21)
  • social audits and citizen report cards (PAC report card reduced bribery in service delivery in Bangalore)
  • expert inputs and research-based advocacy: white papers, data to committees and ministries (PRS Legislative Research)
  • coalition building (NCPRI united civil society for the RTI Act)
  • strategic legal interventions: court cases, stay orders, amicus curiae
  • symbolic protests: satyagraha, padayatras, hunger strikes (Anna Hazare's Lokpal movement)
  • digital campaigns: hashtags, viral videos, petitions (#SaveAarey mobilised urban youth)
  • RTI-based exposes (MPLADS fund misuse)
  • international advocacy and moral pressure: global bodies and indices for reputational pressure
Key point

Best practices: PAC report card (Bangalore: citizen audits cut corruption in municipal services) · NCPRI RTI campaign (coalition success, led to the RTI Act) · #SaveAarey (Mumbai, 2019: digital activism led to policy reversal on urban deforestation) · CEHAT (research-based lobbying shaped national policy on patient rights) · Anna Hazare's Jan Lokpal movement (2011: Gandhian methods put anti-corruption on the national agenda).

Value addition

Lobbying: so called as big business leaders roamed the lobbies of the US Congress to influence policy. Today: formally connecting with government, regular meetings with officials and ministers, presenting the interests of the section one represents to get a favourable policy outcome; used principally by organised corporate groups, business houses, business associations. Not bad per se; in India neither legal nor illegal. Problems: (a) paid lobbyists: Nira Radia and Vaishnavi Corporate Communications (getting specific leaders placed in specific ministries); Deepak Talwar Associates (accused of bribing civil servants); (b) legislators as lobbyists: H.G. Mudgal disqualified from the provisional Parliament after Independence for lobbying for a private organisation (Bombay Bullion Organisation); (c) bureaucrats, serving or retired: advising the Minister to tweak rules for certain organisations, pushing files to accommodate a private player. Santhanam Committee 1962: proper system of accreditation of lobbyists; no civil servant should interact with a representative of a business organisation or group unless fully accredited.

Way forward / key point

Business associations (FICCI, ASSOCHAM, CII) methods: lobbying · political donations via electoral bonds · critical assessment of government economic policies/legislation · commissioning studies · workshops and conferences · technical inputs · appointment to advisory/ad hoc panels · consensus building · interaction with analogous institutions abroad, like WTO.

Problems AND Effectiveness

Problems

  • disproportionate influence of some: caste and community groups, trade and peasant unions, business associations like FICCI, CII after 1991
  • subversion of democratic institutions: legislators and parties heavily influenced lose independent thinking
  • unorganised interests neglected (e.g. educational development)
  • extra-legal methods: bandhs, hartals, rail roko, chakka jam, destruction of public property
  • divisive: prevent emergence of a true national identity
  • block progressive initiatives: some Khap Panchayats sanction honour killings in inter-caste marriages
  • many lack transparency in internal functioning (e.g. RSS)
Note

Khap Panchayats: SC 2011: a Khap advocating or sanctioning honour killing should be banned altogether under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act; an officer who does not check its illegal decisions would also be penalised. As victims are generally SC/ST, the accused can also be prosecuted under the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989. Some states like Rajasthan proposed laws against honour killing, but regular IPC provisions (e.g. Sec 302) suffice if rationally enforced. Khaps thrive on weak local bodies, judicial inadequacies, political patronage. But some Khaps have done consensus building and socially oriented community service.

Key point

Effectiveness depends on: sectional (RSS, Khap Panchayats, caste associations: more effective in India) vs promotional (human rights organisations, PETA) · funding (lobbying needs a lot of money) · political branding (linked to certain parties, so effectiveness depends on that party's role in government) · membership (quantity and quality) · government reliance on them (Make in India needs business associations).

Way forward / key point

Way forward: legal recognition and regulatory framework: absence of formal status limits legitimacy and accountability; registration and regulation akin to US and UK lobbying laws · code of conduct and financial transparency: mandatory ethics, disclose funding sources and lobbying (vital in telecom, pharma where opaque lobbying undermines trust) · consultative role in legislative committees and policy dialogues (e.g. farmers' associations in agricultural reforms and MSP) · digital platforms like MyGov to gather feedback systematically.

5 · Comptroller & Auditor General (CAG)#

Handout only · CAG pp. 2-7
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2018 (appointment, terms, powers) · 2016 (Art 149; policy audit). Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The CAG audits the spending of Union, States and bodies funded by the government, and reports to Parliament. India's CAG audits after the money is spent; the UK CAG also controls release of funds.
Value addition

Ambedkar in the Constituent Assembly: "the most important office under the Constitution"; one of the 4 pillars with SC, UPSC and Election Commission. Art 148 creates the office. Principal responsibility: audit accounts of State and Central governments spending taxpayers' money; so significant for financial accountability that it must have independence like the judiciary. Status: service conditions broadly equivalent to a Supreme Court judge, including salary and removal safeguards.

PointFact
Tenure6 years or until age 65, whichever is earlier, under the CAG (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971; changes require amending the Act, not the Constitution
Post-retirement restrictionArt 148(4): CAG ineligible for further government office under the Union or any State
Dutiesprescribed under Art 149; laid down in the CAG (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971, enacted by Parliament
Accounts formadvises President (Art 150)
Net proceedscertifies shared Union-State proceeds (Art 279)

Functions

Audit and report on

  • all expenditure from Consolidated Fund, Public Account, Contingency Fund of India / States / UTs with legislative assembly
  • trading, manufacturing accounts and profit and loss statements kept by any department of Centre, States, UTs with legislature (e.g. Railways, Posts)
  • receipts of Union and States: rules designed to secure an effective check on assessment and collection of revenue
  • stocks and stores and government inventory: ensure no pilferage
  • Receipts and expenditure of bodies substantially financed by government
  • And of bodies not substantially financed when requested by the President or Governor
  • Public Sector Undertakings
  • Advises the President on the form of accounts of Union and States (Art 150)
  • Certifies net proceeds shared by Union with States (Art 279)
  • assists the Finance Commission in a fair understanding of states' actual financial position
  • audits accounts of Autonomous District Councils under Schedule VI
Memory aid · CARSE

Audit-on: Consolidated and other funds · Accounts (trading, P&L) · Receipts · Stores · Entities financed (bodies, PSUs). (own mnemonic)

PSU Audit AND Accounts

PSU typeCAG audit
Statutory corporations (special law, governed by own statute, generally government-owned; LIC, FCI, RBI)CAG may conduct full, partial or no audit, depending on the law
Government companies (registered under Companies Act / joint stock; at least 51% government ownership)audited by private auditors appointed in consultation with CAG; CAG can also do a supplementary audit over and above it
Key point

PSUs = government-owned enterprises for commercial and economic activity outside the regular departmental structure; some set up as State monopolies in strategic sectors. Performance audit: both categories may face comprehensive performance audit through the Audit Board (introduced 1968), where applicable.

Key point

Separation of accounting and auditing: accounts are prepared by the department or undertaking; audit is by an external authority for independence and no conflict of interest. Departmentalisation of Accounts (1976): before 1976 CAG did both compilation of accounts and audit; the scheme transferred preparation and compilation of Union accounts to ministries and departments, leading to the Indian Civil Accounts Service (ICAS) and the Controller General of Accounts (CGA) under the Finance Ministry. Now: CAG primarily audits Union accounts, continuing both accounting and auditing for most States, subject to arrangements. Significance: clearer separation, stronger financial accountability and independent oversight.

Issues IN Working PYQ 2018

Mode of appointment

  • No role of a broad-based committee as in NHRC, CVC, Information Commissioners
  • Entirely at the discretion of the central government
  • UK: CAG heading the National Audit Office appointed by House of Commons resolution after agreement between PM and chair of PAC (generally an opposition member)
  • USA: Comptroller General heading GAO appointed by the President, ratified by the Senate
  • Australia: ratification and confirmation by the whole Public Accounts Committee
  • India is a member of ASOSAI and INTOSAI and should abide by their mission statement and charter so that complete independence is guaranteed
  • Experts recommend a broad-based committee of PM, LoP in Lok Sabha and CJI for appointments like CAG, UPSC, Election Commission

Who should be appointed

  • experts: career auditors and accountants of the Indian Audit and Accounts Service (IAAS)
  • Till date among 14 CAGs only the first three came from IAAS
  • Of the remaining 11, 10 were retired IAS officers
  • adverse effect on morale and motivation of IAAS officers
  • conflict of interest: an IAS officer who served a department is asked to audit the same department as CAG
  • post-retirement incentive for IAS officers may lead to politicisation of civil services
  • so: encadre the post, earmarked only for IAAS members
  • Narhari Rao first CAG
  • K. Sanjay Murthy present (as per handout, verify)
Note

Single vs multi-member body: experts debate making CAG multi-member like UPSC and Election Commission. But the real problem is understaffed IAAS, not single-member form: of about 250,000 auditable entities CAG effectively audits only about 60,000 a year, so more IAAS officers, ground staff and junior officers are needed. In the USA and UK too the CAG is a single-member body, with a heavier caseload than India's; no study shows a multi-member institution performs better.

Types OF Audit

AuditQuestion it asks
Compliance (legality / statutory / transactions audit)was money spent according to the financial law of the country? legal availability of money spent; was the mandated activity authorised by Parliament?
Financialare proper books of accounts maintained; are recorded transactions a faithful representation of what actually took place?
Performance (includes efficiency audit)e.g. number of schools set up under an amount, and their standards and facilities: efficiency of money spent. CAG does only performance audit/reporting, not performance evaluation; reasons for inefficiency are outside CAG's domain
Propriety (from "proper")questions the wisdom and faithfulness of government spending; e.g. 2G spectrum on first-come-first-served, audited by then CAG Vinod Rai: Rs 1.76 lakh crore revenue loss. Need: it is taxpayers' money, not officials' own, so officials cannot be expected to show the caution they would with their own; major frauds and scams come to light only through propriety audit
Memory aid · CFPP

Four audits: Compliance · Financial · Performance · Propriety. The 1971 Act does not clearly state the types of audit; Sec 23 authorises CAG to define the scope of audit. (own mnemonic)

Value addition

Against propriety audit: benefit of hindsight: a decision may look unwise without the context in which it was made · auditors know little of administration: seasoned auditors lack experience of decisions under administrative circumstances · red tapism: fear of CAG, CVC, CBI makes officials keep files undecided · slows development. Paul Appleby (1950s, American public-administration expert): CAG oversight created fear among civil servants and delayed decisions; he even recommended abolishing the office.

Way forward / key point

For: CAG's role is more than tallying accounts, "which any B.Com. graduate can do" · supreme audit institutions worldwide do propriety audit · CAG should not be nit-picking, but if a prima facie case of fraud, impropriety, irregularity comes up, exposing it is not just his right but his duty · purpose is not fault-finding but better financial governance · instead of questioning it, streamline internal audits and respond proactively to audit queries · best potential to expose irregularities, embezzlement, bribery, fraud.

Key point

2G case: 2008 licences allocated on First-Come-First-Served instead of auctions; CAG estimated a presumptive loss of up to Rs 1.76 lakh crore; government defended low spectrum prices as promoting affordable telecom; SC 2012 quashed 122 licences and directed fresh allocation by auction; the auction raised about Rs 9,400 crore, below expectations, partly due to changing market conditions and limited bidders. CAG's jurisdiction: normally examines policy implementation, but may question policy decisions where they involve financial irregularities, wasteful expenditure or loss to the exchequer (PYQ 2016)

Comptroller vs Auditor; PPPs; Local Bodies

Key point

Comptroller function: before public money is released, verify that the expenditure has been authorised by Parliament and approve release of funds. Key difference: UK CAG exercises pre-expenditure control; Indian CAG audits after the money has been spent.

Should CAG audit PPPs?

  • PPP:
    • Government-private collaboration for infrastructure (highways, airports, ports)
    • Government gives approvals, land, support as agreed
    • Private gives investment and expertise, develops and operates
  • BOT: private builds and operates for a concession period, recovers investment through tolls or user fees, then transfers to government
  • VGF:
    • One-time or deferred government grant for economically justified but financially unviable PPP projects
    • 2020 VGF Scheme extended to social infrastructure (health, education, water supply, waste management)
  • question:
    • PPPs may be largely privately financed, so what is CAG's jurisdiction? Public interest: public assets, natural resources, concessions, revenue sharing
    • Irregularities in concession period, toll collection, revenue sharing harm public interest
    • So PPPs may fall in CAG purview, depending on applicable law and contract, even with private financing
  • Telecom revenue sharing:
    • Operators pay licence fees as a percentage of Adjusted Gross Revenue (AGR)
    • Underreporting or inadmissible deductions cut the government's share
    • AUSPI v Union of India (2014): SC upheld CAG's authority to examine private telecom operators' accounts to verify revenue-sharing payments
Note

Local bodies (ULBs, PRIs): in some states CAG provides broad supervision and guidelines for local-body accounts and audit; it does not conduct audits as such; the broad overview is accepted by state governments principally to get the conditional grants recommended by the Finance Commission.

WHY CAG IS WEAK: THE Famous Quote

"CAG is a ..."Meaning
prosecutor with a law that hobbles his functioninglaw does not clearly indicate the audits he is to perform; does not give adequate powers to obtain documents from government; audit queries are not answered properly, yet law gives no power to act against departmental officials who refuse; so the law itself makes him shaky
judge without the power to sentencecan clearly indicate a case of financial fraud but cannot punish; can only make allegations in the report to Parliament; action stays at the government's discretion; can establish guilt but not pass punishment
litigant without the right to appealafter his report goes to Parliament and then the PAC, he has no power to question or appeal on action taken; if Parliament and government pay no heed to his report he has no power to challenge it
Way forward / key point

Effectiveness of an audit depends on (1) system for evaluating CAG reports: by Parliament and PAC: adequate discussion time, members present. In the USA and UK the CAG is part of the legislative branch; in India independent of both legislature and executive; a long-pending reform is to make CAG part of the legislature so Parliament debates reports more seriously, holding government accountable and improving financial governance.

Way forward / key point

(2) Quality of audit reports: under INTOSAI guidelines senior auditors from other countries review India's audit process and make recommendations (and Indian auditors go abroad): the peer review mechanism. First peer review of India's CAG in 2012 found around 50% of CAG's irregularity allegations were not backed by concrete evidence.
