1998PYQ 1(Prelims 1998)

Consider the following events: 1. Indigo Revolt 2. Santhal Rebellion 3. Deccan Riot 4. Mutiny of the Sepoys The correct chronological sequence of these events is

Answer: (D)

(D) ✓ — Santhal Rebellion in Rajmahal hills under the leadership of Sidhu and Kanho in 1855-56. Sepoy mutiny took place in 1857. Indigo revolt took place in 1859–63 in Bengal; the Deccan riot took place in 1875 in parts of Pune and Satara districts of Maharashtra (both covered in future chapters — Ch 22 'The Indigo Revolt (1859–63)' + Ch 22 'Maharashtra').

Wrong options:

(A) ✗ — The sequence 4, 2, 1, 3 opens with the Sepoy Mutiny (1857) placed before the Santhal Rebellion — but the Santhal rising of 1855–56 had already been crushed before the Mutiny broke out.

(B) ✗ — Doubly wrong: it opens with the Mutiny (1857) before the Santhal Rebellion (1855–56), and it also places the Deccan Riots (1875) before the Indigo Revolt (1859–63), reversing both pairs.

(C) ✗ — Opens correctly (Santhal 1855–56 → Mutiny 1857) but reverses the last pair: the Indigo Revolt (1859–63) came well before the Deccan Riots (1875), so placing 3 before 1 fails.

2006PYQ 2(Prelims 2006)

Which one of the following revolts was made famous by Bankim Chandra Chatterjee in his novel 'Anand Math'?

Answer: (D)

(D) ✓ — Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay's novel Anandamath (1882) was inspired by the Sanyasi Rebellion. Its song Vande Mataram became the anthem of the Indian National Movement.

Wrong options:

(A) ✗ — The Bhil uprising belongs to the Khandesh & Rajputana hills of western India (1818–31), where EIC control arrived after the Third Anglo-Maratha War — a tribal revolt with no connection to Bankim's novel. Anandamath was inspired by the Sanyasi Rebellion alone.

(B) ✗ — The 1783 Rangpur rebellion was a peasant revolt of North Bengal against the oppressive revenue farmers Debi Singh & Gobinda Singh — remembered for its organised, targeted character, not for any literary treatment.

(C) ✗ — The Bishnupur & Birbhum revolts (1789) were risings of local zamindars & their ryots against the EIC's revenue demands — minor in scale and a frequent UPSC distractor, with no novel behind them.

2018PYQ 3(Prelims 2018)

After the Santhal rising subsided, what was/were the measure/measures taken by the Colonial Government? 1. The territories called 'Santhal Paraganas' were created. 2. It became illegal for a Santhal to transfer land to a Non-Santhal. Select the correct answer using the codes given below.

Answer: (C)

(C) ✓ — after the rising was crushed, a separate administrative unit — Santhal Parganas — was created, with formal protections for tribal culture & identity (statement 1), and the British imposed restrictions on the transfer of Santhal land to non-tribals within the Santhal Parganas (statement 2). Both measures were taken — Both 1 and 2.

Wrong options:

(A) ✗ — "Only 1" undercounts: statement 2 is equally true — the British imposed restrictions on transferring Santhal land to non-tribals within the Santhal Parganas, an early form of protective land legislation.

(B) ✗ — "Only 2" undercounts: statement 1 is equally true — the separate administrative unit called Santhal Parganas was created after the rising, with formal protections for tribal culture & identity.

(D) ✗ — "Neither" fails on both counts: both measures as the rising's direct political impact — the Santhal Parganas unit and the land-transfer restrictions.

2020PYQ 4(Prelims 2020)

With reference to the history of India, 'Ulgulan' or the Great Tumult is the description of which of the following events?

Answer: (D)

(D) ✓ — 'Ulgulan' (the Great Tumult) is the Munda name for Birsa Munda's rising of 1899–1900 in Chhota Nagpur — against loss of land to outsiders, British oppression, and missionary activity.

Wrong options:

(A) ✗ — "Ulgulan" (the Great Tumult) is the Munda name for Birsa Munda's rising of 1899–1900 in Chhota Nagpur. The Revolt of 1857 is a separate event four decades earlier, never called Ulgulan.

(B) ✗ — The Mappila/Moplah revolts belong to Malabar — Muslim ryots against Janmi landlords; the 1921 phase carries its own name, Mapilla Revolt, not "Ulgulan".

(C) ✗ — The Indigo Revolt (1859–63) was a revolt of Bengal cultivators against European indigo planters, not a tribal rising. "Ulgulan" names the Munda movement only.

FYQ 1

With reference to the British land revenue systems that drove the pre-1857 peasant revolts, consider the following statements: 1. Under the Farming System of 1772 in Bengal, the right to collect land revenue was auctioned to the highest bidder — the revenue farmer. 2. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 made the zamindar the permanent owner of land; in practice, he charged high rents, imposed illegal cesses (abwabs), and evicted tenants at will. 3. The Rent Act of 1859 gave occupancy rights to a ryot who had cultivated the same plot continuously for more than twelve years. Which of the statements given above are correct?

Answer: (D)

(d)

(d) ✓ — All three correct. (1) Farming System 1772 = revenue farmer via auction (verbatim).

(2) PS1793 made zamindar permanent owner; in practice he charged high rents, imposed illegal cesses (abwabs), and evicted tenants at will — these were extractive practices the system enabled, not legal rights granted. (3) Rent Act 1859 gave occupancy rights after 12 years on same plot (verbatim).

Memory hook: Farming System 1772 (revenue farmer) → PS1793 (zamindar permanent) → Rent Act 1859 (occupancy after 12 yrs).

FYQ 2

With reference to the indigo system in Bengal and Bihar, which one of the following is correct?

Answer: (B)

(b)

(b) ✓. The indigo system was "a debt trap by design" — loan at the outset locked the ryot, below-market buy-back kept him in debt, soil exhaustion damaged his food-crop land. Active in Bengal till 1860, Bihar till 1918.

Why others wrong: (a) ryots were the cultivators, not African labour. (c) European private planters drove indigo, not state monopoly. (d) Birsa Munda led the Munda Ulugulan 1899-1900, not the Indigo Revolt.

Memory hook: Indigo = loan trap → forced cultivation → below-market buyback → soil exhaustion. Bengal 1860 / Bihar 1918.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

FYQ 4

With reference to the Indian Forests Act 1878 and its three forest categories, which one of the following is correct?

Answer: (B)

(b)

(b) ✓, side box The Indian Forests Act 1878 — How the British Stole the Forest. Reserved = prohibit-unless-permitted (commercial priority); Protected = permit-unless-prohibited (transitional); Village = assigned but State-controlled.

Why others wrong: (a) reverses Reserved vs Protected logic; (c) the Act extinguished traditional rights, not protected them; (d) the Act was passed under Lytton76-80 (Viceroy 1876–80), not Curzon99-05.

Memory hook: Reserved = "prohibit unless permitted" (timber) · Protected = "permit unless prohibited" (transitional) · Village = nominal but State-owned. Passed under Lytton76-80 for railway-sleeper demand.

FYQ 5

With reference to the Abkari (excise) system imposed on tribals, which one of the following is correct?

Answer: (A)

(a)

(a) ✓, side box "The Abkari System — Taxing the Tribals' Own Drink". State monopoly + contractor auction + criminalised home brewing. Toddy/mahua were tied to religious rituals + festivals + daily nutrition.

Why others wrong: (b) home brewing without licence was criminalised. (c) the system squarely targeted tribal/Indian brewing. (d) excise was the second-largest provincial revenue after land revenue.

Memory hook: Abkari = State monopoly + contractor auction + home-brewing banned → tribals forced to buy back their own drink at inflated prices.

FYQ 7

With reference to the Paika Rebellion of 1817 in Khurda (Odisha), which one of the following is correct?

Answer: (A)

(a)

(a) ✓. Paikas = traditional landed militia (warrior class). Bakshi Jagabandhu = Raja of Khurda's military commander. Multi-class uprising — elite leadership + peasants + salt makers + Kondh tribals. Heavily debated as candidate for India's "First War of Independence".

Why others wrong: (b) Paikas were of Odisha, not Khasi hills. (c) British crushed the core uprising by 1818; Jagabandhu surrendered under negotiated terms in 1825. (d) Jagabandhu was the Raja's commander, not British-appointed.

Memory hook: Paika 1817 = landed-militia + Bakshi Jagabandhu + multi-class + "First War of Independence" candidate.

FYQ 8

With reference to the 1783 Rangpur Rebellion in North Bengal, which one of the following is correct?

(a) ✓. Revenue farmers + Great Bengal Famine 1770 = double squeeze. Peasants targeted grain stores, prisons, courts — they knew their oppressors. Self-imposed-tax government. Crushed by EIC.

Why others wrong: (b) Debi Singh + Gobinda Singh were the oppressors, not allies. (c) Sati Act 1829 post-dates the 1783 rebellion by 46 years. (d) Birsa Munda led the Munda Ulugulan 1899-1900.

FYQ 12

FYQ 13

With reference to the character of pre-1857 revolts, consider the following statements: 1. They were all-India movements with coordinated leadership across regions. 2. They lacked the modern vocabulary of rights, democracy, and "No Taxation Without Representation". 3. They were unmistakably political — religion provided ideology, organisation, and the habit of collective action. Which of the statements given above are correct?

Answer: (B)

(b)

(b) ✓ — Statements 2 + 3 correct.

Why statement 1 wrong: per 'Were these revolts examples of modern nationalism?', the revolts were NOT all-India movements — no unity across regions, no coordination, only local leaders. That's why they fell short of "modern nationalism".

Why statement 2 correct: 'Then why call them "pre-history of modern nationalism"?' — "Their vocabulary was not the modern one of rights, democracy, colonialism, or 'No Taxation Without Representation.'"

Why statement 3 correct: 'Then why call them "pre-history of modern nationalism"?' — they were "unmistakably political", with religion providing ideology, organisation, and collective action.

Memory hook: Pre-1857 revolts = NOT all-India, NOT modern-vocabulary, BUT unmistakably political. INM later called them "pre-history of modern nationalism".

FYQ 14

With reference to Birsa Munda and the Munda Ulugulan (1899–1900), which one of the following is correct?

(a) ✓. Birsa Munda → loss of land + British oppression + missionary activity. Belief in supernatural protection (bullets to water). Christmas 1899 timing was deliberate — confronting the missionary religion eroding Munda identity. Prelims 2020 anchor: "Ulgulan = Birsa Munda's Revolt 1899–1900".

FYQ 15

With reference to the Rampa Rebellion / Fituri in the Rampa Hills of Andhra, which one of the following is correct?

Answer: (A)

(a)

(a) ✓. Phase 1 grievances: commercial forest extraction + road acquisitions + moneylender land loss + Podu ban + forest-resource restrictions + Abkari toddy tax. Phase 2 (1920) merged with NCM; Alluri Sitarama Raju re-initiated Fituri after NCM withdrawal 1922; captured and executed 1924.

Why others wrong: (b) Birsa Munda led Munda Ulugulan in Chhota Nagpur, not Rampa. (c) Alluri concluded India could be liberated only by force after NCM withdrawal — opposite of Gandhian non-violence. (d) crushed by 1880; no tribal self-rule survived.

Memory hook: Rampa Fituri — Phase 1 (1870s-1880 forest/toddy) → Phase 2 (1920 NCM-merge under Alluri) → 1922 NCM-withdrawal → 1924 Alluri executed. The Revolt of 1857

FYQ 1 "The pre-1857 peasant and tribal revolts were the 'pre-history of modern nationalism' — political in nature, even if pre-modern in vocabulary." Examine this view with reference to specific revolts.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Defend or qualify the "pre-history of modern nationalism" verdict using specific revolt examples.

Guiding themes:

1. Open with the verdict — INM leaders' label "pre-history of modern nationalism" captures both what these revolts were (political) and what they lacked (modern nationalism's all-India coordination). 'Understanding the Character of Pre-1857 Revolts'.

2. Evidence of pre-modern character: not all-India, no inter-regional coordination, no modern vocabulary of rights / democracy / colonialism. 'Understanding the Character of Pre-1857 Revolts'.

3. Evidence of political character: ideology (restore moral order), organisation (religious sects provided leadership + collective-action habits), programme (meetings + plans), political awareness (Rangpur targeted grain stores + prisons + courts; Kols targeted property not lives; Santhals knew exactly who their oppressors were). 'Understanding the Character of Pre-1857 Revolts'.

4. Specific revolt examples: Santhal Hool 1855-56 (Sido + Kanhu Murmu's prior public warning, "unholy trinity" = British + zamindar + moneylender — 'Tribal Uprisings — Pre-1857'; Faraizi Dudu Mian ("land belongs to God" slogan as proto-political ideology — 'Religion-Led Revolts'; Paika 1817 (multi-class uprising candidate for "First War of Independence" — 'Civil Uprisings'.

5. Close: the INM leaders' label is fair — pre-modern in form, political in substance. Different vocabulary, same moral architecture.

Revise: 'Understanding the Character of Pre-1857 Revolts' and 'Tribal Uprisings — Pre-1857' + 'Religion-Led Revolts' and 'Civil Uprisings'

FYQ 2 "The Indian Forests Act 1878 was less a piece of forestry legislation than a legal mechanism for the dispossession of forest-dwelling communities." Discuss with reference to its three-category structure and its colonial purpose.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Evaluate the Forests Act 1878 as a dispossession instrument rather than scientific forestry.

Guiding themes:

1. Open with the colonial-purpose framing — the Act was passed under Viceroy Lytton76-80, driven by the 1870s railway boom (each mile = 2,000 wooden sleepers needed). State needed absolute control over commercial forests. 'Drivers of the Tribal Revolt' side box.

2. The three categories as a dispossession architecture: Reserved Forests (everything prohibited unless permitted — commercial-timber priority, all customary rights extinguished); Protected Forests (transitional category, State retained power to upgrade to Reserved); Village Forests (nominal community assignment, State never surrendering ownership). 'Drivers of the Tribal Revolt' side box.

3. The legal masterstroke — customary rights transformed into State-granted privileges. Privileges can be taxed, restricted, revoked. Overnight, collecting firewood for cooking, grazing a cow, clearing a subsistence patch made millions of forest-dwellers into trespassers on their ancestral land. 'Drivers of the Tribal Revolt' side box close.

4. The Act's connection to tribal revolts (pre-1857 + post-1857): forest dispossession fed grievances of Kol (see 'Tribal Uprisings — Pre-1857'), Khasi (see 'Tribal Uprisings — Pre-1857'), Santhal (see 'Tribal Uprisings — Pre-1857'), Munda (see 'Post-1857 Tribal Uprisings'), Rampa (see 'Post-1857 Tribal Uprisings'). Forest laws became a tribal-resistance flashpoint into the 1920s — Alluri Sitarama Raju's NCM-era forest-law violations (see 'Post-1857 Tribal Uprisings').

5. Close: Forest Rights Act 2006 today attempts to correct what the 1878 Act did — testimony to the persistence of the dispossession.

Revise: 'Drivers of the Tribal Revolt' — side box "The Indian Forests Act 1878 — How the British Stole the Forest" + 'Tribal Uprisings — Pre-1857' and 'Post-1857 Tribal Uprisings'

FYQ 3 Compare and contrast Civil Uprisings and Peasant Revolts of the pre-1857 period along the axes of leadership, grievance, and political character.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Two-column compare-contrast with named examples; close with a synthesis on what each contributed to "pre-history of modern nationalism".

Guiding themes:

1. Open with the typology — 'Two Kinds of Uprisings' distinguishes Civil Uprisings (elite-led: Raja / zamindar / mansabdar) from Peasant Revolts (peasant-led). 1857 itself was the biggest Civil Uprising.

2. Leadership axis — Civil: dispossessed elite (Chait Singh of Benaras, Poligars, Veluthampi, Bundela chiefs, Paika's Bakshi Jagabandhu — 'Civil Uprisings'. Peasant: ryots themselves (Rangpur 1783, Mysore 1830-31 — 'Peasant Revolts Led by Peasants'.

3. Grievance axis — Civil: loss of privilege under Rule of Law / Subsidiary Alliance interference / annexation. Peasant: revenue-farmer extraction + famine + indebtedness. Both share the deep economic squeeze, but the elite saw it as loss of status, the peasant as loss of subsistence.

4. Political-character axis — Civil uprisings carried the more traditional vocabulary of restoring a king or a privileged order; Peasant revolts more directly targeted oppressive instruments (grain stores, prisons, courts in Rangpur — 'Peasant Revolts Led by Peasants'. Both were political in the pre-modern sense per 'Understanding the Character of Pre-1857 Revolts'.

5. Synthesis: Civil uprisings contributed the leadership template and the multi-class coalition (Paika 1817 as the strongest example — 'Civil Uprisings'; Peasant revolts contributed the targeting-the-instrument political precision. Together they form the "pre-history of modern nationalism" (see 'Understanding the Character of Pre-1857 Revolts').

Revise: 'Two Kinds of Uprisings' and 'Civil Uprisings' + 'Peasant Revolts Led by Peasants' and 'Understanding the Character of Pre-1857 Revolts'

FYQ 4 "Tribal uprisings of the colonial period were not merely economic revolts but defences of an entire way of life." Examine with reference to the major pre- and post-1857 tribal uprisings.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Demonstrate that tribal uprisings were defences of autonomy + forest-relationship + cultural identity, not just economic protests.

Guiding themes:

1. Open with the "way of life" framing — 'Drivers of the Tribal Revolt' opens with the two things that gave tribal life its shape: tribal autonomy (their own customary law) + relationship with the forest (home, livelihood, identity). British rule destroyed both.

2. Economic dimension — high land revenue + cash payment + moneylender arrival + indebtedness + Jhum ban + Abkari (see 'Drivers of the Tribal Revolt'). All real, all material.

3. But the deeper grievance was way-of-life destruction: Indian Forests Act 1878 turned customary rights into State-granted privileges (see 'Drivers of the Tribal Revolt'); Abkari criminalised toddy/mahua brewing tied to religious rituals (see 'Drivers of the Tribal Revolt'); Kandh resistance was triggered by the British ban on Meriah human sacrifice — a humanitarian intervention to the British, a religious-autonomy attack to the Kandhs (see 'Tribal Uprisings — Pre-1857').

4. Specific revolt examples: Santhal "unholy trinity" framing — British + zamindar + moneylender — recognised the structural attack on Santhal identity, not just income (see 'Tribal Uprisings — Pre-1857'); Birsa Munda's deliberate Christmas 1899 timing was an act of cultural defiance against missionary religion (see 'Post-1857 Tribal Uprisings'); Rampa Phase 1 grievances list (see 'Post-1857 Tribal Uprisings') explicitly include forest-resource restrictions and toddy tax — economy + culture inseparable.

5. Close: this is why tribal revolts kept recurring — the way-of-life attack was never reversed under British rule. Forest Rights Act 2006 (see 'Drivers of the Tribal Revolt') is independent India's belated acknowledgment.

Revise: 'Drivers of the Tribal Revolt' and 'Tribal Uprisings — Pre-1857' + 'Tribal Uprisings — Pre-1857' and 'Post-1857 Tribal Uprisings' + 'Post-1857 Tribal Uprisings'

FYQ 5 "Religion played a dual role — both as grievance and as organiser — in the pre-1857 revolts." Substantiate with reference to specific revolts and assess religion's contribution to political mobilisation.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Show religion-as-grievance + religion-as-organiser dynamic, with specific revolt examples; assess what religion contributed to political mobilisation in the pre-1857 organisational vacuum.

Guiding themes:

1. Open with the dual-role framing — 'Drivers of the Peasant Revolt', 'Drivers of the Peasant Revolt': religion played both grievance and organiser roles.

2. Religion as grievance — Christian missionary conversions + ending of revenue-free / rent-free religious land tenures + social-reform laws perceived as religious interference (Sati Abolition 1829, Lex Loci 1850, Hindu Widow Remarriages 1856). 'Drivers of the Peasant Revolt'.

3. Religion as organiser — pre-1857 India had no political parties, no mass-reach newspapers, no formal organisations. Religion filled the organisational vacuum: a religious sect already had a leader, a philosophy, a sense of community, the habit of collective action. 'Drivers of the Peasant Revolt'.

4. Specific revolt examples: Sanyasi-Fakir cooperation despite Hindu-Muslim differences because economic grievances aligned (see 'Religion-Led Revolts'); Titu Mir's Wahabi sect with the distinctive beard + dress + bamboo fort at Narkelberia (see 'Religion-Led Revolts'); Faraizi Dudu Mian's "land belongs to God" slogan that made economic resistance into religious duty (see 'Religion-Led Revolts'); Moplah Revolts where Muslim-vs-Brahmin religious identity intensified economic grievance (see 'Religion-Led Revolts').

5. Close: religion's contribution to mobilisation was real but limited — it gave bonds of unity within a sect, not across sects; it kept revolts intensely local; the absence of secular nation-level mobilisation is precisely what marks pre-1857 as "pre-history" (see 'Understanding the Character of Pre-1857 Revolts') rather than fully formed nationalism. The INM had to build secular political organisation from scratch.

Revise: 'Drivers of the Peasant Revolt' and 'Religion-Led Revolts' — full coverage + 'Understanding the Character of Pre-1857 Revolts' for the synthesis
