Europeans came for Asia's goods, the Portuguese won the sea with a navy and a State, the Dutch and English companies took over with capital and Charters, and the EIC's 1661 powers turned a trader into a ruler.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Europe wanted Asia's goods, the Ottomans closed the land road, and the Portuguese opened the sea road as a State enterprise. The Dutch and English companies, backed by capital and Charters, took the sea from them, and the EIC's 1661 powers started the shift from trader to ruler.

  1. Why Asia? Asia was the economic power of the world: Indonesia gave spices, India textiles, China silk and tea. Arabs and the merchants of Genoa and Venice carried them to a Europe that was not wealthy, and each handoff made huge profit. Da Gama's 1498 cargo sold at 800% profit, and in the 1600s a kilogram of clove cost the equivalent of a kilogram of gold.
    → But in 1453 the road was cut.
  2. The road closes. In 1453 the Ottoman Empire captured Constantinople and (the teacher corrects it to 'effectively') blockaded the land routes between Europe and Asia. Necessity drove the Age of Discovery, led first by Portugal and Spain. The era gave the astrolabe, the magnetic compass, latitudes and longitudes, geography as a discipline and the caravel, a ship that could sail at an angle against the wind.
    → Two rivals now claimed one ocean, and someone had to draw a line.
  3. The line. Pope Alexander VI mediated the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494. An imaginary line in the Atlantic, about 1,185 miles west of the Cape Verde Islands, gave lands to the east to Portugal and lands to the west to Spain. Brazil's eastern tip fell on the Portuguese side, so Brazil became the only Portuguese colony in the Americas.
    → Armed with the line, Portugal sent Da Gama round Africa.
  4. Calicut and Cochin. Da Gama reached Calicut in 1498 and got permission to trade from the Zamorin, but faced Arab opposition and left without a factory. In 1500 Cabral set up the first factory at Calicut, which Arab merchants destroyed, and then the first permanent factory at Cochin under the Raja of Cochin's protection. In 1502 Da Gama bombarded Calicut, and in 1503 Albuquerque built Fort Manuel at Cochin, the first European fort in India.
    → Now Portugal needed a way to hold the sea.
  5. Blue water, then Goa. Almeida 05-09, the first Governor, chose naval supremacy over territory (the Blue Water Policy) and forced every non-Portuguese ship to buy a Cartaz. In 1509 he beat a coalition of Gujarat, Mamluk Egypt and the Zamorin at Diu. Albuquerque 09-15 took Goa from the Sultan of Bijapur in 1510, a rare land conquest, and Nuno da Cunha 29-38 moved the HQ to Goa in 1530 and acquired Bassein (1534) and Diu (1535).
    → But a State trading empire built on force carried the seeds of its own fall.
  6. The fall of the Portuguese. The Goa Inquisition (1560-1812) and forced conversions bred resistance. St Francis Xavier, who spearheaded conversions, died on Shangchuan Island in 1552, not Goa. The causes of decline were political (the Marathas), religious backlash, corruption and personal trade, the English and Dutch, and overextension with Brazil as the priority.
    → Those English and Dutch were already at the gates.
  7. The companies arrive. After the English beat the Spanish Armada in 1588, British and Dutch ships could sail to Asia. The VOC was founded in 1602 by merging six companies and was the first joint-stock company with tradable shares. It reached Masulipatnam in 1605 and took Cochin in 1663. It was beaten at Colachel in 1741 by Marthanda Varma 29-58 and at Bedara in 1759, lost its posts through the Kew Letters of 1795 and was dissolved in 1799.
    → The English company had its own Charter ladder to climb.
  8. A licence that became power. The English EIC began on 31 December 1600 under a Royal Charter of Elizabeth I 58-03, a 15-year monopoly pooled from 215 London merchants. James I 03-25 made it perpetual in 1609 and Cromwell 51-60 made it a permanent joint-stock company in 1657. The 1661 Charter of Charles II 60-85 gave it forts, troops, war and peace and civil and criminal jurisdiction, so it became a quasi-State.
    → With those powers it needed a base on the west coast.
  9. Surat, Swally and Roe. In 1608 Hawkins went to Jahangir 05-27's court for a Surat factory but was expelled under Portuguese pressure. The lesson was to defeat Portugal first, and in 1612 Thomas Best won the Battle of Swally. Jahangir then issued a Royal Farman in 1613 for a permanent factory at Surat, and Thomas Roe (1615-19) won the Farman of 1618 for liberal trading rights across the Mughal Empire.
    → One more royal wedding moved the base to Bombay.
  10. Presidencies. In 1661 Catherine of Braganza's dowry gave England Bombay and Tangier and 500,000 pounds, and in 1668 the Crown gave Bombay to the EIC for 10 pounds a year. The EIC's first Presidency was Surat (1618), then Madras (1652/1653), Bombay (1687) and Calcutta (1699/1700), each named after its fort. Each began as a trade HQ and grew into a province of British India.
    → Two smaller rivals were left in the field.
  11. Danes, French and the order. The Danes (company 1616, Tranquebar 1620, Serampore 1755) sold their mainland posts to Britain by 1845. The French company (1664) opened at Surat in 1668 and acquired Pondicherry in 1674, which Francois Martin 01-06 made the capital in 1701. Pondicherry's occupiers ran Portuguese, Danes, French, Dutch, English, then India in 1954, so the order of arrival is a favourite Prelims test.

In simple words#

  • Asia had what Europe wanted: spices from Indonesia, textiles from India, silk and tea from China. Arabs and the traders of Genoa and Venice carried them, and the Ottoman capture of Constantinople (1453) made Europe look for a new sea route.
  • Portugal and Spain split the new world at Tordesillas (1494). Vasco Da Gama reached Calicut in 1498, and the Portuguese then ran trade as a State enterprise (the Estado da India) through forts, a navy, the Cartaz pass and Goa (1510).
  • After the English beat the Spanish Armada (1588), the Dutch VOC (1602) and the English EIC (Charter of 1600) broke the Portuguese-Spanish monopoly. These were joint-stock companies, not State enterprises.
  • The EIC beat the Portuguese at Swally (1612), won a Farman for Surat (1613), and got liberal trading rights through Roe (1618). The 1661 Charter then gave it forts, troops, war and peace, making it a quasi-State.
  • The EIC built three Presidencies (Madras, Bombay, Calcutta; Surat first). The Dutch fell at Colachel (1741) and Bedara (1759), the Danes and French stayed small, and Pondicherry (1674) became the French centre.

5 · How the Dutch and English broke the Portuguese-Spanish monopoly#

Story link

Because the Portuguese-Spanish hold had to break, the 1588 Armada defeat opened the sea. Ask: why did the Dutch and English go to Indonesia first?

  • By the second half of the 16th century two new naval powers had emerged: Britain and the Dutch Republic (Netherlands).
  • Both wanted to break the strict monopoly held by Spain and Portugal over European international trade and to achieve freedom of navigation in the oceans.
  • 1588: the British Navy defeated the Spanish Armada ("Invincible Armada") in the Atlantic, severely weakening Spanish-Portuguese naval supremacy.
  • By 1580 Portugal had entered a political union with Spain, so defeating Spain also opened trade routes Portugal had controlled.

  • India came later, for a commercial reason: the people of the spice islands had little interest in English or Dutch goods but readily accepted Indian cotton textiles.To buy spices in Indonesia you first needed cloth from India.

7 · The English EIC and the Royal Charter of 1600#

Story link

Because the EIC lived on a licence, its Charter shaped its fate. Ask: who gave the Charter, and what did it give?

  • The English East India Company was formed on 31 December 1600: its founding document was a Royal Charter issued by Queen Elizabeth I 58-03.
  • A Royal Charter is essentially a licence granted by the Monarch.If it was not renewed, the company would legally cease to exist.
  • This shaped the EIC's relationship with the British State for the next two and a half centuries: its survival depended on the goodwill of whoever held power in Britain.
  • The EIC was the 4th joint-stock company in England, formed by pooling money from 215 London merchants ("merchant adventurers") who wanted to challenge Dutch dominance in European trade with the East.
  • The stated goal was trade with the East, not empire-building or colonialism.
Handout note
  • Britain or England? In 1600 it was England (not Britain) that granted the Charter.
  • England and Scotland united in 1707 to create the Kingdom of Great Britain
  • In 1801 Ireland was added, creating the United Kingdom.
  • Before 1707 "British" technically means English.

What the Royal Charter of 1600 gave the EIC

  • 1. A monopoly over England's trade with the East Indies for 15 years.
  • 2. The right to carry gold (foreign exchange) out of England to purchase eastern goods.
  • 3. Management by a body of private people: a Governor, a Deputy Governor and 24 members, which later evolved into the Board or Court of Directors.

10 · How the EIC broke Portuguese dominance: Hawkins, Swally and Surat#

Story link

Because the EIC wanted Surat, it first had to beat Portugal. Ask: what is the difference between 1612 and 1613?

  • 1608: the EIC decided to set up a factory at Surat, then the most important port on India's west coast and firmly within Portuguese-dominated territory.
  • It sent Captain William Hawkins to the court of Jahangir 05-27.
  • Jahangir 05-27 first gave Hawkins a Mansab (rank of 400) and a Jagir, but Hawkins was ultimately expelled from the Mughal court due to persistent Portuguese pressure.
  • The lesson:Portugal had to be defeated militarily before the EIC could establish itself on the west coast.

11 · The Royal Farman of 1618 and Thomas Roe (1615-19)#

Story link

Because one factory was not enough, England sent an ambassador. Ask: what did Roe's Farman of 1618 give?

  • England wanted liberal trading rights across the entire Mughal Empire, so King James I 03-25 sent Thomas Roe as formal ambassador to Jahangir 05-27's court.
  • Roe arrived at Surat in 1615 and stayed until 1619.
  • Jahangir 05-27 refused a formal treaty between equals (a trading company was no sovereign equal).
  • Through sustained diplomacy and personal rapport Roe got a series of privileges and a Royal Farman (1618) allowing the EIC to set up factories throughout the Mughal Empire with liberal trading rights: lower customs duties and ease of doing business.
  • 1616-19: new factories at Agra, Ahmedabad and Broach (Bharuch).
  • With Surat they formed the Surat Presidency (1618): the EIC's first administrative Presidency in India, coordinating English trade on the west coast until the later shift to Bombay (1687).
  • Portuguese enraged
  • naval conflicts continued. 1631-32: Shahjahan 27-58 ordered his Bengal governor Qasim Khan to defeat the Portuguese and eject them from Hugli, because they were misusing their trade privileges with piracy and the slave trade from their Bengal base

15 · Why Ceylon did not become part of British India: the Crown Colony#

Story link

Because Ceylon was too important for a company to run, Britain took it directly. Ask: who ruled India, and who ruled Ceylon?
