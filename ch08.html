Teacher's board

Europe was not wealthy; spices and the 800% profit

AspectDetail
Europe was not wealthyEastern goods were luxuries only the rich could afford. A common European could not afford Indian cotton or Chinese silk. The profit came from supplying Europe's wealthy classes, and Genoa and Venice guarded this position zealously
Spices held a special placeUntil the 1600s European food was as spiced as Indian food. Spices preserved and made palatable the meat stored through winter (no industrial refrigerator: clove and pepper did the job)

The EIC triangle trade (teacher's triangle)

(1) Buy goods: the EIC buys goods (cloth) from India
(2) Sell Indian goods: the EIC sells Indian goods to Indonesia
(3) Sell goods: Indonesian goods (spices) come back to the EIC
(4) The EIC sells Indonesian goods to the rest of Europe for profit
  • Shift over time: as European incomes rose in the 17th and 18th centuries and spices became more widely available, Indian cotton textiles became the most sought-after commodity from the East. But in the beginning it was spice that started everything.
Teacher's board

"As people came out of poverty / as the size of the middle class expanded, demand increased" (incomes rose: underlined).

4.0 · Da Gama at Calicut (1498)

AspectDetail
Da Gama at Calicut (1498)Armed with the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) and backed by the Portuguese Crown, Vasco Da Gama reached Calicut (Kozhikode, Kerala) in 1498, sailing round the Cape of Good Hope, the southernmost point of Africa
He traded and leftReceived permission to trade from the Zamorin of Calicut but faced heavy opposition from Arab traders. No factory or permanent settlement on this voyage
Note

The handout adds: the Cape was named "Cape of Good Hope" by Portugal's King John II, as it represented hope of finally finding a sea route to India.

Handout alert

The teacher wrote "NOT COCHIN" beside Calicut: Da Gama landed at Calicut (1498), and the first permanent factory was at Cochin (1500), not his landing place.

Why trade, not empire? (the teacher's board)

Teacher's board

Primary purpose of the Europeans = Trade, not empire building. But empire building did take place in the Americas (weak states), while India had strong states (land powers, i.e. strong armies). And the board adds: resources from mining (gold, silver) are an extra plus; if empire building is possible, there are greater trade profits (+) and they can get land revenue (LR) and other taxes. With political control, as government in any territory you can export goods to the home country at a low price.

  • Why South India was vulnerable (handout): when the Portuguese arrived, the Mughal Empire (1526-1858) did not yet exist in India. South India was divided between the Vijayanagara Empire (1336-1646) and the Deccan Sultanates, without a unified central naval power. This fragmentation let the Portuguese dominate trade and some territory in the 16th century.
  • Character of the Portuguese approach: part commerce, part conquest, part coercion.
Teacher's board

Learn the west coast from north to south: Diu, Surat, Daman, Bombay, Goa, Mahe, Calicut, Cochin, Travancore. Calicut = a small kingdom; Cochin = a small kingdom on the northern border of Travancore.

Note

4.1 · Structure of trade: a State enterprise

  • Unlike the private joint-stock companies the British and Dutch later set up, Portuguese trade was a State enterprise called the Estado da India, directly controlled by the Portuguese Crown through its official administration. The Portuguese government was the trader.
Teacher's board

[Like a PSU / Govt]. The English EIC and the Dutch VOC were private companies. The teacher's side note: the French EIC was initially a private company; later taken over by the French Govt.

Depot, Factory, Settlement

  • In this period a "factory" did not mean a manufacturing unit. It was a trading post: warehouses for storing goods, offices for trade, homes for company employees, and often a fort for protection. The word comes from "factor," a merchant agent; the factory was where factors worked.

Teacher's board

[Think bus depot] for a depot ("for going out of India": goods waiting for the ship). A factory is also called a trading post; its function is the "Office." A settlement has multiple factories and warehouses and "collects LR (land revenue) and consumes LR." Don't confuse "LEJ" here: a depot has no (L)egislative, (E)xecutive, (J)udicial jurisdiction; a settlement has sovereign territorial rights: L E J. [Taxes can be charged only by the Ruler / Govt.]

4.3 · Blue Water Policy and the Cartaz System (Almeida 05-09, 1505-09)

AspectDetail
1st GovernorFrancisco de Almeida 05-09 was the 1st Governor of the Portuguese Estado da India (Viceroy in common usage, but the legal title was Governador)
Blue Water PolicyEmphasis on NAVAL supremacy rather than territorial conquest. Since India had powerful land-based States, the Portuguese chose to dominate oceans and maritime trade routes. The objective: a monopolistic trading regime without extensive territorial expansion
Why South India was vulnerableNo Mughal Empire yet (1526-1858); South India divided between Vijayanagara (1336-1646) and the Deccan Sultanates, with no unified central naval power
Their approachCommerce, coercion in trade, and some (boxed) conquest: Goa 1510
Teacher's board

Blue Water = oceans and seas. The decision to focus on the blue waters was expected, since the primary goal of the Age of Discovery (16th c) was trade. Goa 1510 = the exception to this Blue Water policy. Almeida 05-09 was the "CEO of the company in India"; from 1510+ the Governor = CEO + CM of Goa. WHY: they had a better navy, the goal was oceanic trade, and Indian armies were strong (so land conquest was hard); the south was also weak since Talikota 1565 (the note is partly unclear on the slide).

Teacher's board

Territorial army was strong for Indian kingdoms. Europe's navy was usually greater than the Indian powers' navy (Marathas and Travancore had good navies, and books talk about it). Land-locked states focus on the territorial army; states with a coast focus on the territorial army plus a Navy, in budget and technology. (Examples on the board: Portugal, Dutch, French, British, Spain.)

Teacher's board

Cartaz = a toll booth on the sea. On land a car on a highway pays at a TOLL booth; in the oceans a ship on a trade route pays Cartaz fees.

Cartaz and the Battle of Diu
ItemDetail
Cartaz SystemA naval licensing system, part of the Blue Water Policy: all non-Portuguese ships had to buy a pass (Cartaz) and pay a licence fee. Pay and get a pass; refuse and face attack
TargetThe Arab traders who controlled the Arabian Sea for generations; also local Indian rulers and, later, Mughal ships
Battle of Diu (1509)Almeida 05-09 decisively defeated a joint Muslim naval coalition: Sultanate of Gujarat, Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt, Zamorin of Calicut
Backed byThe Ottoman Empire (troops and galleys) and the Republic of Venice (whose spice trade was being destroyed)
ResultPortuguese naval supremacy in the Arabian Sea for the next century
Teacher's board

Not a battle for Diu as a territory, but for the domination of the Arabian Sea. The coalition was "all stakeholders of the old routes": Arabs (Egypt), Indian (Gujarat, Zamorin), military ships from the Ottoman. 1502: Arabs and the Zamorin defeated in India; 1509: top player of the Arabian Sea. The teacher also notes that only under Akbar 56-05 was the coastal area added to the Mughal state.

Note

The handout: the coalition's scale shows how seriously the established trading powers of the Old World viewed the Portuguese threat; Venice's trade was being destroyed by Portuguese dominance of the Indian Ocean.

The teacher marked [Babur 26-30 not in India] (Babur 26-30 had not yet arrived in 1510). Teacher's board

Religion = Soft Power. Conversion reduces opposition to the political power of the (Christian) Portuguese and gives a narrative of brotherhood: social power (success at conversion) increases political power. Abolition of Sati (or any social reform) can have two intents: (1) Conversion, by weakening the people's bond with their religion (abolishing, making fun of, or targeting the legitimacy of rituals); (2) Noble intent: reform or women's empowerment.

4.7 · Portuguese contribution: New World crops

  • The Portuguese introduced these New World crops that permanently transformed Indian agriculture and cuisine: tobacco, red chillies, papaya, pineapple, guava.
  • Cocoa and rubber are also New World plants, but reached India only in the British period, not through the Portuguese.
  • The Columbian Exchange: European exploration of the Americas in the 15th and 16th centuries brought the Old and New Worlds into contact. Plants, animals and microbes from Europe, Asia and Africa went to the Americas, and many others went from the Americas to Europe, Asia and Africa.

4.8 · Why did the Portuguese decline?

CauseDetail
i) PoliticalStrong regional powers rose in Persia, Egypt and North India; especially the rise of the Marathas directly threatened the Portuguese (1739: Marathas defeated the Portuguese); teacher: Konkan coast: Marathas
ii) Religious backlashJesuit activity and the brutality of the Goa Inquisition (1560-1812) produced deep local resistance
iii) Economic and administrative decayCorruption, inefficiency, decline in discipline, and employees' piracy and illicit trade (teacher: "personal trade") eroded the institutions
iv) External competitionThe English and Dutch, with superior naval technology, joint-stock finance and more disciplined commercial organisation, displaced them
v) Imperial overextensionFocus diverted to Brazil (far more lucrative) plus lack of support from Lisbon (the Govt) starved the Estado da India
Remember

Takeaway: the Portuguese set the stage for all later European expansion in India: colonial administration centred in Goa, armed maritime trade, and religious conversion as a tool of political control became patterns the British would study, adapt and eventually supersede.

5 · How the Dutch and English broke the Portuguese-Spanish monopoly#

  • By the second half of the 16th century two new naval powers had emerged: Britain and the Dutch Republic (Netherlands).
  • Both wanted to break the strict monopoly held by Spain and Portugal over European international trade and to achieve freedom of navigation in the oceans.
  • 1588: the British Navy defeated the Spanish Armada ("Invincible Armada") in the Atlantic, severely weakening Spanish-Portuguese naval supremacy.
  • By 1580 Portugal had entered a political union with Spain, so defeating Spain also opened trade routes Portugal had controlled.
  • With the Armada defeated, British and Dutch ships could travel safely to Asia via the Cape of Good Hope.
  • First priority for both: Indonesia (the spice trade of the East Indies). India came later, for a commercial reason: the people of the spice islands had little interest in English or Dutch goods but readily accepted Indian cotton textiles. To buy spices in Indonesia you first needed cloth from India.
Note

The handout's clarification: the Netherlands, Holland and "the Dutch Republic" all refer to the same country. "Dutch" is the adjective; "Holland" is technically a region within the Netherlands but is often used as a general name.

Remember

The trilateral (triangle) trade: England → India → Indonesia → England. This is the commercial logic that shifted the EIC's focus from Indonesia to the Indian subcontinent. (Compare the teacher's triangle in section 1.)

6 · The Dutch VOC#

  • 1602: the Dutch East India Company (VOC, Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie) was established by a Charter issued by the Dutch Parliament, by merging six smaller Dutch companies that had been competing for eastern trade.
  • Takeaway (handout): the VOC was the world's first multinational company, and the first to issue publicly tradable stocks and shares on a formal exchange: the world's first joint-stock company. An ordinary person could become a part-owner by buying shares, without a say in management: the ancestor of the BSE, NSE, NYSE and the modern shareholder economy.
  • Charter powers: the right to wage war, sign treaties, acquire territory, build forts and mint its own currency: powers that ordinarily belong to a State, showing how European powers saw companies as an extension of State power.
  • The first Dutch expedition (1595) went to the East Indies (Indonesia), not India.
  • Dutch settlements in India: Masulipatnam (Northern Sarkars) 1605 (first factory), Pulicat (Carnatic) 1610, Surat (Gujarat) 1616, Bimilipatam (Golconda) 1641, Karikal (near Pondicherry) 1645, Chinsura (Bengal) 1653, later Kasimbazar, Baranagore, Patna, Balasore, Nagapatnam, Agra, Cochin (where they captured the Portuguese fort in 1663).
  • At its peak in 1669 the VOC was the richest private company in the world: 150 merchant vessels, 40 warships, an army of 10,000 soldiers, 50,000 employees; over its life 5,000 voyages from the Netherlands to the East Indies.
  • 1799: bankrupt and dissolved; possessions taken over by the Dutch government as the Dutch East Indies (Indonesia), a Dutch colony until independence in 1945 after World War II.
Handout alert

Prelims 2022 trap: the Gajapati dynasty and the Dutch. The claim that the Dutch received land grants from the Gajapati dynasty of Odisha is INCORRECT. The Gajapatis were a 15th-century dynasty centred in Odisha whose power had declined well before the Dutch arrived. Do not confuse later local rulers of the Odisha coast with the Gajapati dynasty itself.

7 · The English EIC and the Royal Charter of 1600#

  • The English East India Company was formed on 31 December 1600: its founding document was a Royal Charter issued by Queen Elizabeth I 58-03.
  • A Royal Charter is essentially a licence granted by the Monarch. If it was not renewed, the company would legally cease to exist. This shaped the EIC's relationship with the British State for the next two and a half centuries: its survival depended on the goodwill of whoever held power in Britain.
  • The EIC was the 4th joint-stock company in England, formed by pooling money from 215 London merchants ("merchant adventurers") who wanted to challenge Dutch dominance in European trade with the East. The stated goal was trade with the East, not empire-building or colonialism.
Note

Britain or England? In 1600 it was England (not Britain) that granted the Charter. England and Scotland united in 1707 to create the Kingdom of Great Britain; in 1801 Ireland was added, creating the United Kingdom. Before 1707 "British" technically means English.

What the Royal Charter of 1600 gave the EIC

  1. A monopoly over England's trade with the East Indies for 15 years.
  2. The right to carry gold (foreign exchange) out of England to purchase eastern goods.
  3. Management by a body of private people: a Governor, a Deputy Governor and 24 members, which later evolved into the Board or Court of Directors.
Note

The handout's forex aside: for a State, forex is the currency that matters in international trade; domestic currency works only within a country's borders (like a household's internal reward system for children, valid only inside the home). Until 1971, Gold was forex, until the Dollar took over. (Same idea as the teacher's board in section 1.)

8 · Charters until 1793: the EIC's changing legal character#

The EIC's legal existence depended on Charter renewal, and the Charters also tell the shift of power in Britain from the Monarch to Parliament.

  • 1601-03, the first voyage (James Lancaster): the fleet sailed not to India but to Indonesia, consistent with the EIC's original priority of the spice trade. They reached Java and Sumatra, where they set up the very first EIC factory. On the way Lancaster's fleet looted a Portuguese ship, the Sao Tome, in the Strait of Malacca (a cargo of textiles and spices). William Dalrymple and others note that the EIC's first major profit came not from trade but from outright piracy. Shareholders got substantial dividends; the fleet returned in 1603.
  • 1609, James I 03-25: the original 15-year monopoly became a perpetual monopoly.
  • 1657, Cromwell 51-60's Charter: Context: Charles I 25-49 tried to levy taxes without Parliament's consent, which triggered the English Civil War (1642-51). The Parliamentarians (largely Protestant) defeated the Royalists; Charles I 25-49 was beheaded in 1649; England became a Republic under Oliver Cromwell 51-60 (the handout writes his years as 51-60). After Cromwell 51-60's death in 1658 his son briefly took over, but the experiment proved autocratic and unpopular. In 1660 the Monarchy was restored under Charles II 60-85. Cromwell 51-60 issued a new Charter in 1657, declaring the previous Royal Charter invalid, and reorganised the EIC into a permanent joint-stock company. Earlier, money was raised voyage by voyage (pool, voyage, profits, fund dissolved); from 1657 one continuous unified capital fund, giving a stable financial foundation.
  • 1661, Charles II 60-85's Charter (after the Restoration) was arguably the most consequential legally: it turned the EIC from a commercial trading body into a quasi-State power. It gave the right to build forts, command troops, make war and peace, and exercise civil and criminal jurisdiction over settlements in India. (The right to mint its own money came in 1676.)
  • From 1793 Parliament takes over: the British Parliament begins passing Acts for the renewal of the EIC's Charter, mirroring the democratisation of Britain (power moving from Crown to elected representatives). Indian constitutional history and British political history are inseparable threads.
Remember

Takeaway (1661): the 1600 EIC was a merchant association with a trade monopoly; the 1661 EIC had the legal powers of a State within its territories. A trading company had, through successive Charters, accumulated the powers of a sovereign.

9 · The Danish East India Company#

  • Established 1616, out of envy of Dutch and English profits. Its Charter was issued by King Christian IV 88-48 of Denmark-Norway, granting a 12-year monopoly on trade between Denmark-Norway and the East Indies.
  • The Danes were never in the same league: weaker finance, limited military capacity. The first Danish company ended around 1650; a second Danish EIC was set up in 1670 but wound down gradually, selling all its Indian mainland settlements to the British EIC by 1845.

Danish settlements in India

  1. 1620: Tranquebar (Tharangambadi), Tamil Nadu: first factory; first fort: Fort Dansborg; official administrative HQ of Danish India from 1620 until 1845.
  2. 1624: Pondicherry (brief): under Roland Crappe; a short-lived factory, abandoned for lack of resources, long before the French arrived.
  3. 1625: Masulipatnam (Andhra Pradesh); Pipli and Balasore (Odisha).
  4. 1755: Serampore (Srirampur / Frederiksnagore), Hooghly district, Bengal: a parwana from Nawab Alivardi Khan 40-56. Tranquebar remained the HQ, but Serampore became the most commercially successful and culturally significant Danish post.
  • Colonial powers in Tranquebar, Serampore and the Nicobar Islands (the Nicobars were sold separately to the British in 1868; today part of India's Andaman and Nicobar Islands UT).
  • 1799-1800: Serampore Mission Press, associated with Baptist missionaries including William Carey: translated the Bible into Indian vernacular languages, ran schools, and made an immense contribution to early printing and education in Bengal. Their architectural legacy at Tranquebar and Serampore and their missionary work (not forced conversions) are the most visible Danish traces.

10 · How the EIC broke Portuguese dominance: Hawkins, Swally and Surat#

  • 1608: the EIC decided to set up a factory at Surat, then the most important port on India's west coast and firmly within Portuguese-dominated territory. It sent Captain William Hawkins to the court of Jahangir 05-27. Jahangir 05-27 first gave Hawkins a Mansab (rank of 400) and a Jagir, but Hawkins was ultimately expelled from the Mughal court due to persistent Portuguese pressure.
  • The lesson: Portugal had to be defeated militarily before the EIC could establish itself on the west coast.
  • 1612, Battle of Swally (Suvali): Captain Thomas Best led a small EIC armed trading fleet to a decisive victory over the Portuguese. The Mughal State had its own reason to welcome this: Mughal ships traded in the Arabian Sea but were perpetually vulnerable to Portuguese piracy, a source of humiliation for a great land power. Impressed by English sea capability, Jahangir 05-27 issued a Royal Farman in 1613, giving the EIC the legal right to set up a permanent factory at Surat.
Remember

1612 versus 1613: 1612 = the Battle of Swally (military victory) and the local Mughal governor's temporary permission/parwana; 1613 = the legally permanent establishment, when Jahangir 05-27 ratified the local agreement with an imperial farman.

11 · The Royal Farman of 1618 and Thomas Roe (1615-19)#

  • England wanted liberal trading rights across the entire Mughal Empire, so King James I 03-25 sent Thomas Roe as formal ambassador to Jahangir 05-27's court. Roe arrived at Surat in 1615 and stayed until 1619.
  • Jahangir 05-27 refused a formal treaty between equals (a trading company was no sovereign equal). Through sustained diplomacy and personal rapport Roe got a series of privileges and a Royal Farman (1618) allowing the EIC to set up factories throughout the Mughal Empire with liberal trading rights: lower customs duties and ease of doing business.
  • 1616-19: new factories at Agra, Ahmedabad and Broach (Bharuch). With Surat they formed the Surat Presidency (1618): the EIC's first administrative Presidency in India, coordinating English trade on the west coast until the later shift to Bombay (1687).
  • Portuguese enraged; naval conflicts continued. 1631-32: Shahjahan 27-58 ordered his Bengal governor Qasim Khan to defeat the Portuguese and eject them from Hugli, because they were misusing their trade privileges with piracy and the slave trade from their Bengal base.
Note

Roe's account, The Embassy of Sir Thomas Roe to the Court of the Great Mogul, is one of the most important primary sources on the Mughal Empire of this period (court administration, wealth, politics, daily life).

12 · Anglo-Portuguese Treaty of 1661: dowry diplomacy#

  • The treaty, signed at Whitehall, London, marked the marriage of King Charles II 60-85 of England and Princess Catherine of Braganza of Portugal.
  • 1. Portugal paid a dowry of 500,000 pounds in cash.
  • 2. England received Bombay and Tangier (Morocco).
  • 3. Trade and military cooperation, with England agreeing to protect Portugal's remaining possessions.
  • Result: a continued Portuguese presence in Goa, Daman and Diu under English protection, lasting until 1961, when independent India took over these territories militarily, after a people's movement.
  • 1668: the British Crown transferred Bombay to the EIC at an annual rent of 10 pounds: the start of Bombay's journey from a Portuguese dowry to the commercial capital of British India.

13 · The Dutch: rise, dominance and fall in Asia#

  • 1623, the Amboyna Massacre: the Dutch killed English traders in the spice islands, a brutal episode that ended English ambitions in Indonesia; the Dutch had effectively removed both the English and the Portuguese from Indonesia.
  • 1658: the Dutch removed the Portuguese from Ceylon.
  • Anglo-Dutch wars 1654-67, settled in the Treaty of Breda (1667): (1) the British leave Indonesia to the Dutch; (2) the Dutch leave the English alone in India; (3) both keep minor presences in each other's primary domains.
  • Cochin: captured from the Portuguese in 1663, held as HQ of Dutch Malabar (not the overall HQ).

  2. 1759, Battle of Bedara (also Battle of Chinsura): the British EIC defeated the Dutch in Bengal, crushing their remaining ambitions in India.

14 · Napoleon's link: the Kew Letters (1795)#

  • 1795, during the French Revolutionary Wars (1792-1804) leading into the Napoleonic Wars (1804-15): the French army invaded the Netherlands, overthrew the Dutch government and set up a puppet State, the Batavian Republic, controlled by France.
  • Britain declared all Dutch overseas colonies enemy territory. The exiled Dutch leader Prince William V 51-95 had fled to London and issued the "Kew Letters": orders telling Dutch colonial governors to hand over their territories to the British "for safekeeping," so France could not seize them. The British used this as their legal basis to take over the remaining Dutch posts.
  • By 1795 the British occupied the remaining Dutch settlements in South Asia: Cochin (Kerala), Chinsura (Bengal), Pulicat and Tuticorin (Coromandel), and the coastal regions of Ceylon (Trincomalee and Colombo).

15 · Why Ceylon did not become part of British India: the Crown Colony#

  • 1795-96: the British captured the Dutch areas of Ceylon and gave administration to the EIC, through the Madras Presidency.
  • Treaty of Amiens, 1802: a peace treaty in Europe between Britain and France along with its Dutch ally, the Batavian Republic. The Dutch formally and permanently ceded the coastal regions of Ceylon to Britain.
  • The British government decided Ceylon was too strategically important to be run by a trading company: it removed the EIC's administration entirely and declared Ceylon a British Crown Colony, ruled directly by an appointed British Governor, separate from the EIC's Indian empire.
Remember

Takeaway: India was a colony ruled by the EIC (a commercial entity with State-like powers); Ceylon was a Crown Colony where the British State itself governed directly.

16 · What were Presidencies?#

  • A Presidency was a regional headquarters of EIC trade. A settlement got Presidency status when it had grown large enough with significant warehouses, offices, residential buildings and a fort.
  • Initially small coastal towns; as the EIC annexed surrounding territories the towns grew into cities and the Presidencies expanded into provinces of British India (governed directly by the British). The Presidencies were named after the major forts built within them.

17 · How each of the three Presidencies came to be#

17.1 · Madras Presidency (from 1652/1653)

  • The EIC's first factory in India was a temporary one at Masulipatnam (Machilipatnam) in the Northern Sarkars (Andhra Pradesh) in 1611. (Northern Sarkars = the northern coastal districts of Andhra Pradesh, outside Mughal direct control.)
  • 1639: the EIC, led by Francis Day, purchased land at the village of Madraspatanam from Hindu Nayakas (provincial governors of the Vijayanagara Empire, 1336-1646) and built Fort St George (1639-44): the first fort built by the EIC in India and the anchor of the first permanent settlement.
  • 1690: the EIC purchased a fort at Cuddalore (south of Madras) from the Maratha Chhatrapati Rajaram 89-00 and named it Fort Saint David. Its history: partly built by the Dutch from 1608; construction halted due to Portuguese pressure on the Vijayanagara Empire; a Hindu merchant eventually completed it. In 1677 Shivaji 74-80 conquered the nearby Jinji fort and region including Cuddalore before it passed to the EIC.
  • The Madras Presidency was formally established as the Presidency of Fort St George in 1652, on the orders of the Court of Directors in London; because of the ship travel time, it was implemented in India in 1653. (Both dates appear in sources: the London order versus the on-ground implementation.)

17.2 · Bombay Presidency (1687)

  • From 1613 to 1687 Surat was the EIC's main trade centre on the west coast. The shift happened because of the growing power of the Marathas in Gujarat, making Surat increasingly risky. Bombay, a cluster of seven islands, offered a more defensible location.
  • Charles II 60-85 received Bombay in 1661 as part of the Portuguese dowry and transferred it to the EIC in 1668 at 10 pounds per annum. The Bombay Presidency was established in 1687, replacing Surat as the western HQ. Bombay Castle (Casa da Orta) was originally built by the Portuguese and expanded by the EIC through the 18th century.

17.3 · Calcutta Presidency (from 1699/1700)

  • The EIC's Bengal presence began with a factory at Hugli in 1651, permitted by a nishan from Shah Shuja, the Mughal governor of Bengal and son of Shahjahan 27-58.
  • 1690: the EIC founded a settlement at the village of Sutanuti: the Calcutta establishment.
  • The Calcutta Presidency was formally established in 1699/1700 as the Presidency of Fort William, with its own Governor.
  • Through the 18th century the EIC developed Calcutta Port; Calcutta came to account for 60% of all EIC trade from India. 1800: Fort William College was established by Wellesley 98-05 for training EIC civil servants. 1870: the Calcutta Port Act created the Calcutta Port Commissioners and Port Trust, the first formal legal authority governing the port.
  • Covered later (in the chapter on the Battle of Plassey): Child's War, the 1690 pardon and 1691 Farman, and the 1717 Farman.

19 · The French in India and the story of Pondicherry#

  • The French East India Company (Compagnie des Indes Orientales) was founded in 1664 by Jean-Baptiste Colbert under King Louis XIV 43-15; great-grandfather of Louis XVI, overthrown in the French Revolution of 1789).
  • It started as a private company but was later nationalised, making it in today's terms a Public Sector Undertaking (PSU).

French settlements in India (handout list)

  1. 1668, Surat: first factory, on a farman given by Aurangzeb 58-07 in 1667.
  2. 1669, Masulipatnam: farman from the Sultan of Golconda (1518-1687).
  3. 1673, Chandarnagar: permission from Shaista Khan 64-88, Mughal governor of Bengal; financial crunch delayed it: a settlement only in 1690-92.
  4. 1674, Pondicherry: acquired from Sikandar Adil Shah (1672-86) of Bijapur and his local governor Sher Khan Lodi. Developed as a city by Francois Martin 01-06 (Governor-General 1701-06, earlier Governor), the real founder of French power in India: centralised French administration with Pondicherry as capital (1701) and built Fort Louis.
  5. 1723, Yanam: permission from Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48, also called Asaf Jah I.
  6. 1724, Mahe (Malabar coast): acquired from the Nair ruler Vazhunnavar in 1721; trade settlement built 1724-25 after overcoming a rebellion; named after Mahe de La Bourdonnais 35-46, who came with a fleet from Mauritius.
  7. 1739, Karaikal: granted by Pratap Singh 39-63, Maratha ruler of Tanjore, as a reward for French help in a succession dispute. (The Thanjavur Maratha dynasty was founded not by Shivaji 74-80 directly but by his half-brother Venkoji (Ekoji) in 1674, defeating the Nayaka rulers of Madurai.)
  8. 1721, Mauritius a French colony, but proper control only by 1735 under Mahe de La Bourdonnais 35-46 (Governor 1735-46). He came again with a fleet in 1746 and helped Dupleix 42-54 capture Madras in the 1st Carnatic War (1746-48).
  9. 1949: a referendum in Chandernagore merged it into India; the rest stayed under French control until 1954.

Recall sheet#

Read once, hide it, then rebuild the chapter from memory.

  1. Why Europe wanted Asia: spices (Indonesia), textiles (India), silk and tea (China). Arabs then Genoa and Venice, profit at each handoff. Da Gama's 1498 cargo: 800% profit; clove = gold. Spices preserved winter meat. Later Indian cotton took over.
  2. 1453 Ottoman blockade (effectively) then Age of Discovery: astrolabe, compass, latitude and longitude, geography, Caravel.
  3. Tordesillas 1494: Pope Alexander VI; 1,185 miles west of Cape Verde; east Portugal, west Spain; Brazil's eastern tip.
  4. Da Gama 1498 at Calicut (Zamorin), not Cochin; no factory. Cabral 1500: Calicut factory destroyed; Cochin first permanent factory. 1502 Calicut bombarded. 1503 Fort Manuel, Cochin.
  5. Depot (storage), factory (office, factors, farmans), settlement (LEJ, taxes). Portuguese trade = State enterprise (Estado da India). Farman (King), Parwana (Governor), Nishan (Prince).
  6. Almeida 05-09: Blue Water, Cartaz, Diu 1509 (Gujarat, Mamluks, Zamorin; Ottomans and Venice). Albuquerque 09-15: Goa 1510, Luso-Indian, Sati regulation, Christianity as control. Nuno da Cunha 29-38: Goa 1530, Bassein 1534, Diu 1535.
  7. Inquisition 1560-1812; Xavier died at Shangchuan Island 1552 (Prelims 2021); body in Bom Jesus, Goa. New World crops: tobacco, red chillies, papaya, pineapple, guava (cocoa and rubber came with the British).
  8. Decline: Marathas (1739), Inquisition backlash, corruption and piracy, English and Dutch, overextension (Brazil).
  9. Armada 1588; Portugal-Spain union 1580. Triangle trade England, India, Indonesia.
  10. VOC 1602: first multinational and first joint-stock; Masulipatnam 1605; Pulicat 1610; HQ Pulicat then Nagapattinam; Cochin = Malabar HQ only; Chinsura = Bengal HQ; Gajapati trap (Prelims 2022); Colachel 1741, Bedara 1759, Kew 1795, dissolved 1799.
  11. EIC: 31 Dec 1600 Charter of Elizabeth I 58-03 (15 years, 215 merchants, 4th joint-stock). Lancaster; 1609 perpetual; 1657 Cromwell 51-60 permanent joint-stock; 1661 quasi-State; 1793 Parliament.
  12. Hawkins; Swally 1612 (Best); 1613 Surat farman (1612 versus 1613). Roe 1615-19: 1618 farman; Surat Presidency. Bombay dowry 1661; leased 1668.
  13. Presidencies: Madras 1652/53 (Fort St George, Francis Day 1639), Bombay 1687, Calcutta 1699/1700 (Fort William). Ceylon: Amiens 1802, Crown Colony.
  14. Danes: 1616, Tranquebar 1620, Serampore 1755, sold 1845. French: 1664 Colbert; Surat 1668; Pondicherry 1674 (Martin); Yanam 1723; Mahe 1724; Karaikal 1739; Chandernagore 1949; 1954.
