Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • The relation between the Carnatic and Hyderabad (Prelims 2021): the Carnatic was the southern part of the Hyderabad Subah, not a separate independent State
  • Three classes of regional State: Successor (Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad), Rebel (Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, Afghans), Already Autonomous (Rajputs, Mysore, Travancore)
  • Founders and years: MQK 17-27 (1717), Sadat Khan 22-39 (1722), Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48 (1724); de facto sovereign, none declared independence
  • Provincial offices: Subahdar (Nizamat, the Sword), Diwan (Diwani, the Purse), Bakshi (audit and intelligence); who reported to whom
  • The five-step recipe of a successor State; Tankha versus Vatan Jagirs; Bengal's 15 Zamindars and 50% of LR; the 1751 Chauth settlement; Safdar Jung 39-54 and Farukhabad

Mains hooks

  • No Mains PYQ is mapped here; the teacher marks Chapters 2 to 7 as very little for Mains and less for Prelims
  • What to prepare: the five-step recipe by which a province became autonomous without declaring independence
  • Why the founders stayed 'autonomous but never independent' (legitimacy, Delhi ambitions, military help, trade)
  • The India the EIC stepped into: competing, partially autonomous States, with legitimacy and real power come apart

Governors turned Mughal provinces into States by taking over the Sword, the Purse and the Eyes, while keeping the emperor's name as a shield.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: The emperor's own governors took his Sword, Purse and Eyes into one hand through a five-step recipe, yet kept his name on top, so Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad became real States on paper still Mughal. That gap between paper and power is what the EIC stepped into.

  1. Control is not rule. The teacher opens with a warning: control can exist without rule (absentee colonialism, like Angola and Mozambique, where raw materials leave and few people live on the ground). Rule means doing L, E, J, making and implementing laws, plus governance of health, education and infrastructure. For the EIC in Bengal, Plassey 1757 was control and Buxar 1764 was rule.
    → But what kind of India was the EIC stepping into?
  2. A patchwork. By the mid-18th century the Mughal State no longer worked as one unified polity. Power decentralised from 1707 to 1800 into three classes of State: Successor, Rebel and Already Autonomous. The teacher lists the later emperors as proof of decline, circling FS 13-19 and Md Shah 19-48 as the reigns in which the Successor States arose.
    → Now to the first class: the governors who stabbed the emperor in the back.
  3. Three founders, three years. Bengal was set up by Murshid Quli Khan (MQK 17-27) in 1717, Awadh by Sadat Khan 22-39 in 1722, Hyderabad by Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48 in 1724. Each ruled de facto as sovereign, yet none declared independence. Four reasons: symbolic legitimacy, ambitions at Delhi (Nizam was Wazir 1722-24 and Mir Bakshi 1739-40), military assistance and trade barriers.
    → So how did a mere governor get so much power?
  4. Sword, Purse, Eyes. Akbar 56-05's system split a province in three. The Subahdar (Nizamat) held the Sword and reported to the Emperor; the Diwan (Diwani) held the Purse and reported to the Imperial Diwan; the Bakshi held military audit and intelligence and reported to the Mir Bakshi. The Diwan and the Bakshi both bypassed the Nazim: checks and balances.
    → But every founder had to break exactly this design.
  5. The recipe. Extract the Subahdari, end the checks and balances with personal loyalists, build revenue strength (field survey, efficient LR system), co-opt the men of money (merchants, bankers and Ijaradars given Mansabs) and convert Tankha Jagirs into Vatan Jagirs. The result is a diffused autonomous polity: a Nawab on top of a network of powerful actors, with the Mughal link kept as a symbol.
    → Bengal shows the recipe at its clearest.
  6. Bengal: two hats and fifteen friends. In 1717 MQK 17-27 became Nazim while already Diwan, ending Akbar 56-05's checks. His field surveys and intermediary Zamindars paid off: by 1727 just fifteen collected 50% of Bengal Suba's LR. The Jagat Seths, a hereditary title of a banking family, funded it all. Proof of diffusion: in 1740 Alivardi Khan 40-56 toppled Sarfaraz Khan with a Jagat Seth's backing and bought the emperor's approval with a promised crore.
    → Then an outside shock deepened the break.
  7. Bengal breaks away. From 1745 to 1751 Raghuji Bhonsle of Nagpur raided Bengal and Alivardi could not beat him. The 1751 settlement gave Bhonsle the Chauth (one-fourth of revenues) of Bengal and Bihar plus direct control of Orissa. Alivardi stopped sending revenue to Delhi and stopped seeking approval for appointments. After him came Siraj 56-57, Mir Jafar 57-60, Mir Qasim 60-63, Mir Jafar again, and in 1765 the Diwani passed to the EIC.
    → Awadh followed its own road to the same place.
  8. Awadh: Sadat, Safdar, Shuja. Sadat Khan 22-39 became Subahdar in 1722 as a reward for crushing revolts and kept ambitions at the centre. Passed over for Mir Bakshi, he convinced Nadir Shah to invade Delhi in 1739, and died by suicide. Safdar Jung 39-54, Wazir in 1748, annexed Farukhabad and was forced to resign in 1753: Awadh's virtual break. Shuja 54-75 fought at Panipat (1761) and lost at Buxar (1764); Awadh was annexed in 1856.
    → In the south, the Nizam had gone home for good.
  9. Hyderabad: the Nizam goes home. Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48 (Chin Qulich Khan, Asaf Jah I) led the Turani group, helped remove the Sayyid Brothers in 1720, and took Hyderabad in 1724 after beating Mubariz Khan. The Marathas beat him in 1728 and 1738. After serving as Mir Bakshi (1739-40) he gave up on the centre. Hyderabad kept the emperor's name on coins and the Khutba, tolerated its Hindu majority, and converted Tankha to Vatan Jagirs most aggressively.
    → And inside Hyderabad lay a smaller State of its own.
  10. The Carnatic: a tenant downstairs. The Hyderabad Subah had two parts: Hyderabad north of the Krishna, and the Carnatic (Hyderabad-Karnatic), twelve districts south with capital Gingee and its own Nawab who reported to the Nizam. Saadatullah Khan (1710-32) moved the capital to Arcot and made the post hereditary; Dost Ali Khan (1732-40) was killed by the Marathas; Anwar-ud-din Khan (from 1744) was the Nizam's pick but became autonomous too. This is the Prelims 2021 trap.
    → Put the three States side by side and the pattern shows.
  11. The gap the EIC stepped into. Across all three, a powerful Mansabdar concentrated power, none declared independence, the Carnatic was a successor State inside a successor State, and the emperor became a constitutional monarch with no executive power. Legitimacy and real power had come apart, which made the subcontinent vulnerable to outside penetration.

In simple words#

  • By the mid-18th century the Mughal State was a patchwork of regional powers: Successor, Rebel and Already Autonomous States.
  • Bengal (1717), Awadh (1722) and Hyderabad (1724) were built by powerful Mansabdars who ruled de facto as sovereign but never declared independence.
  • Akbar 56-05 kept a province safe with checks and balances (Subahdar, Diwan and Bakshi each reported to a different officer at Delhi). The founders ended the checks and used a five-step recipe.
  • The result was a diffused autonomous polity:
    • A Nawab on top of a network of Zamindars, merchants and bankers, not an absolute ruler. Bengal and Awadh made a virtual break (1751 and 1753)
    • Hyderabad's Nizam moved south for good after 1740
  • Inside Hyderabad lay the Carnatic, a state within a state. The emperor became a constitutional monarch with no executive power: legitimacy and real power had come apart, which let the EIC step in.

Teacher's board
  • Akbar 56-05's Mansabdari system = checks and balances in any province.
  • Nazim / Subadar = gun power (military head).
  • Diwan = political + finance power (CM + FM). Bakshi = intelligence power.
  • Each reports to a different officer at Delhi, all under the King.
Key takeaway
  • To end the emperor's authority in a province:
    • End the checks and balances
    • Concentrate all power in yourself or your supporters.
  • This is the recipe in Section 4.
Teacher's board
  • Mansabdar is the general term (like "animal")
  • Mir Bakshi, Nazim, Naib Nazim are particular posts (like lion, tiger, cheetah). Naib = Deputy.
  • Nazim = Subadar = Nawab of Suba = Governor.
  • "Nizam" was the title of the Nazim of the Hyderabad Suba.
Handout alert

Nawab ≠ Subahdar always. A Nawab could run a sub-region within a Suba: Alivardi Khan 40-56 was Nawab of Patna (inside the Bengal Subah) / Naib Nazim of Bihar before becoming Subahdar of all Bengal in 1740. Naib Nazim = Deputy Subahdar.

Key takeaway
  • The Diwan did not report to the Subahdar but directly to the Imperial Diwan at Delhi.
  • Akbar 56-05 made the two co-equal.
  • This separation of the Sword (Nizamat) from the Purse (Diwani) kept the Mughal Empire stable for over a century.
  • Every successor State founder had to dismantle it.

3.1 Geographical administration of the Bengal Subah (handout tables)

  • Territory: Bengal Subah (West Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, East Bengal). Capital: Murshidabad (in West Bengal).
  • Nizamat (Home Minister: law and order, criminal justice, defence / war commander) = Nazim (Subahdar), directly answerable to the Emperor.
  • Diwani (revenue, civil administration, civil justice) = Diwan, directly answerable to the Emperor (under the supervision of the Imperial Diwan, i.e. the Wazir).
  • Parallel audit and intelligence officer = Bakshi of the Subah, reports directly to the Mir Bakshi at Delhi, bypassing the Nazim.
Key takeaway
  • The Nazim, the Diwan and the Bakshi were mutually independent. None could overrule the others.
  • All three reported only to their imperial counterparts at Delhi.

4 · How all three successor States were built: the recipe#

Story link

Because every founder had to break the design of the last section, a five-step recipe appears. Ask: which step ends the checks, and which buys the men of money?

The recipe for a successor State

  • 1 Extract the Subahdari: take the Mansab of Subahdar from the emperor, who depended on powerful Mansabdars (their army) to stay in power
  • 2 End checks and balances: fill the Diwan, Naib Nazim and war commander posts with personal loyalists
  • 3 Build revenue strength:
    • Strong revenue administration
    • Field survey, efficient LR system
    • Finance is crucial for everything else
  • 4 Co-opt the men of money: merchants, bankers and Ijaradars get Mansabs, fusing wealth with political power (teacher: intermediary Zamindars)
  • 5 Convert Tankha Jagirs into Vatan Jagirs: hereditary, for the loyalty of other powerful Mansabdars in the province
  • Result: a diffused autonomous polity: a Nawab on top of a network of powerful actors, with the Mughal link kept as a symbol
Mnemonic · The five-step recipe, in order
EveryEmpireBows,Cracks,Crumbles
EExtract the SubahdariEEnd checks and balances (personal loyalists)BBuild revenue strength (field survey, efficient LR system)CCo-opt the men of money (merchants, bankers, Ijaradars get Mansabs)CConvert Tankha Jagirs into Vatan Jagirs
Order kept: the steps run in this sequence. The empire bows to its governors, cracks, and crumbles.
  • Step 4 in the handout: convert Tankha Jagirs (temporary, transferable) into Vatan Jagirs (hereditary). Vatan = hereditary. The emperor's historic tool of control (shifting Jagirs at will) was traded away for local loyalty of provincial Mansabdars to the regional ruler.
  • Step 5 in the handout (co-opting): intermediary Zamindars, revenue farmers, merchants, moneylenders and bankers were promised security of property and reasonable taxes and, most unprecedentedly, given Mansabs.
  • The Mansabdari system was traditionally lineage-based (military / noble bloodlines only).
  • Appointing merchants and bankers to Mansabs was a structural transformation: administrative rank and commercial wealth, historically kept apart, now merged.
  • All the steps were taken while accepting the symbolic sovereignty of the Mughal Emperor.
Handout note
  • Count check. Handout heading says "four-step process", its text lists Steps 1 to 5, its diagram shows six steps (adds "keep the Mughal symbolic link").
  • The slide has five steps plus the result.
  • Learn the recipe, not the number.

5 · Bengal: Murshid Quli Khan (MQK, 1717–27)#

Story link

Because the recipe worked first in Bengal, watch MQK wear two hats. Ask: whose money makes the Zamindars loyal?

  • Bengal Subah = Bengal, Orissa and Bihar: vast and commercially rich.
  • Capital: Murshidabad, on the banks of the Hooghly, then the major port (the British later developed Calcutta as an alternative).
  • MQK was the last Bengal Governor appointed as per the wish of the Emperor.
  • His path (handout):
    • Diwan from 1700
    • Under Farukhsiyar (1713–19) he also became Deputy Governor of Bengal Suba and Governor of Orissa.
  • In 1717, when elevated to the Mansab of Nazim of Bengal Subah, he already was Diwan (combining finance and executive in one man = ending Akbar 56-05's checks and balances).
  • He still sent tribute to Delhi, but the emperor's approval for appointments in Bengal Suba was now a paper formality.
  • Revenue: brilliant administrator. Collected LR through intermediary Zamindars and got detailed field surveys done for fair assessment.
  • The intermediaries grew powerful: by 1727 just fifteen were collecting 50% of Bengal Suba's LR.
  • Coercion:
    • Mansabdars who resisted had their Tankha Jagirs in Bengal taken and were given Jagirs in Orissa
    • resisting Zamindars were punished by reducing the land from which they collected LR.
Teacher's board
  • MQK's rise = finance + executive + military power in one man.
  • Field surveys gave a "justified / right LR demand." LR chain: ryots → intermediary Zamindars (I2) → Diwan of Suba → Imperial Diwan, money going to the King or to a Jagirdar (J).
  • "Co-opting" powerful people = the 15 Zamindars.

6 · The Jagat Seths: why Bengal's power was diffused, not centralised#

Story link

Because the Nawab's power rested on a network, the bankers matter. Ask: what does the 1740 coup prove about who really chose the Nawab?

  • "Jagat Seth" was a hereditary title, not a personal name, held by successive heads of a banking family: Manikchand under MQK
  • Mehtab Rai and Swarup Chand under Alivardi Khan 40-56.
  • Already powerful before 1717, under the successor State they rose to what Dutch records called the richest banking house in the world.
  • They loaned to Zamindars (so revenue reached the State on time), loaned to merchants, and ran Hundi services (pre-modern wire transfer).
  • Under Shuja-ud-din Md Khan (1727–39) a Jagat Seth was appointed Treasurer of Bengal and Daroga of the Royal Mint: central bank plus the Nawab's personal banker in one family.
Key takeaway

Power was diffused, not centralised: a network of Nawab + intermediary Zamindars + merchants + bankers, with the Nawab on top but dependent on their cooperation.

Key takeaway
  • Proof: the coup of 1740. Alivardi Khan 40-56 (Nawab of Patna) overthrew and killed the sitting Nawab Sarfaraz Khan (1739–40) with the Jagat Seth's backing.
  • He secured formal approval by promising Emperor Md Shah one crore rupees plus Sarfaraz's personal wealth, and approval was duly given. The emperor had been reduced to a transactional figure.

7 · Bengal's virtual break: the Maratha raids and Alivardi Khan 40-56#

Story link

Because an outside raider took a share of the revenue, Bengal stopped paying Delhi. Ask: which three provinces does Nagpur pull from?

  • Under MQK, Bengal was autonomous but tribute still was sent to Delhi.
  • Under Alivardi the break deepened, triggered by an external shock.

The virtual break of Bengal

  • 1745-51:
    • Raghuji Bhonsle of Nagpur raids Bengal repeatedly
    • Alivardi cannot defeat him

  • Part of the revenue now flows to Nagpur
  • Alivardi stops sending revenue to Delhi and stops seeking the emperor's approval for provincial Mansabdar appointments
  • Virtual break complete, but independence never declared

8 · Awadh: from Sadat Khan 22-39 to the virtual break of 1753#

Story link

Because Awadh's founders kept their eyes on Delhi, its break came through a Wazir's misstep. Ask: who is Sadat, who is Safdar, who is Shuja?

  • Founder: Sadat Ali Khan (1722–39), appointed Subahdar in 1722 as a reward for suppressing local revolts.
  • He installed his nephew and son-in-law Safdar Jung 39-54 as Deputy Governor (Naib Nazim) and his own loyalist as Diwan
  • tribute to Delhi was reduced.
  • Revenue grew from efficient land assessment and taxes on trade.
  • He created a new loyal class of local elites (Indian Muslims, Afghans, Hindus) by giving them Mansabs and Jagirs.
  • Unlike MQK, Sadat Khan 22-39 kept ambitions at the centre: he wanted a senior Mansab at Delhi (the Central Government).
  • He convinced Nadir Shah to invade Delhi in 1739 because the Mir Bakshi post had gone to Chin Qulich Khan (another name of Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48) instead of him.
  • Nadir Shah ill-treated him after the victory
  • He committed suicide.
  • Safdar Jung 39-54 was appointed Wazir by Ahmad Shah Bahadur (1748–54) in 1748.
  • He misused the office to annex Farukhabad into Awadh.
  • Other Mansabdars opposed, and in 1753 he was forced to resign.
  • The resignation was Awadh's virtual break: no more revenue to Delhi, no imperial authority inside Awadh, though legally still part of the empire.
  • Shuja-ud-Daula 54-75 was appointed Wazir by Shah Alam II 60-06.
  • He fought alongside Ahmad Shah Abdali 47-72 at the Third Battle of Panipat (1761) and was later defeated by the EIC at the Battle of Buxar (1764).
  • Awadh survived nominally until EIC annexation in 1856 from Nawab Wajid Ali Shah.
Handout note
  • Discrepancy to check. The slide calls Safdar Jung 39-54 the nephew of Sadat Khan 22-39
  • The handout calls him nephew and son-in-law.
  • The handout in Chapter 2 also calls Sadat Khan 22-39 "of Irani group" in the Karnal passage.

9 · Hyderabad: Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48's path from Delhi to the Deccan#

Story link

Because the Nizam failed at the centre, he turned south. Ask: which two posts did he hold at Delhi, and when did he give up?

  • Hyderabad was set up within the empire in 1724 by Chin Qulich Khan, aka Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48, aka Asaf Jah I (Nizam, 1724–48), after defeating Mubariz Khan, the sitting Nazim of the Deccan.
  • He was the leader of the Turani group.
  • In Aurangzeb 58-07's Deccan wars he and his father built strong roots in the region.
  • 1720: the Turani and Irani groups removed the Sayyid Brothers, kingmakers who controlled Md Shah, and formally restored imperial authority.
  • But the structural problem remained: Md Shah was uninterested in governance. (Remember: 5% of Mansabdars controlled 61% of all Jagir land revenue.)
  • As Wazir (1722–24) Nizam tried to redistribute Jagirs from big Mansabdars to smaller ones to ease the Jagirdari crisis, but the bigger Mansabdars did not agree.
  • The Marathas defeated him in 1728 and 1738, forcing him to pay Chauth and Sardeshmukhi (10% hereditary Maratha claim in the Deccan).
  • He served as Mir Bakshi (1739–40) during Nadir Shah's invasion.
  • By 1740 he gave up on reforming the centre and moved to Hyderabad permanently.

10 · How Nizam established Hyderabad as a successor State#

Story link

Because Hyderabad moved furthest from the Mughal system, it shows the recipe at its most aggressive. Ask: what are the three marks of this State?

AspectDetail
Symbolic sovereignty (by 1748)The emperor's name on coins and in the Friday Khutba, but zero influence over war, peace, treaties or appointments
ToleranceThe Hindu majority was economically powerful, so Nizam followed religious tolerance and punished opponents regardless of community
Most aggressive changeOf the three States, Hyderabad converted Tankha to Vatan Jagirs most aggressively and made merchants, moneylenders and Ijaradars Mansabdars ("upstarts") on the largest scale
Key takeaway
  • Merchants and moneylenders got property security and became Mansabdars (called upstarts), and in return gave financial support.
  • The most pronounced transformation of the Mansabdari system was in Hyderabad.
  • Result: the same diffused polity: Nawab at top, influential sections under him.

11 · The Carnatic: a State within a State#

Story link

Because Hyderabad had a smaller State inside it, the Prelims 2021 trap lives here. Ask: is the Carnatic independent, or the southern part of the Hyderabad Subah?

  • Hyderabad had a smaller State inside its borders, the Carnatic. By law it was part of the Hyderabad province.
  • But it had its own ruler, the Nawab, and ran its own government.
Teacher's board

Parallels for the Carnatic: India → J&K (Art. 370); Assam → Meghalaya as an autonomous State within Assam (Art. 244A); Hyderabad → Carnatic, practically autonomous.

11.1 Backstory: the Vijayanagar Empire (1336–1646)

  • For three hundred years the Vijayanagar Empire was the strongest power in South India, and the Carnatic was part of it.
  • Provinces were ruled by Nayaks (teacher: Nayak = CM), Telugu Hindu governors from warrior families.
  • Nayaks divided their provinces into Palayams, each run by a local chief called a Poligar (Palayakkarar) with his own private army and fort.

11.2 The long decline (1565–1698)

  • 1. A. Defeat of Vijayanagar at Talikota (1565).
  • A four-state alliance of the Deccan Sultanates (Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, Bidar) destroyed the Vijayanagar army.
  • The empire officially continued until 1646, but this defeat was the real end of its power.
  • 2. B. Nayaks independent, then defeated. After 1565 the southern Nayaks of Madurai, Tanjore and Gingee ruled autonomously
  • Distance protected them for a while
  • By 1646 Bijapur and Golconda conquered and divided the Carnatic between themselves.
  • 3. C. Sultanates defeated by the Mughals.
  • Aurangzeb 58-07 conquered Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687).
  • The whole Carnatic now came under Mughal rule.
  • 4. D. Gingee under the Marathas, then lost to the Mughals.
  • Shivaji 74-80 captured Gingee Fort from Bijapur in 1677.
  • After the Mughals executed Sambhaji 80-89 in 1689, the next Chhatrapati, Rajaram 89-00, made Gingee his base against the Mughals.
  • In 1690 Zulfiqar Khan (Nawab of Carnatic) surrounded the fort.
  • The siege lasted eight years (the longest by the Mughals)
  • the Mughals finally captured Gingee in 1698. (Zulfiqar Khan later became Wazir under Jahandar Shah 12-13, 1712, until his execution in February 1713.)

12 · Synthesis: the common pattern and what it made possible#

Story link

Because all three States share one pattern, the EIC faced a gap between paper and power. Ask: what did the emperor become?

PointDetail
Concentration of powerAcross Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad (and the Carnatic): a powerful Mansabdar extracts the Subahdari, dismantles checks and balances, builds revenue strength, converts Tankha to Vatan, and co-opts merchants and bankers into the Mansabdari system
No independenceNone declared it: the Mughal umbrella served their interests; practically independent, including decisions of war and peace. Each developed its own diplomacy, army and fisc
Unique CarnaticPart of the Hyderabad Suba but autonomous: a successor State inside a successor State. Fragmentation was not only horizontal (Subahs breaking from the empire) but also vertical (sub-regions within a successor State developing their own semi-autonomous identity)
Mughal authorityThe emperor became a constitutional monarch with no executive power: a ceremonial head of State whose legitimacy was still useful to others
The virtual breaks, in order
Key takeaway
  • The India into which the EIC was stepping: competing, partially autonomous States, each with its own elite coalition and its own fiscal and military interests, and, critically, a world where legitimacy and real power had come apart.
  • That gap between formal authority and actual power is exactly what made the subcontinent vulnerable to outside penetration.
