0 · Control versus rule (the teacher's opening)#

Teacher's board

Control ≠ Rule. Control is possible without ruling (absentee colonialism, e.g. Angola, Mozambique: extract raw materials, few people on the ground). Rule = doing L, E, J (making laws, implementing them) plus governance of local health, education, infrastructure, run through IAS, IPS.

Teacher's board

EIC in Bengal: Battle of Plassey 1757 → control / conquest began. Battle of Buxar 1764 → rule began.

1 · Framework: the three classes of regional State#

By the mid-18th century the Mughal State had ceased to function as a single unified polity. What remained was a patchwork of regional powers.

ClassExamplesHow they arose
Successor StatesBengal, Awadh, HyderabadPowerful Mansabdars who extracted governing authority over a Subah from the emperor (this chapter)
Rebel StatesMarathas, Sikhs, Jats, Afghans (Farukhabad, Rohilkhand)Formed by those who openly rebelled against the empire
Already AutonomousRajputs, Mysore, TravancoreNever under effective Mughal control to begin with
Teacher's board

Successor states: "the Mughal emperor is stabbed in the back by his provincial governors / Subadars", who establish their own rule in B, A, H but never declared independence formally. Rebel states: they FOUGHT the Mughal State (boxed), took some territory, and established their own State on it. Already autonomous: "even when the Mughal was powerful, he did not rule here."

2 · Why autonomous but never formally independent?#

  • Bengal was set up as a successor state by Murshid Quli Khan (MQK, 1717–27) in 1717; Awadh by Sadat Khan 22-39 in 1722; Hyderabad by Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48 in 1724.
  • Each ruled de facto as sovereign; none declared independence.
Teacher's board

De facto = on-ground reality. De jure = on paper. All of these were Mansabdars, some of them ministers in the Central Govt and some ministers in the Provincial Govt. The State sketch: at the Centre, a Head of State with three branches; in each Province, a similar tree.

Note

The teacher's side column "EIC" lists 1757, 1764, 1798 against Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad. The slides and handout do not explain 1798. Check it against your own notes.

Four reasons (and the teacher's twist on each)
ReasonDetailTeacher's ink
Symbolic legitimacy (Social power)A Mughal Subahdar's title commanded the obedience of local noblesIf you declare independence, opponents may unite against you in the name of the emperor
Ambitions at Delhi (Political power)Prized Mansabs at the Centre: Wazir (PM / Imperial Diwan) and Mir Bakshi (War Paymaster). Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48 was Wazir 1722–24 while also Subahdar of Hyderabad, and became Mir Bakshi in 1739–40 while still Subahdar of HyderabadIndependent Hyderabad has no influence; Hyderabad with a minister at the Centre has some influence on the rest of the Mughal empire
Military assistance (Military power)Provinces could rely on each other's help in times of warIf independent, an attack on Hyderabad is no longer an attack on the Mughal empire, so the help is gone
Trade barriers (Economic power)Political independence could hurt trade, because tariffs may then apply. (Think EU members giving up partial sovereignty for open borders and trade.)Domestic trade is easier, cheaper, lower taxes

3 · Mughal provincial titles and the Bengal Subah's structure#

Bakshi of the SubahMilitary audit and intelligencethe Mir Bakshi at the Centre, bypassing the Nazim
The design (Akbar 56-05Checks and balances. Every successor State's founder dismantled itnone
Teacher's board

Akbar 56-05's Mansabdari system = checks and balances, in any province. Nazim / Subadar = gun power (the military head). Diwan = political power + finance power (think CM + FM). Bakshi = intelligence power. Each of the three reports to a different officer at Delhi, all under the King.

Remember

How to end the authority of the emperor in a province? End the checks and balances and concentrate all power in yourself or in your supporters. That is exactly the four-to-five-step recipe in Section 4.

Teacher's board

Mansabdar is the general term (like "animal"); Mir Bakshi, Nazim, Naib Nazim are particular posts (like lion, tiger, cheetah). Naib = Deputy. Nazim = Subadar = Nawab of Suba = Governor. Example: "Nizam" was the title of the Nazim of the Hyderabad Suba.

Handout alert

Nawab ≠ Subahdar always. A Nawab could administer a sub-region within a Suba: Alivardi Khan 40-56 was Nawab of Patna (a region inside the Bengal Subah) or Naib Nazim of Bihar before becoming Subahdar of the whole Bengal Subah in 1740. The Naib Nazim = Deputy Subahdar.

Remember

The Diwan did not report to the Subahdar. He reported directly to the Imperial Diwan at Delhi. Akbar 56-05 deliberately made the two co-equal. This separation of the Sword (Nizamat) from the Purse (Diwani) is what kept the Mughal Empire stable for over a century. Every successor State founder had to dismantle it.

3.1 Geographical administration of the Bengal Subah (handout tables)

  • Territory: Bengal Subah (West Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, East Bengal). Capital: Murshidabad (in West Bengal).
  • Nizamat (Home Minister: law and order, criminal justice, defence / war commander) = Nazim (Subahdar), directly answerable to the Emperor.
  • Diwani (revenue, civil administration, civil justice) = Diwan, directly answerable to the Emperor (under the supervision of the Imperial Diwan, i.e. the Wazir).
  • Parallel audit and intelligence officer = Bakshi of the Subah, reports directly to the Mir Bakshi at Delhi, bypassing the Nazim.
Remember

The Nazim, the Diwan and the Bakshi were mutually independent. None could overrule the others. All three reported only to their imperial counterparts at Delhi.

The Bakshi's four functions

#FunctionWhat it meant
1Mansabdari recordsWho holds what rank, who got promoted, demoted, joined or died; registers updated constantly
2Military payrollCalculating each Mansabdar's entitlement, assigning him a Jagir of the right value, transferring Jagirs every 2-3 years to prevent local roots, and in wartime controlling war expenditure
3Anti-corruption inspectionTwo mechanisms by Akbar 56-05: Dagh ("branding": every horse branded with an imperial mark on its flank; at inspection only branded horses counted) and Chehra ("face": each soldier's face, height, complexion and marks recorded in a register)
4Intelligence (waqia-navis)Information collectors in every province, district and significant town, sending daily reports to Delhi: court intrigues, market prices, harvest conditions, military movements, suspicious behaviour by the Nazim or Diwan, rebellions brewing, foreign travellers arriving. Reports flowed up through the Bakshi system, the Mir Bakshi being the chief recipient
Remember

The Bakshi knew who every noble was (records), what they were paid (payroll), whether they were honest (dagh-chehra) and what they were doing (intelligence). That is why he was the second-highest official in the Empire.

4 · How all three successor States were built: the recipe#

The recipe for a successor State

1 Extract the Subahdari: take the Mansab of Subahdar from the emperor, who depended on powerful Mansabdars (their army) to stay in power
2 End checks and balances: fill the Diwan, Naib Nazim and war commander posts with personal loyalists
3 Build revenue strength: strong revenue administration
field survey, efficient LR system; finance is crucial for everything else
4 Co-opt the men of money: merchants, bankers and Ijaradars get Mansabs, fusing wealth with political power (teacher: intermediary Zamindars)
5 Convert Tankha Jagirs into Vatan Jagirs: hereditary, for the loyalty of other powerful Mansabdars in the province
Result: a diffused autonomous polity: a Nawab on top of a network of powerful actors, with the Mughal link kept as a symbol
  • Step 4 in the handout: convert Tankha Jagirs (temporary, transferable) into Vatan Jagirs (hereditary). Vatan = hereditary. The emperor's historic tool of control (shifting Jagirs at will) was traded away for local loyalty of provincial Mansabdars to the regional ruler.
  • Step 5 in the handout (co-opting): intermediary Zamindars, revenue farmers, merchants, moneylenders and bankers were promised security of property and reasonable taxes and, most unprecedentedly, given Mansabs. The Mansabdari system was traditionally lineage-based (military / noble bloodlines only). Appointing merchants and bankers to Mansabs was a structural transformation: administrative rank and commercial wealth, historically kept apart, now merged.
  • All the steps were taken while accepting the symbolic sovereignty of the Mughal Emperor.
Note

Count check. The handout heading says "four-step process", its text lists Steps 1 to 5 and the diagram shows six steps (it adds "keep the Mughal symbolic link"). The slide has five steps plus the result. Learn the recipe, not the number.

5 · Bengal: Murshid Quli Khan (MQK, 1717–27)#

  • Bengal Subah = Bengal, Orissa and Bihar: vast and commercially rich. Capital: Murshidabad, on the banks of the Hooghly, then the major port (the British later developed Calcutta as an alternative).
  • MQK was the last Bengal Governor appointed as per the wish of the Emperor.
  • His path (handout): Diwan from 1700; under Farukhsiyar (1713–19) he also became Deputy Governor of Bengal Suba and Governor of Orissa. In 1717, when elevated to the Mansab of Nazim of Bengal Subah, he already was Diwan (combining finance and executive in one man = ending Akbar 56-05's checks and balances).
  • He still sent tribute to Delhi, but the emperor's approval for appointments in Bengal Suba was now a paper formality.
  • Revenue: brilliant administrator. Collected LR through intermediary Zamindars and got detailed field surveys done for fair assessment. The intermediaries grew powerful: by 1727 just fifteen were collecting 50% of Bengal Suba's LR.
  • Coercion: Mansabdars who resisted had their Tankha Jagirs in Bengal taken and were given Jagirs in Orissa; resisting Zamindars were punished by reducing the land from which they collected LR.
Teacher's board

MQK's rise = finance + executive + military power in one man. Field surveys gave a "justified / right LR demand." The LR chain on the board: ryots → intermediary Zamindars (I2) → Diwan of Suba → Imperial Diwan, with the money going either to the King or to a Jagirdar (J). "Co-opting powerful people" is written beside the 15 Zamindars.

6 · The Jagat Seths: why Bengal's power was diffused, not centralised#

  • "Jagat Seth" was a hereditary title, not a personal name, held by successive heads of a banking family: Manikchand under MQK; Mehtab Rai and Swarup Chand under Alivardi Khan 40-56.
  • Already powerful before 1717, under the successor State they rose to what Dutch records called the richest banking house in the world.
  • They loaned to Zamindars (so revenue reached the State on time), loaned to merchants, and ran Hundi services (pre-modern wire transfer).
  • Under Shuja-ud-din Md Khan (1727–39) a Jagat Seth was appointed Treasurer of Bengal and Daroga of the Royal Mint: central bank plus the Nawab's personal banker in one family.
Remember

Power was diffused, not centralised: a network of Nawab + intermediary Zamindars + merchants + bankers, with the Nawab on top but dependent on their cooperation.

Remember

Proof: the coup of 1740. Alivardi Khan 40-56 (Nawab of Patna) overthrew and killed the sitting Nawab Sarfaraz Khan (1739–40) with the Jagat Seth's backing. He secured formal approval by promising Emperor Md Shah one crore rupees plus Sarfaraz's personal wealth, and approval was duly given. The emperor had been reduced to a transactional figure.

7 · Bengal's virtual break: the Maratha raids and Alivardi Khan 40-56#

  • Under MQK, Bengal was autonomous but tribute still was sent to Delhi. Under Alivardi the break deepened, triggered by an external shock.

The virtual break of Bengal

1745-51: Raghuji Bhonsle of Nagpur raids Bengal repeatedly
Alivardi cannot defeat him
1751 settlement: Bhonsle gets annual Chauth (one-fourth of revenues) of Bengal and Bihar plus direct control of Orissa
Part of the revenue now flows to Nagpur
Alivardi stops sending revenue to Delhi and stops seeking the emperor's approval for provincial Mansabdar appointments
Virtual break complete, but independence never declared

8 · Awadh: from Sadat Khan 22-39 to the virtual break of 1753#

  • Founder: Sadat Ali Khan (1722–39), appointed Subahdar in 1722 as a reward for suppressing local revolts. He installed his nephew and son-in-law Safdar Jung 39-54 as Deputy Governor (Naib Nazim) and his own loyalist as Diwan; tribute to Delhi was reduced.
  • Revenue grew from efficient land assessment and taxes on trade. He created a new loyal class of local elites (Indian Muslims, Afghans, Hindus) by giving them Mansabs and Jagirs.
  • Unlike MQK, Sadat Khan 22-39 kept ambitions at the centre: he wanted a senior Mansab at Delhi (the Central Government). He convinced Nadir Shah to invade Delhi in 1739 because the Mir Bakshi post had gone to Chin Qulich Khan (another name of Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48) instead of him. Nadir Shah ill-treated him after the victory; he committed suicide.
  • Safdar Jung 39-54 was appointed Wazir by Ahmad Shah Bahadur (1748–54) in 1748. He misused the office to annex Farukhabad into Awadh. Other Mansabdars opposed, and in 1753 he was forced to resign. The resignation was Awadh's virtual break: no more revenue to Delhi, no imperial authority inside Awadh, though legally still part of the empire.
  • Shuja-ud-Daula 54-75 was appointed Wazir by Shah Alam II 60-06. He fought alongside Ahmad Shah Abdali 47-72 at the Third Battle of Panipat (1761) and was later defeated by the EIC at the Battle of Buxar (1764).
  • Awadh survived nominally until EIC annexation in 1856 from Nawab Wajid Ali Shah.
Note

Discrepancy to check. The slide calls Safdar Jung 39-54 the nephew of Sadat Khan 22-39; the handout calls him nephew and son-in-law. The handout in Chapter 2 also calls Sadat Khan 22-39 "of Irani group" in the Karnal passage.

9 · Hyderabad: Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48's path from Delhi to the Deccan#

  • Hyderabad was set up within the empire in 1724 by Chin Qulich Khan, aka Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48, aka Asaf Jah I (Nizam, 1724–48), after defeating Mubariz Khan, the sitting Nazim of the Deccan. He was the leader of the Turani group.
  • In Aurangzeb 58-07's Deccan wars he and his father built strong roots in the region.
  • 1720: the Turani and Irani groups removed the Sayyid Brothers, kingmakers who controlled Md Shah, and formally restored imperial authority. But the structural problem remained: Md Shah was uninterested in governance. (Remember: 5% of Mansabdars controlled 61% of all Jagir land revenue.)
  • As Wazir (1722–24) Nizam tried to redistribute Jagirs from big Mansabdars to smaller ones to ease the Jagirdari crisis, but the bigger Mansabdars did not agree.
  • The Marathas defeated him in 1728 and 1738, forcing him to pay Chauth and Sardeshmukhi (10% hereditary Maratha claim in the Deccan).
  • He served as Mir Bakshi (1739–40) during Nadir Shah's invasion. By 1740 he gave up on reforming the centre and moved to Hyderabad permanently.
  • Succession after Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48: Nazir Jung (1748–50) → Muzaffar Jung (1750–51, 3 months) → Salabat Jung 51-62, French dominated under Bussy) → Nizam-Ali-Khan (1762–1803).

AspectDetail

ToleranceThe Hindu majority was economically powerful, so Nizam followed religious tolerance and punished opponents regardless of community
Most aggressive changeOf the three States, Hyderabad converted Tankha to Vatan Jagirs most aggressively and made merchants, moneylenders and Ijaradars Mansabdars ("upstarts") on the largest scale
Remember

Merchants and moneylenders got property security and became Mansabdars (called upstarts), and in return gave financial support. The most pronounced transformation of the Mansabdari system was in Hyderabad. Result: the same diffused polity: Nawab at top, influential sections under him.

11 · The Carnatic: a State within a State#

Hyderabad had a smaller State inside its borders, the Carnatic. By law it was part of the Hyderabad province. But it had its own ruler, the Nawab, and ran its own government.

Teacher's board

Parallels for the Carnatic: India → J&K with Art. 370; Assam → Meghalaya as an autonomous State within Assam (Art. 244A); Hyderabad → Carnatic (part of Hyderabad), practically autonomous.

11.1 Backstory: the Vijayanagar Empire (1336–1646)

  • For three hundred years the Vijayanagar Empire was the strongest power in South India, and the Carnatic was part of it.
  • Provinces were ruled by Nayaks (teacher: Nayak = CM), Telugu Hindu governors from warrior families. Nayaks divided their provinces into Palayams, each run by a local chief called a Poligar (Palayakkarar) with his own private army and fort.

11.2 The long decline (1565–1698)

  1. A. Defeat of Vijayanagar at Talikota (1565). A four-state alliance of the Deccan Sultanates (Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, Bidar) destroyed the Vijayanagar army. The empire officially continued until 1646, but this defeat was the real end of its power.
  2. B. Nayaks independent, then defeated. After 1565 the southern Nayaks of Madurai, Tanjore and Gingee ruled autonomously; distance protected them for a while. By 1646 Bijapur and Golconda conquered and divided the Carnatic between themselves.
  3. C. Sultanates defeated by the Mughals. Aurangzeb 58-07 conquered Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687). The whole Carnatic now came under Mughal rule.
  4. D. Gingee under the Marathas, then lost to the Mughals. Shivaji 74-80 captured Gingee Fort from Bijapur in 1677. After the Mughals executed Sambhaji 80-89 in 1689, the next Chhatrapati, Rajaram 89-00, made Gingee his base against the Mughals. In 1690 Zulfiqar Khan (Nawab of Carnatic) surrounded the fort. The siege lasted eight years (the longest by the Mughals); the Mughals finally captured Gingee in 1698. (Zulfiqar Khan later became Wazir under Jahandar Shah 12-13, 1712, until his execution in February 1713.)

12 · Synthesis: the common pattern and what it made possible#

PointDetail
Concentration of powerAcross Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad (and the Carnatic): a powerful Mansabdar extracts the Subahdari, dismantles checks and balances, builds revenue strength, converts Tankha to Vatan, and co-opts merchants and bankers into the Mansabdari system
No independenceNone declared it: the Mughal umbrella served their interests; practically independent, including decisions of war and peace. Each developed its own diplomacy, army and fisc
Unique CarnaticPart of the Hyderabad Suba but autonomous: a successor State inside a successor State. Fragmentation was not only horizontal (Subahs breaking from the empire) but also vertical (sub-regions within a successor State developing their own semi-autonomous identity)
Mughal authorityThe emperor became a constitutional monarch with no executive power: a ceremonial head of State whose legitimacy was still useful to others
Remember

The India into which the EIC was stepping: competing, partially autonomous States, each with its own elite coalition and its own fiscal and military interests, and, critically, a world where legitimacy and real power had come apart. That gap between formal authority and actual power is exactly what made the subcontinent vulnerable to outside penetration.

Recall sheet#

Read once, hide it, then rebuild the chapter from memory.

  1. Three classes: Successor (Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad), Rebel (Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, Afghans), Already Autonomous (Rajputs, Mysore, Travancore).
  2. Founders: MQK 1717-27 (Bengal), Sadat Khan 22-39 (Awadh), Nizam-ul-Mulk 24-48 (Hyderabad). De facto sovereign, never declared independence.
  3. Four reasons not independent: symbolic legitimacy, ambitions at Delhi (Wazir, Mir Bakshi), military help, trade barriers.
  4. Nazim = Sword (Nizamat), Diwan = Purse (Diwani, reports to Imperial Diwan), Bakshi = audit and intelligence (reports to Mir Bakshi). Akbar 56-05's checks; every founder dismantled them.
  5. Nawab is not always Subahdar: Alivardi was Nawab of Patna / Naib Nazim of Bihar before 1740.
  6. Bakshi: records, payroll, dagh and chehra, waqia-navis. Second-highest official.
  7. Recipe: extract Subahdari, end checks, revenue strength, co-opt men of money (Mansabs to merchants), Tankha to Vatan.
  8. Bengal: MQK Diwan from 1700, Nazim 1717, 15 Zamindars 50% LR 1727, Jagat Seths (Manikchand, Mehtab Rai, Swarup Chand). Coup 1740 by Alivardi vs Sarfaraz.
  9. Bengal break: 1745-51 Bhonsle raids; 1751 Chauth of Bengal and Bihar plus Orissa; Alivardi stops revenue to Delhi. Siraj 56-57, Mir Jafar 57-60, Mir Qasim 60-63, Mir Jafar 63-65, Diwani to EIC 1765.
  10. Awadh: Sadat Khan 22-39 1722; Safdar Jung 39-54 Wazir 1748, annexed Farukhabad, resigned 1753; Shuja Panipat 1761, Buxar 1764; annexed 1856 (Wajid Ali Shah).
  11. Hyderabad: Nizam 1724 beat Mubariz Khan; Wazir 1722-24; Marathas beat him 1728, 1738; Mir Bakshi 1739-40; left Delhi 1740; Hindu majority tolerated; most aggressive Tankha to Vatan.
  12. Carnatic: Hyderabad Subah = Hyderabad (north of Krishna) + Hyderabad-Karnatic (12 districts south; capital Gingee). Saadatullah 1710-32 hereditary, capital to Arcot. Dost Ali 1732-40. Anwar-ud-din 1744.
  13. Vijayanagar 1336-1646; Nayaks, Palayams, Poligars; Talikota 1565; Gingee siege 1690-98.
  14. Synthesis: emperor became a constitutional monarch with no executive power; legitimacy and real power had come apart.
