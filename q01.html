2025PYQ 1 Mughal Religious Vision (see 'What was Akbar56-05's religious

vision — Sulh-i-Kul, Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, Din-i-Ilahi?')

Mains 2025 · 10 marks · 150 words "Examine the main aspects of Akbar's religious syncretism."

1. Ibadat Khana (1575) — House of Worship at Fatehpur Sikri; multi-faith debates; exposed Akbar to diversity of religious thought and to bitter Ulama rivalries (see 'What was Akbar56-05's religious vision — Sulh-i-Kul, Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, Din-i-Ilahi?').

2. Mahzar (1579) — Infallibility Decree — gave Akbar authority to adjudicate religious disputes; effectively made the Emperor, not the clergy, the final word on matters of faith (see 'What was Akbar56-05's religious vision — Sulh-i-Kul, Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, Din-i-Ilahi?').

3. Din-i-Ilahi (1582) — syncretic spiritual order borrowing from Islam, Hinduism, Zoroastrianism, & Christianity; very few formal followers; significance lay in the principle expressed (see 'What was Akbar56-05's religious vision — Sulh-i-Kul, Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, Din-i-Ilahi?').

4. Sulh-i-Kul — universal peace — State must treat all religions with equal respect; no single faith should dominate policy. Became the ideological foundation of Mughal governance under Akbar and continued to influence Mughal politics long after his death (see 'What was Akbar56-05's religious vision — Sulh-i-Kul, Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, Din-i-Ilahi?').

Closing line: the four-part sequence (Ibadat Khana → Mahzar → Din-i-Ilahi → Sulh-i-Kul) shows Akbar moving deliberately from open inter-faith debate, to State authority over religion, to a syncretic spiritual formulation, to a governing principle that outlasted his reign. FYQ 2

Which one of the following best explains why three of the most consequential battles in Indian history (1526, 1556, 1761) were fought at Panipat?

Answer: (B)

Panipat sat in a narrow corridor between the Thar Desert and the Himalayan foothills, with the Yamuna acting as a defensive choke-point. ✓ — 'Mughal Take Over from Lodis': Three geographical factors converged at Panipat — proximity to the Khyber Pass (primary invasion route), narrow corridor between Thar Desert (south) and Himalayan foothills (north), and the Yamuna to the east as a defensive choke-point.

FYQ 6

In the Mansabdari System under Akbar (r. 1556–1605), what did the term Sawar rank denote?

Answer: (B)

The number of horsemen the Mansabdar must maintain and provide to the King ✓ — 'How was rank determined? Zat + Sawar': Each Mansabdar carried two ranks — Zat (personal rank, governing seniority and status) and Sawar (the number of horsemen he must maintain and provide to the King). Net rank = Zat + Sawar.

FYQ 9

Answer: (B)

FYQ 1 Examine the institutional foundations laid by Babur and Akbar for the Mughal Empire's longevity.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Identify the founding-era institutions and weigh how each contributed to the Mughal Empire's later longevity.

Guiding themes:

1. Babur's establishment of the dynasty (1526) — Panipat I victory, geographic anchor in the Delhi heartland, integration of Central Asian (Timurid) and Indian governance traditions (see 'Mughal Take Over from Lodis').

2. Akbar's land revenue revolution — the Zabti system based on scientifically measured demand + a salaried bureaucracy under the Mansabdari system replaced the older revenue-farming model (see 'How did Akbar56-05 build the Mughal State — the Mansabdari System' and 'Mughal Land Revenue (LR) system').

3. Akbar's religious-administrative synthesis — Sulh-i-Kul as State doctrine; Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, and Din-i-Ilahi as institutional expressions; Watan Jagirs for Rajput integration (see 'How did Akbar56-05 build the Mughal State — the Mansabdari System' and 'What was Akbar56-05's religious vision — Sulh-i-Kul, Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, Din-i-Ilahi?').

4. Persianate court culture under Humayun's Safavid alliance — Persian language, art, and administrative grammar became the imperial standard (see 'Humayun30-40 & Sher Shah Suri40-45 (1540-45)').

5. Conclusion: the founding generations built four institutional pillars — political (dynasty), economic (Zabti), military-administrative (Mansabdari), and ideological (Sulh-i-Kul) — that together explain why the Mughal Empire could absorb pressures for nearly two centuries (see 'What was Akbar56-05's religious vision — Sulh-i-Kul, Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, Din-i-Ilahi?').

FYQ 2 Discuss the evolution of the Mughal land revenue system from Akbar's Zabti to the late-Mughal Ijaradari, and its consequences for the State and the peasantry.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Trace the change from Akbar's scientifically measured Zabti to the auction-based Ijaradari, and assess what each meant for State capacity and peasant welfare.

Guiding themes:

1. Akbar's Zabti (1556-1605) — scientifically measured demand based on area + average yield + standard prices, with the Patwari-Qanungo-Amalguzar hierarchy reporting on actual cultivation. State capacity high; peasant burden predictable (see 'Mughal Land Revenue (LR) system').

2. Continuation under Akbar's successors — Jahangir, Shah Jahan, and early Aurangzeb retained the framework, with limited shifts to Ijaradari at the margins (see 'Mughal Land Revenue (LR) system').

3. Jahandar Shah (1712-13) — first institutionalisation of Ijaradari in the richest provinces (Punjab + Bengal); private bidders bought 3-5 year revenue rights at auction (see 'Mughal Land Revenue (LR) system').

4. Farukhsiyar (1713-19) — fatal extension of Ijaradari into royal Khalisa lands; the State's own income now came through middlemen with extractive incentives (see 'Mughal Land Revenue (LR) system').

5. Consequence for the State — short-term cash inflow but loss of administrative knowledge about real revenue capacity; the State's grip on revenue weakened over decades.

6. Consequence for the peasantry — Ijaradars had 3-5 year horizons, no incentive for long-term investment; over-extraction, peasant flight, and the rise of revenue arrears characterise the later-Mughal countryside (see 'Mughal Land Revenue (LR) system').

7. Conclusion: the Zabti-to-Ijaradari trajectory mirrors the wider Mughal decline — institutional rigour gave way to short-term cash, with State capacity and peasant welfare both eroding in parallel.
