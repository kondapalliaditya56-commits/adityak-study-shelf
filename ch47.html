Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • Labour: Madras Labour Union 1918 (B.P. Wadia), AITUC 1920 (Lajpat Rai first president), Trade Union Act 1926, Whitley Commission 1929 (report 1931), Trade Disputes Bill 1929, HMS 1948 (Ashok Mehta, T.S. Ramanujam, G.G. Mehta), Lokhande as labour pioneer.
  • Left: CPI Tashkent 1920 (M.N. Roy, Vanguard) and Kanpur 1925; Dange, Muzaffar Ahmad, P.C. Joshi; CSP 1934 (JP, Masani, Narendra Dev); Comintern 1928 and 1935; People's War 1941.
  • Capitalists: FICCI 1927, Bombay Plan 1944 (eight drafters), Bombay Manifesto 1936, Currency League 1926, Birla, Bajaj.
  • Dalits: Vital-Vidhvansak 1888, Vaikom, Self-Respect 1925, Mahad 1927, Communal Award and Poona Pact 1932, ILP 1936, AISCF 1942.
  • Peasants: AIKS 1936 (Sahajanand, N.G. Ranga), Kisan Manifesto, Tebhaga one-half to one-third, Worli, Punnapra-Vayalar, Telangana, Bakasht.

Mains hooks

  • Many voices enriching the nationalist movement in the Gandhian phase: workers, capitalists, Dalits, women, peasants.
  • Role of women in the freedom struggle, especially the Gandhian phase; age, gender and religion barriers.
  • Gandhi and Ambedkar: divergent methods, common goal of amelioration of the downtrodden.
  • Examine how the INM marginalised workers, capitalists, Dalits and women in different ways (FYQ).

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: The national movement was a coalition, and each constituency joined on its own terms. Workers entered late, were pulled in at Gaya 1922, and were split by CPI's Comintern-driven swings. Capitalists funded the INM but stopped where capitalism itself was threatened. Dalits and women found the INC useful but built their own platforms (Ambedkar, Periyar, AIWC), while peasants moved from Gandhian protest to AIKS to armed 1946 revolts. Everywhere the INC absorbed rather than represented, and the unfinished business passed to the Republic.

  1. Workers appear but wait. Modern industry created workers in the second half of the 19th century. Early unions were welfare bodies (Lokhande 1890) and then the Madras Labour Union (1918). Their strikes had no wider politics.
    → But the INC Moderates saw no reason to take up their cause.
  2. Moderates look away. Naoroji's 1886 rule kept class issues out of Congress to protect unity with zamindars and Indian industry. Moderates opposed the Factory Acts yet backed workers against British employers (Assam tea 1881-82, GIP Signallers 1899).
    → So Swadeshi, the Extremists and the Russian Revolution had to push labour in.
  3. Labour enters the mainstream. Swadeshi strikes, the Tilak trial protest and the war-time price rise drew workers in. AITUC (1920) gave an all-India body; C.R. Das at Gaya (1922) argued Congress must absorb class demands or lose them.
    → Now a Left grew inside and outside the INC.
  4. The Left grows and splits. CPI arrived via Tashkent 1920 and Kanpur 1925 (Dange, Muzaffar Ahmad, Joshi). The Trade Union Act 1926 recognised unions while Meerut and the 1929 bills coerced them. The 1928 Comintern line made CPI call Congress bourgeois.
    → But 1935 and 1941 turned the line around again.
  5. Capital backs the INM, conditionally. Colonial tariffs and credit starved Indian business, so FICCI (1927) tied itself to the INC. Business loved Swadeshi, funded Gandhi, cut British cloth imports by half in the CDM and drafted the Bombay Plan, but feared strikes and class war.
    → Meanwhile the INC's silence on caste left Dalits to speak for themselves.
  6. Dalits build their own road. Walangkar, Vaikom, Periyar's Self-Respect Movement and Guruvayur attacked untouchability. Ambedkar moved to water (Mahad 1927), burnt the Manusmriti and demanded separate electorates.
    → So the 1932 Communal Award forced Gandhi and Ambedkar into direct conflict.
  7. Poona Pact and after. Gandhi's fast unto death ended with joint electorates and more reserved seats. INC won only half of reserved seats in 1937; Ambedkar's ILP and AISCF were squeezed. After 1946 he drafted the Constitution, quit over the Hindu Code Bill and converted in 1956.
    → Women's story ran in parallel, also without power.
  8. Women: participation without power. Reformers spoke about women, not with them. Gandhi's frame of sisterhood, charkha and picketing brought millions out, yet INC never made women decision-makers; AIWC (1927) was nationalist first, feminist second.
    → And in the countryside, a Depression turned peasants radical.
  9. Peasants radicalise. Prices halved 1929-33 but rent and debt did not; CDM politicised the peasantry and the left organised it. AIKS (1936) with Sahajanand and Ranga issued the Kisan Manifesto; Karachi 1931 and Faizpur 1936 tied INC to them.
    → But governing in 1937-39 split INC from the Kisan Sabha.
  10. Ministries, Haripura and war. INC ministries struck middle-path agrarian deals and used police on Bakasht (about 600 arrests). Haripura 1938 drew a line, AIKS passed to the CPI, and war, famine and Quit India made peasants the backbone of 1942.
    → After 1945 the anger became open revolt.
  11. The 1946 revolts and two readings. Worli, Bakasht, Punnapra-Vayalar, Tebhaga and Telangana fought feudal extraction, some serving national integration and some outside the Gandhian frame. Chandra reads them as anti-imperial; Bandyopadhyay as autonomous class war.

In simple words#

  • Moderates kept class issues out of Congress (Naoroji 1886) except where the employer was British.
  • C.R. Das at Gaya 1922: absorb workers and peasants or they will form their own class movements.
  • CPI's 1928 Comintern line (INC is bourgeois) isolated it; 1935 and 1941 flipped it again.
  • Capitalists backed the INM as long as it fought the British, not when it threatened property; the Bombay Plan 1944 asked the future state to plan.
  • Gandhi: change the heart; Ambedkar: change the law. Poona Pact traded separate electorates for more reserved seats.
  • Women were called on as lawbreakers, not lawmakers; the AIWC put nation before feminism.
  • Peasants moved Gandhian protest (1930) to AIKS (1936) to armed revolt (1946); historians split on whether this was anti-colonial or a class war.

1 · The Working Class Movement: origins and the Moderates (no class notes)#

Story link

Because modern industry (railways, telegraphs, mills) came late and under colonial rule, the Indian worker became a social fact long before a political force. Ask: why did the early INC look away from workers?

1.1 Who was the modern Indian worker?

  • No meaningful number of modern workers before the second half of the 19th century; railways, telegraphs, textile mills created the class.
  • Contrast: Europe had a large organised working class by the early 19th century; India's industrial capitalism came later and under colonial conditions.
  • Early action = immediate economic grievances only: scattered, short-lived strikes with no wider politics. A social fact long before a political force.
  • 1870: Sasipada Bannerjee (Brahmo Samajist) set up a Working Men's Club and monthly journal Bharat Shramjeevi.
  • 1880: Narayan Lokhande started the Anglo-Marathi weekly Deen Bandhu; by 1890 he set up the Bombay Mills & Mill Hands Association, a philanthropic welfare body, not a modern trade union.
  • First modern organised trade union: Madras Labour Union (1918), founded by B.P. Wadia.
  • The all-India force emerged only through its growing link with the Indian National Movement (INM).

1.2 Why did the Moderates pay so little attention to workers?

  • INC (1885) Moderates: Congress should take up only political questions common to all Indians, not social divisions or class grievances.
  • Dadabhai Naoroji formalised this at the 1886 INC session: Congress would not take up issues that could set Indians against one another.
  • Practical logic: a strong pro-peasant stand would antagonise zamindars who were part of the nationalist coalition. Premature class conflict was a strategic liability, not a moral failing.
  • Economic view: Indian industry was nascent, faced British imports and British-owned firms, with no government support. Moderates saw industrial capitalism as the path to modernise India.
  • So they opposed the Factory Acts of 1881 and 1891 (reduced working hours raised costs for Indian mill owners).
  • The one consistent exception: workers in foreign-owned (British) enterprises
    • 1881-82: strongly backed workers in the Assam tea gardens (British-owned)
    • 1899: Signallers' Strike on the Great Indian Peninsular (GIP) Railways, the first organised industrial strike in India, backed by INC
  • Pattern: Indian employer, INC stood aside; British employer, INC engaged.

1.3 How did workers first enter the INM?

  • By the late 19th century, growth in industrial workers made the Moderate position hard to hold. Extremists (Lal, Bal, Pal, Aurobindo Ghosh) argued INC must take up all workers' issues for a real mass movement.
  • Swadeshi Movement (1905-11) was the turning point: important strikes in foreign enterprises led by C.R. Das, B.C. Pal, Liaquat Hussain.
  • First attempt at an all-India union of workers came in the Swadeshi years; it failed, but showed new awareness. Workers were drawn into the INM beyond their workplace concerns.
  • Tilak trial (1908): Tilak tried for sedition for writing in Kesari supporting Prafulla Chaki and Khudiram Bose (Muzaffarpur Conspiracy Case, 1908); one of the largest working-class demonstrations till then took place in his support.
  • Russian Revolution of 1905 and workers' role in it impressed the Extremists: organised labour as a source of strength; cautious start of socialist thinking in INC circles.
  • When the INM receded after Swadeshi, the working class movement receded with it: the two were clearly linked.

2 · Workers in and after World War I: AITUC and Gaya (no class notes)#

Story link

Because the Swadeshi surge faded, labour waited for the next national wave. The war and the Russian Revolution lit it again. Ask: what finally pulled the INC towards workers?

1.4 The revival during and after World War I (1914-18)

  • Revival driven by economic hardship (wartime inflation eroded real wages) and political awakening.
  • Russian Revolution of 1917 showed organised workers could be a decisive political force: some read it as a model of revolution, others as a model of mass organisation; both readings coexisted in India.
  • Developments of the period
    • Home Rule League Movement (HRL, 1916-18): workers participated actively
    • Ahmedabad mill strike (1918) led by Gandhi, produced the Textile Labour Association (TLA, 1920), an early working model of Gandhian arbitration between labour and capital
    • Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919): workers among main participants
    • Non-Cooperation Movement (NCM, 1920-22): strikes in many sectors; J.M. Sen Gupta led strikes in railways, steamer services and Assam tea plantations; workers struck during the Prince of Wales visit (November 1921)
  • Workers moved from the margins of the INM into its mainstream.

1.5 AITUC (1920): the first all-India organisation of workers

  • All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC), 1920, under Lala Lajpat Rai: the first all-India organisation of workers; it represented India at the International Labour Organisation (ILO) of the League of Nations from the start.
  • Lajpat Rai was the first Indian leader to explicitly connect imperialism with capitalism: colonial rule and capitalist exploitation are parts of one structure (a colonial power wants cheap raw materials and a captive market, so its economic interests and its business class align).
  • This gave labour a role in the anti-colonial movement itself. The 1920s saw stronger, longer strikes, and an organisation to coordinate them.
  • After independence, rival federations emerged: Hind Mazdoor Sabha (HMS) founded at Howrah, 29 December 1948 by socialists and Forward Bloc unionists Ashok Mehta, T.S. Ramanujam, G.G. Mehta (Prelims 2018, answer (d)).
Handout alert
  • Handout alert: Sec. 1.5 says AITUC was formed under Lajpat Rai; the PYQ alert line says founder = N.M. Joshi, first president = Lala Lajpat Rai. Use: Lajpat Rai = first president, N.M. Joshi = founder-figure of the early union movement.

1.6 INC Gaya Session (1922): a change of position

  • C.R. Das argued: if Congress keeps ignoring workers' and peasants' grievances, they will launch independent class movements (workers against capitalists, peasants against landlords) and fragment the national coalition. Only fix: bring their demands into the nationalist programme.
  • INC accepted: formally welcomed AITUC (1920) and, after 1922, began supporting workers' and peasants' issues.
  • Gandhi's middle path: trusteeship and arbitration. Those with capital hold it in trust for society; labour-capital disputes are settled by negotiation, not confrontation. Not anti-capitalist; an attempt to make capitalism compatible with an equitable social order.

3 · The Communist Party of India and the Left within the INC (no class notes)#

Story link

Because 1917 inspired workers everywhere, communist parties sprang up worldwide. Ask: who built the Indian party, and what did each leader own?

1.7 CPI and the growth of the Left

  • CPI first established at Tashkent (Uzbekistan, USSR), 1920, by M.N. Roy, Abani Mukherji and others (Prelims 2005: Roy headed the group). Set up inside India at the Kanpur Conference, December 1925, to unite scattered communist groups into a single legal party.
  • Key leaders: S.V. Ghate; M. Singaravelu Chettiar (Madras; presided over Kanpur; first to celebrate May Day in India, 1923).
  • Satyabhakta convened Kanpur but wanted a purely nationalist party independent of the Comintern; outvoted by Ghate and Singaravelu, he resigned.
  • S.A. Dange, Muzaffar Ahmad, P.C. Joshi = three tallest leaders of CPI (1925-1947). Dange, Muzaffar Ahmad and Shaukat Usmani were imprisoned in the 1924 Kanpur Bolshevik Conspiracy Case.
  • M.N. Roy's emigre communist journal = Vanguard (Prelims 1995).

4 · Trade Union Act 1926, the British response and the Whitley Commission (no class notes)#

Story link

Because the Left and labour kept growing, the British answered with law (recognise), rules (restrict) and force (arrest). Ask: which tool was which?

1.8 The Trade Union Act, 1926

  • Landmark: gave legal recognition to trade unions for the first time; protected union funds from arbitrary seizure; gave immunity to members and office-bearers for legitimate strike action.
  • Turned unionism from an informal, legally vulnerable activity into a recognised institution with enforceable rights.
  • Irony: the same government that tried to suppress labour (Meerut Case 1929, Trade Disputes Bill 1929) gave it legal standing, partly under ILO pressure, partly to channel agitation into regulated forms.

1.9 British approach to the rise of the Left

  • Measures were reformative, regulatory (Trade Union Act 1926) and coercive.
  • Public Safety Bill (1929): powers of deportation. Trade Disputes Bill (1929): significant restrictions on labour action (Prelims 2017: the Trade Disputes measure banned strikes and set up tribunals).
  • Context for the bomb in the Central Legislative Assembly (CLA) on 8 April 1929 by Bhagat Singh and B.K. Dutt: the Assembly was discussing these very bills. It was designed to be heard, not to kill, to force attention on laws passed over the people's heads.
  • Meerut Conspiracy Case (1929): many communists, including foreign nationals, arrested under Section 121A IPC (1860) for conspiracy to deprive the King of sovereignty over British India. Triggers: ongoing railway strikes and attempts to set up a branch of the Comintern. The trial dragged on for years and organisationally weakened the movement.
Handout alert
  • Handout alert: handout calls the 1929 measure a Bill; Prelims 2017 calls it the Trade Disputes Act 1929. The key answer is (d) a system of tribunals and a ban on strikes.

The Whitley Commission (Royal Commission on Labour, 1929)

  • Chaired by J.H. Whitley; appointed 1929; six Indian members including G.D. Birla and N.M. Joshi; report in 1931.
  • Found poverty the root cause of India's industrial problems; critical of British employers' role.
  • Recommendations
    • Legal recognition of trade unions and collective bargaining
    • Standardised wages in cotton and jute industries
    • Works committees at factory level
    • Conciliation officers for disputes
    • Systematic labour statistics
    • Tripartite consultative bodies (government, employers, workers): foundation of the post-independence Indian Labour Conference
  • Limited implementation under colonial rule, but the blueprint for Factories Act (1948) and Minimum Wages Act (1948).

5 · CPI's break with the INC, Comintern reversals and the final phase (no class notes)#

Story link

Because a Comintern order in 1928 turned the CPI against Congress, labour lost the biggest mass platform; a second order in 1935 and a third event in 1941 swung it back and forth. Ask: what did each swing cost?

1.10 The CPI's break with the INC

  • 6th World Congress of the Comintern (1928): oppose all non-communist political forces. CPI 1925 called INC a bourgeois party and Nehru and Bose representatives of propertied interests; cut itself off from India's largest mass movement.
  • CPI 1925 opposed the Civil Disobedience Movement (CDM, 1930-34), called Swaraj illusory, and called the Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931) proof of compromise. By 1934 the communists themselves wound up the WPPs inside INC.
  • INC called workers the "hands & feet" of the CDM. Participation fell short, but: after Gandhi's arrest in May 1930, Sholapur textile workers struck so seriously that the British imposed martial law; GIP railway workers lay on the tracks to stop trains.
  • Congress Socialist Party (CSP, 1934) formed inside INC by Jayaprakash Narayan, Minoo Masani, Acharya Narendra Dev (Prelims 1996: Narendra Dev, answer (d)). Critical of Gandhi over CDM withdrawal, but rejected CPI's anti-Congress line: stayed inside to shift INC from within.

6 · Indian Capitalists: who they were and what they resented (no class notes)#

Story link

Because the colonial economy was built for British commerce, Indian business grew under deliberate disadvantage. Ask: which grievances were economic and which political?

2.1 Who were Indian capitalists and what were their grievances?

  • Industrialists, traders, mill owners, merchants: grew in the second half of the 19th century with modern industry, under unfavourable conditions.
  • British imports entered at low tariffs, Indian goods faced high duties in Britain (the classic colonial trade trap). No government support, no protection, no representation in economic policy bodies.
  • Five core economic complaints
    • Trade and tariff policy favoured British manufacturers
    • Heavy taxation, including income tax introduced in 1868 despite famines
    • Restrictions on production of some goods, e.g. low-grade steel
    • Government preference to British firms in contracts and procurement
    • No developmental support or institutional credit infrastructure

The tariff story and the Factory Acts

  • 19th-century British textiles entered India virtually duty-free. A modest import duty on cotton in 1894 was cancelled out by a matching countervailing excise duty on Indian mill cloth, forced by the Lancashire lobby.
  • The Fiscal Autonomy Convention after WWI gave India some tariff-setting power; the post-CDM period brought meaningful protective tariffs, a concrete gain through nationalist pressure.
  • Factory Acts of 1881 and 1891 were pushed by the Lancashire lobby: stricter regulation raised Indian costs and cut competitiveness. Indian capitalists and Moderates opposed them. The British regulated out of fear of Indian competition, not sympathy for workers.
  • FICCI (Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce & Industry), 1927: organised voice of Indian business. It refused to negotiate with the British government on constitutional or economic issues without INC approval or participation: big business made the INC its political representative.

8 · The Bombay Plan, INA support and the capitalist triangle (no class notes)#

Story link

Because Indian capital was too weak to build heavy industry alone, its leaders asked the future state to plan. Ask: how did the same men fund Gandhi, the INA and a planned economy?

2.4 The Bombay Plan (1944)

  • Formal title: A Plan of Economic Development for India. Drafted by eight industrialists and technocrats: J.R.D. Tata, G.D. Birla, Purushottamdas Thakurdas, Ardeshir Dalal, Kasturbhai Lalbhai, A.D. Shroff, Lala Shri Ram, John Mathai.
  • Published January 1944, at the height of the Bengal Famine (1943) and QIM repression: the first ever national economic plan for India.
  • Proposed a mixed economy: heavy state investment in basic industries, infrastructure, transport, power, and social welfare (education, housing, healthcare). Aim: double per capita income in 15 years via three five-year plans.
  • Widely read, translated, reprinted, endorsed by FICCI at its 1944 meeting; direct and acknowledged influence on the First Five Year Plan.
  • Criticised from all sides: left said it protected private property; right said it was socialist; the British (Viceroy's office) reportedly considered a rival pamphlet. Upsetting everyone roughly equally marks it as centrist.

2.5 INA trials and the final phase (1945-46)

  • Capitalists funded and backed the INA men's defence. The INA Defence Committee raised funds, G.D. Birla among major contributors; Bhulabhai Desai led the defence at the Red Fort.
  • Even earlier, Indian businessmen in Malaya, Singapore and Burma funded the INA and the Indian Independence League (IIL): among the first open business support of a political movement against the colonial government.

9 · The Voice of Dalits: methods, Vaikom, Self-Respect and Guruvayur (no class notes)#

Story link

Because the INC kept social questions off its agenda, Dalits built their own struggles. Ask: what did symbolic and direct action each win?

3.1 How did Dalit movements operate?

  • Not one force: local struggles, regional assertions, gradually a national political voice, all driven by the caste system's denial of basic dignity.
  • Methods: symbolic (wearing the sacred thread, community pujas, rituals upper castes claimed) and direct action (forcing entry into temples).
  • Early pioneer Gopal Baba Walangkar, a Mahar ex-soldier: 1888 started Vital-Vidhvansak ('Destroyer of Ceremonial Pollution'), the first monthly journal aimed at the untouchable community (Prelims 2020, answer (a)).

3.2 Vaikom Satyagraha (1924-25)

  • In Travancore, the first major organised temple-entry movement of the modern period; led by the Ezhava community, with Narayan Guru and K. Kesava Menon at the forefront.
  • Demand: the right to use public roads near the Vaikom temple barred to lower castes.
  • Became all-India; Periyar (E.V. Ramaswamy Naicker) joined. INC formally supported; Savarnas boycotted Avarnas and pro-Satyagraha Congressmen.
  • Gandhi visited in 1925, met the Queen of Travancore; compromise: some roads opened to Avarnas, others reserved for Savarnas, some temple gates stayed closed. Principle acknowledged, not fully enforced.

3.3 The Self-Respect Movement (1925)

  • Periyar founded it in 1925 in the Madras Presidency: assault on Brahminical dominance; rejected caste hierarchy and Brahminical rituals; promoted self-respect marriages (no Brahmin priest, no Sanskrit mantras).
  • Asserted that lower castes could define their own dignity, ceremonies and rules. Link: Periyar joined Vaikom and founded this movement the same year.
  • PYQs: Prelims 2019 (pairs: Anti-Untouchability League-Gandhi, AIKS-Sahajanand, Self-Respect-E.V. Ramaswami Naicker: all correct, (d)), Prelims 2021, Prelims 2025 (founder = E.V. Ramaswamy Naicker, (a)).

3.4 Guruvayur Satyagraha (1931-33)

  • Guruvayur is in Malabar (today Thrissur district, Kerala), a major Krishna temple.
  • K. Kelappan led a march for temple entry and a fast unto death, broken only on Gandhi's personal appeal. A.K. Gopalan (later a major communist leader) walked across Malabar.
  • Pressure lasted till 1936: the King of Travancore issued a royal proclamation opening all temples in Travancore to all Hindus regardless of caste. (1936 also saw INC Faizpur and AIKS.)

10 · Dr B.R. Ambedkar: a different path, and the Gandhi-Ambedkar divide (no class notes)#

Story link

Because temple entry sought entry into the system that caused the injustice, Ambedkar moved to water, education and power. Ask: what did Gandhi and Ambedkar share, and where did they split?

3.5 Dr B.R. Ambedkar: a different path

  • 1927: Mahad Satyagraha, 15,000 Dalits in Mahad, Maharashtra, asserting the right to draw water from a public tank (Chavdar Tank, Prelims 2013). The colonial administration had granted it; upper castes refused. Ambedkar led Dalits to the tank to drink.
  • December 1927, same Mahad conference: Ambedkar publicly burnt the Manusmriti, the legal text that codified caste hierarchy and the subjugation of Dalits and women.
  • 1934: when temple-entry Satyagrahis agitated at the Kalaram Temple, Nasik, he asked them to stop and boycott temples: no solution within Hinduism. Answer: education and political power, with reservation in education, jobs and representation as non-negotiable compensation.

3.6 Why were Dalits alienated from the INC?

  • The Bengal Renaissance was dominated by upper castes and their concerns; Hindu revivalism of the 1870s-80s hardened caste lines; INC's 1886 policy of avoiding social questions kept Dalit grievances off the agenda.
  • Gandhi upheld the varnashrama system till the 1940s; his approach was moral and religious (penance, glorifying sweepers' work, making untouchability socially unacceptable). It dignified but did not empower. Ambedkar's critique: Dalits treated as objects of upper-caste compassion, not agents.
  • Nehru refused to see caste as a political problem: a social question to dissolve with modernisation. Gandhi offered religion, Nehru economics, neither offered power.

11 · Dalit politics: organisations, Poona Pact and the final chapter (no class notes)#

Story link

Because moral appeals could not give Dalits power, Ambedkar demanded separate electorates; Gandhi's fast forced a compromise. Ask: what was gained and what was lost at Poona?

3.8 The organisations and the political battle

  • 1926: All India Depressed Classes Association (AIDCA) under M.C. Rajah of Madras. 1930: Ambedkar's All India Depressed Classes Congress (AIDCC), anti-INC, to negotiate with the British directly.
  • 1928: before the Simon Commission (1927-29), Ambedkar demanded separate electorates for Dalits: the mechanism given to Muslims in the Indian Councils Act (1909) and accepted by INC in the Lucknow Pact (1916).
  • 1st RTC (1930), boycotted by INC: Ambedkar attended and repeated the demand. 2nd RTC (1931): the decisive Gandhi vs Ambedkar confrontation. Gandhi: Dalits are Hindus; separation would permanently divide Hindu society. Ambedkar: without separate electorates Dalits stay dependent on upper-caste voters and never elect genuine representatives.
  • Communal Award (August 1932), by PM Ramsay MacDonald, sided with Ambedkar: reserved seats via separate electorates. Gandhi began a fast unto death from jail. He backed reservation but opposed separate electorates.
  • Poona Pact (September 1932): reserved seats kept (number increased over the Communal Award) but with joint electorates; the instrument of electoral independence was lost. Ambedkar called signing it one of the worst days of his life. Formalised in the Government of India Act (1935).
  • Harijan Campaign after Poona: Gandhi toured, opened wells and temples, raised funds. Other INC leaders were indifferent; Nehru did not see caste as a political problem. 1937 provincial elections: INC won only half of the reserved Dalit seats; INC ministries (1937-39) did little for Dalits.

3.9 Ambedkar's political organisations

  • 1936: Independent Labour Party (ILP) to unite poor workers and untouchables (merging class and caste, since the poorest workers and most oppressed castes were often the same people); won most reserved seats in Bombay (1937).
  • 1942: Labour Member in the Viceroy's Executive Council; a few days before QIM he formed the All India Scheduled Castes Federation (AISCF, 1942), motto: Dalits are distinct from Hindus.
  • After QIM the AISCF struggled: many Dalits followed Gandhi; late formation and weak structure; communists eroded its poorer-Dalit base; in the 1946 elections it was marginalised by the INC-ML contest.

12 · The Voice of Women: reform, education and economics (no class notes)#

Story link

Because 19th-century reformers spoke about women but not with them, women were subjects of reform, not participants. Ask: whose agenda was women's education?

4.1 Why did 19th-century reform not produce women leaders?

  • Reformers passed laws on sati, widow remarriage, women's education, but treated women as the subject of reform, not its participants.
  • By late 19th century Hindu revivalism pushed back; conservatism returned. Purdah spread among upper-caste Hindus as a marker of respectability.
  • Sanskritisation hurt lower-caste women: purity of women became a symbol of caste honour; widow remarriage, practised by lower castes, was given up. Freedom shrank as caste aspiration rose.

4.2 Women's education: who wanted it and why

  • British: wanted English-educated women to raise pro-Western children and, by keeping women domestic, to stop them joining the INM. Bhadralok: wanted an educated companion. Reformers: to counter the British critique that Indian society was backward, as women's status was a barometer of civilisation. All male agendas.
  • Bethune School (1849): Drinkwater Bethune, Law Member of the Governor-General's Council, with special interest from Dalhousie. Hunter Commission (1882): liberal grants and scholarships for women's education. Social stereotyping remained a barrier.
  • Tarabai Shinde: Comparison Between Women & Men (1882), a fierce argument that men enjoyed all rights while women were reduced to the pativrata stereotype.
  • Pandita Ramabai: Brahmin woman who refused the educated-but-obedient-wife model; stayed unmarried for years, married a Sudra man, widowed, studied medicine in England, converted to Christianity, went to the US and raised funds for a widow's home in Bombay. Criticised even by reformers who claimed to support women.

4.3 The economic dimension

  • Men migrated to city factories; women stayed in villages in agricultural labour with no formal recognition.
  • In factories women got lower wages (domestic-skill stereotype). Mechanisation displaced women's craft and cottage industries more than men's.
  • Prosperity in peasant families often meant withdrawal of women from labour, since confinement was a status sign: development reduced women's public participation.

13 · Women and the INM: participation without power, and Gandhi (no class notes)#

Story link

Because the INC ignored social questions until 1917, women entered nationalism in symbolic roles. Ask: what did Gandhi change, and what stayed the same?

4.4 Women and the INM: participation without power

  • INC until 1917 took up no social questions. Early roles: keepers of the household, guardians of Swadeshi, boycotters. In Swadeshi (1905-11) the 'Mother India' focus kept participation domestic.
  • Exception: Sarala Debi Chaudhrani, involved in the physical culture movement for Bengal's youth (martial exercises).
  • In revolutionary activity women gave shelter, acted as couriers, passed messages: dangerous but invisible.
  • Post-WWI: Annie Besant (central to HRL, 1916-18) and Sarojini Naidu. 1917: Naidu led a delegation to the Secretary of State demanding votes for women. GOI Act (1919) gave women the vote, with property qualifications. 1918: she demanded equal eligibility criteria within INC. 1925: Naidu elected INC President, the first woman in that post.

4.5 Gandhi and women: a transformation

  • Non-violence and moral authority removed the need for physical strength: courage, discipline, willingness to suffer could not be denied to women.
  • Frame shifted from 'motherhood' (passive, domestic) to 'sisterhood' (active equal participants), negating the centrality of women's sexuality to their public role.
  • Used religion: Sita, Draupadi as models of courage. Women's duty: look after the home, but also spin the charkha and picket liquor shops. Men and women equal but with different roles; women physically weaker but greater in strength of soul. By not challenging gender roles directly he disarmed male opposition: strategically brilliant, philosophically limited.

14 · Women: organisations, legislation, the 1940s and the verdict (no class notes)#

Story link

Because the INC would not take up women's issues, women built their own organisations, but put the nation first. Ask: what did legislation give and what remained unfinished?

4.6 Women's organisations

  • Began in the 1900s, independent of INC. Sarala Debi Chaudhrani founded the Bharat Stree Mandal (1910) with branches across India for women's education.
  • All India Women's Conference (AIWC, 1927), founded under Margaret Cousins, an Irish feminist. Took up women's issues directly: education, political rights, marriage reform, women workers' rights. Methods moderate (lobbying, petitions). It placed the INM before women's issues: nationalist first, feminist second.

4.7 Legislative progress

  • 1921-30: several provincial legislatures used powers under the GOI Act (1919) to grant women the vote, subject to property qualifications. GOI Act (1935) extended the vote and gave reserved seats.
  • Women leaders wanted Universal Adult Franchise (making reservation unnecessary): it came only with the Constitution of 1950.
  • Sarda Act (1929), formally the Child Marriage Restraint Act: minimum marriage age 14 for girls, 18 for males. 1930s: bills on property rights, inheritance, divorce, dowry, prostitution passed in central and provincial legislatures.

4.8 The 1940s: the final phase

  • QIM (1942): great participation, especially in rural areas. Lifting of the ban on CPI 1925 in 1942 aided women's participation, many being CPI members since the 1920s-30s.
  • Bengal 1942: leftist women organised the Mahila Atmaraksha Samiti (1942) and did famine relief in the Bengal Famine (1943).
  • Tebhaga Movement (1946), Bengal: sharecroppers demanding two-thirds of produce; high participation by Dalit and tribal women, who fought the British and gave lives.
  • INA: 1,500 women joined the Rani Jhansi Regiment, fought in the Imphal Campaign (1944). Women also took part in the Muslim League's Pakistan campaign.

4.9 The honest assessment

  • Women fought more for nationalism than for their own rights: movements asked them to serve a national cause without addressing their specific conditions. INC accepted participation gratefully and gave women's issues low priority.
  • The Constitution of 1950 gave full political equality; the social and economic transformation demanded by Tarabai Shinde and Pandita Ramabai in the 1880s is still unfinished.
  • Thread: every crisis called on women to sacrifice (Rani Jhansi Regiment in Burma, Tebhaga women who died, picketers jailed), and every time their own demands were deferred.

15 · Peasants in the 1930s: Depression, AIKS and the INC alliance (no class notes)#

Story link

Because prices halved but rent, revenue and debt did not, peasants turned radical in the 1930s. Ask: who gave that anger an organisation?

5.1 Why did peasants become more radical in the 1930s?

  • 1920s movements were local (one district, one landlord, one grievance); even Bardoli (1928) stayed within the Gandhian framework.
  • First: the Great Depression; between 1929 and 1933 agricultural prices fell by roughly half (Rs 10 became Rs 5) while rent, land revenue and debt did not: mass debt and land loss.
  • Second: CDM (1930-34) drew peasants into politics through no-tax and no-rent campaigns; its 1934 withdrawal left them politically conscious but organisationally homeless.
  • Third: the left moved in. CSP (1934) and CPI invested in rural organisation and gave an ideology: 'the zamindari system itself must go'.

5.2 The All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS), 1936

  • Founded at Lucknow on 11 April 1936, timed with the INC session. Swami Sahajanand Saraswati (Bihar) first President; N.G. Ranga first General Secretary.
  • Kisan Manifesto, August 1936: abolish zamindari; tax agricultural wealth on a graduated scale; cancel hereditary debts; grant occupancy rights to tenants (the legal right to stay on and till land; without it a tenant could be evicted at will). Beyond what INC would adopt.
  • Bihar: most militant; central fight over Bakasht lands (tenant plots seized by zamindars during Depression years when tenants could not pay rent). Sahajanand led the Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha (BPKS), founded by him in 1929. Andhra: N.G. Ranga against zamindari and revenue hikes.
  • Bengal: separate vehicle, the Krishak Praja Party (KPP), founded by A.K. Fazlul Huq in 1936, an independent party (not part of AIKS) of Muslim peasants, mainly East Bengal, against largely Hindu zamindars and moneylenders. Demands: abolish zamindari, debt relief, end the Permanent Settlement. Won 36 seats in the 1937 Bengal assembly, then allied with the Muslim League, not INC.
Handout alert
  • Handout alert: Sec. 1.11 says AIKS was established in Bihar; Sec. 5.2 gives Lucknow, 11 April 1936. Use Lucknow; Bihar was Sahajanand's base. Prelims 2019 match: AIKS-Swami Sahajanand Saraswati is correct.

5.3 The INC and the peasant movement

  • Karachi Session, March 1931: president Sardar Patel, resolution drafted by Nehru. Defined Swaraj for the masses: political freedom must include economic freedom. Demands: substantial cut in land revenue and rent, relief for uneconomic holdings, cancellation of agricultural debt, a living wage for workers, state ownership of key industries. The colonial government spread rumours of communist money reaching Karachi.
  • Faizpur Session, 1936: the first INC session held in a village; AIKS and INC held a joint meeting; Kisan demands shaped the INC's 1937 manifesto. Strong INC result: clear majorities in most provinces (not in Bengal, Punjab, Assam, Sindh, NWFP).

16 · Congress ministries, the split and the 1940s peasant upsurge (no class notes)#

Story link

Because governing is not protesting, the INC ministries balanced zamindars and tenants and the Kisan Sabha moved to the CPI. Ask: how did war turn that drift into an upsurge?

5.4 Congress ministries (1937-39): from agitation to administration

  • 28 months of INC rule: the first real governing, for a coalition of peasants, workers, professionals, merchants, industrialists and zamindars.
  • In Bihar and UP the ministries passed incremental agrarian laws (relief for tenants in arrears, protection against eviction, rent cuts) and did not abolish zamindari overnight (risk of agrarian chaos).
  • Bakasht agitation, Bihar: under the BPKS peasants launched Satyagrahas to reclaim seized lands (entering fields, harvesting). The ministry under Sri Krishna Sinha negotiated a settlement: part of the land returned to tenants, zamindars got legal stability on the rest. When the agitation turned volatile, police were used. By August 1939, concessions, legislation and about 600 arrests ended it.
  • Rahul Sankrityayan, scholar-communist and prominent Bihar peasant leader, was arrested in this period for a Satyagraha. His more famous two-year Hazaribagh jail term (1940) came after the ministries resigned (October 1939), under British authority.
  • The left called it betrayal; the balanced reading: an elected government of conflicting interests cannot fully satisfy one group without shortchanging another.

5.5 The split: INC steps back, CPI steps in

  • Haripura Session, 1938, president Subhas Chandra Bose: formal resolution on Kisan Sabhas reaffirmed peasants' right to organise but said Congress could not associate with activities incompatible with its principles and would not allow Congressmen who were Kisan Sabha members to create a hostile atmosphere; Provincial Congress Committees to act. Passed with little discussion.
  • Many activists chose AIKS; the CPI, working inside both CSP and AIKS, filled the space. AIKS kept growing: over 800,000 members by 1939.

5.6 The 1940s: war, Quit India and the peasant upsurge

  • CPI took organisational control of AIKS: by the early 1940s in practice a mass organisation of the Communist Party. Sahajanand Saraswati and N.G. Ranga left AIKS, unable to follow the CPI's pro-British People's War line after 1941.
  • War worsened rural distress: grain requisitioning, forced procurement at below-market prices, inflation, shortages; the Bengal Famine (1943) (link Ch 45) was the worst expression.
  • QIM (1942) (link Ch 44): entire INC leadership arrested within hours of the 8 August resolution; peasants were the backbone, the 'soul' of QIM: cut telegraph lines, disrupted rail, attacked police stations, set up parallel governments (Satara pratisarkar, Ballia, Midnapore uprisings). Nationalist outburst and class struggle fused, since the revenue machinery, zamindar's rent collector and moneylender's ledger were one system.
  • War-end 1945: a radicalised, desperate, experienced peasantry exploded into post-war uprisings.

17 · Post-war peasant movements (1945-1951) (no class notes)#

Story link

Because the war left peasants radical and desperate, 1945 onwards brought five distinct revolts. Ask: which served national integration, and which went outside the Gandhian framework?

5.7 Post-war peasant movements

MovementWhere / whenCore facts
Worli UprisingThane district, Bombay Presidency, 1945-47Worlis: impoverished tribal community treated as bonded labour by landlords, moneylenders, forest contractors. Grievance: Veth Begar (forced unpaid labour). Led by organisers like Godavari Parulekar; a unified strike, refusal to perform forced labour; landlords yielded by 1947.
Bakasht re-occupationsBihar, 1946-47Second, intense phase of the 1930s struggle; evicted peasants demanded restoration of hereditary tenancy; led again by Swami Sahajanand Saraswati; marched on Bakasht lands and planted crops against zamindars' private armies and police. Forced the Bihar Bakasht Disputes Settlement Act (1947).
Punnapra-VayalarTravancore (Kerala), October 1946Dewan Sir C.P. Ramaswami Iyer planned an independent Travancore allied to the British Crown. Coir-factory workers, fishermen, agricultural labourers led by local CPI and CSP cadres, armed with sharpened bamboo sticks, set up self-rule camps at Punnapra and Vayalar. State military with British machine guns crushed it: about 1,000 killed. The Dewan's authority shattered; he fled, ensuring Travancore's integration into India.
TebhagaBengal, 1946-47'Three parts': landlord's share cut from 1/2 to 1/3, tenant keeps 2/3 (Prelims 2013, (a)). Basis: Floud Commission (1940), formally the Bengal Land Revenue Commission, which recommended abolition of the Permanent Settlement; the Bengal government never implemented it.
Telangana Armed StruggleHyderabad, 1946-51Exploitation by the Nizam's feudal landlords (Deshmukhs) via forced labour (Vetti) and evictions; early resistance against the Razakar militia aided national integration. After Operation Polo (1948) the CPI told peasants to refuse disarmament and fight the new Republic; the Indian state suppressed it by 1951.
Key takeaway
  • Tebhaga and Telangana operated entirely outside the Gandhian framework.

18 · Historiography of the peasant upsurge (no class notes)#

Story link

Because the same two decades can be read two ways, Mains asks you to hold both. Ask: was the peasant upsurge part of the anti-colonial struggle or a parallel class war?

5.8 Two ways of looking at the peasant upsurge

Bipan Chandra (Nationalist)Sekhar Bandyopadhyay (Subaltern / Critical)

Key takeaway
  • Synthesis: INC was genuinely anti-colonial and brought peasants into national politics, but it was a multi-class coalition that could not govern without managing propertied interests. The journey from Gandhian CDM (1930) to AIKS (1936) to armed communist rebellion (1946) shows the unresolved tension; the class struggle in the countryside was left to the early years of the Republic.
Handout alert
  • Mains skeleton from the FYQ: 'The INM, while presenting itself as national, marginalised the voices of workers, capitalists, Dalits and women in different ways. Examine.' Four themes: workers (AITUC tolerated, trusteeship vs class struggle), capitalists (funded, but muted publicly), Dalits (Mahad, RTC, Communal Award, Poona, 1956 conversion), women (AIWC, 'lawbreakers not lawmakers'). Verdict: strategic absorption, not genuine representation.
