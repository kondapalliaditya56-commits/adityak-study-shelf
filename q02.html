2003PYQ 1Shah Alam II's capital question

Assertion (A): Shah Alam II spent the initial years as an Emperor far away from his capital. Reason (R): There was always a lurking danger of foreign invasion from the North-West frontier.

Answer: (C)

Why (c) is correct: Assertion A is true — Shah Alam II60-06 did spend the initial years of his reign far from Delhi. But Reason R is false — the cause was fear of his own Wazir, Imad-ul-Mulk, not fear of foreign invasion from the North-West frontier. An emperor afraid to sit in his own capital because of his own minister is the emblem of Mughal decline. You will find this in 'What did India look like by the mid-18th century?' (◆ Takeaway).

Why the other options are wrong:

  • (a) wrong — R is false; the threat was internal (Wazir), not external (frontier).
  • (b) wrong — R is false (same reason).
  • (d) wrong — A is true; Shah Alam II60-06 did keep away from Delhi for years.

2017PYQ 1 Fragmented polity in mid-18th centuryMains 2017 · 10 marks · 150 words

"Clarify how mid-eighteenth-century India was beset with the spectre of a fragmented polity."

Demand of the question: Show how India was a fragmented polity by the mid-eighteenth century. The verb " clarify " means make the case clearly with evidence. The phrase " s pectre of a fragmented polity " tells you the question accepts the premise as a recognised condition; your job is to show how. This chapter (Mughal decline) supplies the cause half — combine with regional-powers chapters for the evidence half.

Suggested intro (~25 words): Mid-eighteenth-century India was indeed beset by the spectre of a fragmented polity — the Mughal Empire had collapsed at the centre and many separate regional powers had filled the space.

Guiding themes (pick 4–5 for a 150-word answer):

1. Mughal collapse — the cause (this chapter is the primary anchor):

  • Simultaneous weakening across the State-power components.
  • Aurangzeb58-07's Deccan wars.
  • Mansabdari break + Jagirdari crisis.
  • Treaty of Bhopal 1738 + Nadir Shah's Battle of Karnal 1739.
  • 1752 emperor under Maratha protection + 1754 Alamgir II54-59 installed by Marathas.

2. Successor States — Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad (see Chapter 3, Successor States).

3. Maratha Confederacy (see Chapter 4, Rise of Marathas).

4. Jats + Afghans — three rebel states (see Chapter 5 — 'The Jat Kingdom (1695-1763)' + 'Farrukhabad (est. 1714)' + 'Rohilkhand (est. 1737–40)').

5. Sikh State (see Chapter 6 — 'How did Banda Bahadur08-15 establish the first Sikh State — and why did it collapse?' + 'What kept the Sikh State alive after Banda Bahadur08-15's defeat?' + 'How did Ranjit Singh01-39 unify the Misls and build a powerful Sikh State?' + 'What were the terms of the 1809 Treaty of Amritsar with the EIC?').

6. Mysore + Travancore + Rajputs (see Chapter 7 — 'Akbar56-05 and Rajputs' through 'What made Tipu82-99 a different kind of ruler — and why did he still lose?').

Suggested conclusion (~20 words): The fragmented polity that haunted mid-eighteenth-century India was already a settled reality — what remained was not one India but many.

2014PYQ 2 Why were so many empire-shaking battles fought at Panipat?Mains 2014 · 10 marks · 150 words

"The third battle of Panipat was fought in 1761. Why were so many empire-shaking battles fought at Panipat?"

Demand of the question: Explain why this one place — Panipat — kept becoming the site of empire-deciding battles. You must cover the geography (terrain) and the historical pattern (1526, 1556, 1761). The verb " w hy " calls for causal explanation.

Suggested intro (~25 words): Many empire-shaking battles were fought at Panipat because Panipat was India's natural Delhi gateway from the northwest — and whoever won there won the throne, as the third battle of Panipat in 1761 again confirmed.

Guiding themes (pick 4–5 for a 150-word answer):

1. Geography — Delhi gateway — Khyber Pass → Panipat–Karnal–Kurukshetra–Thanesar belt → Yamuna-Ganga doab (see Chapter 4, Marathas; see Chapter 5, Afghans).

2. 1st Panipat 1526 — Babur26-30 vs Ibrahim Lodi → birth of Mughal Empire (see Chapter 1, Mughal Background).

3. 2nd Panipat 1556 — Akbar56-05 vs Hemu → consolidation of Mughal rule (see Chapter 1, Mughal Background).

4. 3rd Panipat 1761 — Abdali vs Marathas → peak-and-break of Maratha imperial expansion (see Chapter 4 — Marathas; see Chapter 5, 'Shahu19-49's Release — was Mughal "Pacifist Policy" really pacifist?' for Afghan-side coalition).

5. Sikh-side resistance to Afghan invasions (see Chapter 6 — 'How did Ranjit Singh01-39 unify the Misls and build a powerful Sikh State?').

Suggested conclusion (~20 words): The empire-shaking battles fought at Panipat — 1526, 1556, 1761 — happened because controlling Panipat meant controlling the Delhi throne.

FYQ 1 Trace the link between Aurangzeb's policies and the early-eighteenth-century weakening of Mughal central authority.Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

Demand: Identify Aurangzeb's specific policies (Deccan wars, Jizya, temple destruction, executions) and weigh how each contributed to the weakening of central Mughal authority after 1707.

Guiding themes:

1. Deccan wars (1681–1707) — Aurangzeb's 26-year campaign exhausted the Mughal treasury, drained the army, and stretched the bureaucracy thin (see 'Why did Aurangzeb58-07's wars sow the seeds of decline?').

2. Religious policies — Jizya re-imposition (1679), destruction of select temples (Kashi Vishwanath 1669), and the executions of Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675) & Sambhaji (1689) hardened Sikh and Maratha resistance (see 'How did Aurangzeb58-07's religious policies erode the social foundations of the empire?').

3. Mansabdari decay — personal loyalty to Aurangzeb did not transfer to his weaker successors; the Sawar-fraud and chronic salary arrears destroyed army morale (see 'Why did the Mansabdari system — the empire's own backbone — turn against it?' and 'Why did the Mughal military weakened?').

4. Jagirdari crisis — too many Mansabdars chasing too few productive Jagirs after the Deccan annexations; the result was a fight for revenue rather than service to the State (see 'What was the Jagirdari crisis, & why did it hit so hard?').

5. Conclusion: Aurangzeb's policies did not collapse the Empire by themselves, but they fatally weakened the four institutional pillars Akbar built (see 'Proof of decline — what did factionalism look like in practice?'). The 1707 succession of weak rulers met an Empire already structurally hollow.

FYQ 2 Discuss the role of Nadir Shah's invasion (1739) & the Battle of Karnal in accelerating Mughal decline.Mains-FYQ · 10 marks · 150 words

Demand: Examine how the 1739 invasion converted the Mughal Empire from a weakening centre to a hollow shell.

Guiding themes:

1. Context — by 1739 the Mughal centre was already fragile after three decades of weak post-Aurangzeb succession + the Treaty of Bhopal 1738 (see 'Proof of decline — how the Marathas exposed the military collapse').

2. The Battle of Karnal (Feb 1739) — Nadir Shah of Persia defeated Mughal Emperor Muhammad Shah in less than three hours; the Mughal army was technologically and organisationally obsolete (see 'What did Nadir Shah's invasion reveal — the five-pillar blow').

3. The sack of Delhi (March 1739) — Nadir Shah looted the Peacock Throne, Kohinoor diamond, and an estimated 700 million rupees from Delhi; the Mughal court's prestige collapsed visibly across India.

4. Strategic consequences — Mughal authority over Punjab and Afghan territories west of the Indus was permanently lost (formalised by Nadir Shah's treaty), and regional powers (Marathas, Hyderabad, Awadh) saw the centre's weakness clearly (see 'What did Nadir Shah's invasion reveal — the five-pillar blow').

5. Conclusion: Karnal 1739 was the moment when the Mughal Empire transitioned from a weakening centre to a hollow shell. Within a generation the British East India Company would step into the vacuum left behind.
