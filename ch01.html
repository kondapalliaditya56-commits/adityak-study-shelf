The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: A State is an organisation, and power centralises in a strong personality: Babur's guns founded a Mughal State, Akbar built its machinery (Mansabdari and land revenue), and the machine ran on personal loyalty, so when the King could not deliver, it began to hollow out.

  1. A State is an organisation. Power centralises in a strong personality, and when that personality weakens power leaves either vertically downwards (decentralisation) or sideways to another personality (horizontal displacement, as Kodak gave way to smartphones). A State is the organisation that rules a territory (the country) and its people (the nation), by making laws, implementing them and settling disputes (L, E, J). Sovereignty adds a symbolic head of State.
    → Now watch one such organisation arrive in India.
  2. The Mughal ledger. There are fifteen Mughal monarchs, from Babur 26-30 to Bahadur Shah Zafar 37-57, and the reign codes pass the baton from one to the next. Humayun 30-40 is the only one with two reign periods. The teacher writes the Mughal State as 1526-1858 with an intermission 1540-55.
    → It all starts with a prince who has lost his home.
  3. A prince without a home. Babur 26-30 was born in 1483 in Fergana, a Timurid descendant (the Mughals are 'Mongol' in Persian, but they took pride in Timur, who captured Delhi in 1398). He lost Fergana by 1512, and Kabul (1504) was economically poor. India was rich but politically fragile under the Lodis, and with Ottoman gunners he began attacking from 1519.
    → So he comes down through the Khyber Pass.
  4. Panipat, 1526. Babur 26-30 had about 12,000 men but small bronze cannons, and Ibrahim Lodi was killed. The Delhi Sultanate (1206-1526) ended and the Mughal Kingdom was born, a kingdom and not yet an empire. Panipat saw battles in 1526, 1556 and 1761 because it lay near the Khyber Pass, in the corridor between the Thar Desert and the Himalayan foothills, with the Yamuna as a choke-point.
    → But a kingdom is easier to win than to keep.
  5. The son loses it. Humayun 30-40 was defeated by Sher Shah 40-45 Suri 40-45 at Chausa (1539) and Kanauj (1540), and the Suri dynasty (1540-55) pushed the Mughals out. Sher Shah's three reforms outlasted him: the Patta and Qabuliyat revenue system, the silver Rupiya, and the Grand Trunk Road from Peshawar to Sonargaon.
    → Humayun now needs an ally.
  6. Persia's price. The Safavids of Iran helped, at a price: Shia forms, Kandahar ceded, Persian culture promoted. With 14,000 Iranian troops Humayun 30-40 won at Sirhind (1555), but in January 1556 he fell from the stairs of his library and died. Hemu seized Delhi and declared himself Samrat, and Bairam Khan, guardian of the 13-year-old Akbar 56-05, defeated him at the Second Panipat (1556).
    → Now a boy emperor must build the machine.
  7. Akbar's machine: the Mansabdar. Every Mansabdar held a mansab (a post), with a military and civil role, and two ranks: Zat (personal) plus Sawar (horsemen to maintain). Lineage mattered more than merit, with Birbal the exception. Pay came in cash (Naqdi) or by Jagir, and Mughal land was Jagir, Khalisa, Inam or Mashrut; four-fifths of LR came from Jagirs. A Tankha Jagir was transferable, capped and confiscated on death, while a Watan Jagir was hereditary with 10% Peshkash.
    → Someone has to fill the treasury that pays them.
  8. Land revenue: who gets what. Four stakeholders hold rights in land: owner, tenant, collector and State. The State assessed through the Patwari, Qanungo, Amil and Amalguzar, settled with a Patta and a Qabuliyat, and collected through the Zamindar (about 10% as Nankar / Malikana) and the village Muqaddam. LR was a share of gross produce, and the Khudkasht had occupancy rights while the Pahikasht had none.
    → But a machine run on personal loyalty has a flaw.
  9. The flaw. Mansabdari was a patron-client bond: the King gave rank and Jagir, the Mansabdar gave loyalty and an army. The loyalty was personal, not institutional. After 1707, if the King could not deliver the desired Mansab and Jagir, loyalty dissolved, and the end state was an army of 2 lakh on paper and 1 lakh in reality.
    → A short-of-cash State then reaches for the auction.
  10. The auction door. Ijaradari (revenue farming) arises from need (war, bad harvest, a short treasury) or greed. The Ijaradar paid in advance, worked for profit on a contract of 3 to 5 years, and gave the peasant no Patta. It began as the Muqatai of the Delhi Sultanate, was practically abolished by Akbar 56-05 and then revived by weaker rulers (Jahangir, Shahjahan, Aurangzeb, Jahandar Shah, Farukhsiyar) until Warren Hastings 73-85 formalised it as the Farming System in 1772. When the Zamindar (leader) and the ryots (followers) are both unhappy, a strong revolt follows, as with Churaman 95-21 Jat about 1695.
    → Meanwhile Akbar tried to hold his diverse realm by faith.
  11. Akbar's faith ladder. Akbar 56-05 built the Ibadat Khana at Fatehpur Sikri (1575), issued the Mahzar (1579) making the Emperor the final word on faith, and set up the Din-i-Ilahi (1582), a religio-political experiment rather than a movement. Behind them all stood Sulh-i-Kul, universal peace through equal respect for every faith. Jizya, by contrast, was first imposed in Sindh by Muhammad bin Qasim and institutionalised by Qutb-ud-din Aibak 06-10 and his successors.

In simple words#

  • A State is an organisation that rules a territory (country) and its people (nation)
  • power tends to centralise in a strong personality and leaves when it weakens
  • Babur 26-30 won Panipat (1526) with small bronze cannons
  • Humayun 30-40 lost to Sher Shah 40-45 and returned with Safavid help, and Bairam Khan won Panipat (1556) for Akbar
  • Akbar 56-05 built the Mansabdari System (Zat + Sawar, paid by Jagir or cash) and ran land revenue by Zabti (assessment by the State, collection by the Zamindar for about 10%).
  • The system's fatal weakness was personal loyalty: if the King could not deliver the desired Mansab and Jagir, loyalty dissolved and armies hollowed out.
  • Weak rulers then revived Ijaradari (revenue farming by auction), abolished by Akbar and formalised by Warren Hastings 73-85 as the Farming System in 1772.

Feudal, non-modern and modern

  • Feudal = old in time and regressive.
  • Modern = usually new in time and progressive / better / more efficient.
  • Old is not always feudal or backward.
  • Old can be gold (the handout's example: Lata Mangeshkar).
  • Raja Ram Mohun Roy, the Father of Modern India, wanted a return to the pure past of Vedantic traditions.
  • Teacher's ladder: Feudal → Non-modern (the transition period) → Modern.

Power and its six forms

  • Power = Economic + Political (decision-making) + Military + Social (influence over people, or awe in their hearts) + Intellectual/Technological + Spiritual.
  • A rise or fall in one form of power drives a rise or fall in the others.
  • Example from the handout: a social-media influencer's popularity raises his or her economic power, while a scandal can hurt a politician's political power.

It is in the nature of power to centralise in a strong personality

  • Power centralises itself in a strong personality.
  • That personality can be a human or an institution.
  • When the personality weakens, power leaves in one of two ways: vertically downwards (decentralisation) or by horizontal displacement (another personality replaces it).

Country, nation, state

  • Country = territory (Desh).
  • State = the organisation that rules or governs a country and its people (Rajya).
  • To rule = to make laws, implement them and settle disputes (L, E, J) over domestic affairs. That is also called governance.
  • Nation = the summation of people who are one (fraternity), equal (equality) and supreme (liberty).
  • This is the definition given by the French Revolution (1789).
  • The teacher links it to "We the People" in the Preamble = Nation (Rashtra).
Key takeaway
  • State rules a territory and its people. Country = the territory. Nation = the people.
  • The State talks, trades or fights with the outside world, but it rules only its own country and people.

State structure

  • Teacher's tree: Symbolic sovereign (= President, Head of State) sits over the Legislature, Executive and Judiciary.
  • This structure repeats at the Centre and at the Province.
  • The Executive = the Council of Ministers, which works through military servants and civil servants.
  • The handout says the primary purpose of any constitution is to create the State structure.
  • A modern State structure has separation of power, a well-defined internal structure with clear goals and functions, and mechanisms that keep the whole structure stable from top to bottom.
  • A non-modern State structure has no institutional separation of power and is prone to instability.
Source
  • Student confusion resolved (handout). NCERT says a Modern State has "centralised power exercised over a clearly defined territory".
  • In class we say a Modern State has separation of power. Both are right.
  • A State can be vertically centralised (a strong Union Government with authority over the provinces) and at the same time have horizontal separation of powers at the Union level: Parliament makes laws, the Prime Minister implements them, the Supreme Court resolves disputes.
  • Like parents who centralise power over the home, yet divide it between mother and father.

"Republic of India" and the point of Modern Indian History

  • "Republic of India" = the name of the State (organisation) ruling the Territory and People of India since 26 January 1950.
  • Key goal of the Constitution = to define and create this State (organisation).
  • MIH = the history of the States that ruled the territory of India and its people, for example the Mughal State, the Maratha State, the Awadh State, and the Company State by the EIC.
  • INM (Indian National Movement) = a people's movement.
  • Its goal: end the British colonial State and establish the Nation State (the Republic of India).

3 · Mughal take-over from the Lodis (Babur 26-30)#

Story link

Because the Lodis were fragile and Kabul was poor, a prince from Fergana turned to India. Ask: what beat numbers at Panipat?

Who were the Mughals?

  • Babur 26-30 was born in 1483 to Umar Shaikh Mirza, ruler of Fergana, a semi-autonomous State in present-day Uzbekistan, roughly half the size of modern Haryana.
  • Fergana belonged to the Timurid Empire, and Babur 26-30 was a descendant of Timur (Tamerlane).
  • "Mughal" is a Persian corruption of "Mongol".
  • The Mughals were a Turko-Mongol dynasty.
SideAncestorEmpire
MaternalGenghis Khan (1206–27)Mongol Empire (1206–1368)
PaternalTimur (Tamerlane) (1370–1405)Timurid Empire (1370–1507)
Self-identityTimuridsPride in Timur, who had captured Delhi in 1398
  • They took greater pride in the paternal ancestor, so they called themselves Timurids, not Mughals.

Why Babur 26-30 turned to India

  • Babur 26-30 came to power young but could not hold Fergana
  • By 1512 his Central Asian ambitions had collapsed
  • He held Kabul (captured 1504), but Kabul was economically poor.
  • India was wealthy but politically fragile under the Lodi dynasty.
  • With artillery support from the Ottoman Empire (expert gunners), Babur 26-30 began attacking the Delhi Sultanate from 1519.

  • Babur 26-30's map: Kabul = "eco" (poor). The Khyber Pass is underlined.
  • The dates 1526 (Panipat), 1528 (Agra) and 1529 (Gwalior and the march east) are underlined.

First Battle of Panipat (1526)

  • Babur 26-30 entered India through the Khyber Pass and met Ibrahim Lodi, the last ruler of the Lodi dynasty (1451–1526), on the plains of Panipat.
  • Babur 26-30's army was smaller (about 12,000 men), but his small bronze cannons were decisive, a technology the Lodis could not match.
  • Lodi was killed, the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) ended and the Mughal Kingdom was born.
  • This was still a kingdom, not yet an empire.
  • It became an empire under Akbar 56-05 and reached its greatest extent under Aurangzeb 58-07.
Teacher's board
  • Tech power. Babur 26-30's edge over a fragile Lodi Sultanate was technology, not numbers.
  • The teacher underlines "small bronze cannons", "about 12,000" and "Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526)".

Why three great battles happened at Panipat

  • Three of the most consequential battles in Indian history, 1526, 1556 and 1761, were all fought at Panipat.
  • Geography chose Panipat
  • The commanders merely followed:
  • 1. Panipat lay close to the Khyber Pass, the primary invasion route from Central Asia.
  • 2. It sat in the narrow corridor between the Thar Desert (south) and the Himalayan foothills (north), a natural bottleneck.
  • 3. The Yamuna to the east acted as a defensive choke-point, ruling out flanking manoeuvres.

4 · Humayun 30-40 and Sher Shah 40-45 Suri 40-45#

Story link

Because a kingdom is easier to win than to keep, the son lost it to Sher Shah. Ask: which reforms of the winner did Akbar later build on?

  • Babur 26-30 died in 1530
  • His son Humayun 30-40 succeeded
  • Sher Shah 40-45 Suri 40-45 was an Afghan-Pathan, born Farid Khan in Bihar, c. 1486.
  • He was no foreign invader: his ancestors migrated to India under the Lodis and settled as Jagirdars in Bihar.
  • He won the title Sher Khan by killing a tiger single-handed while serving Bahar Khan Lohani (Governor of Bihar under the Lodis till 1526, then independent Sultan Md Lohani, who refused Mughal sovereignty).
  • Sher Shah 40-45 defeated Humayun 30-40 at Chausa (1539) and Kanauj (1540), founded the Suri dynasty (1540–55) and pushed the Mughals out of India entirely.

6 · How Akbar 56-05 built the Mughal State: the Mansabdari System#

Story link

Because the kingdom was re-won, Akbar built a machine to run it. Ask: who is a Mansabdar, how is he paid, and what is the fatal weakness?

  • Babur 26-30 founded the Mughal Kingdom. Humayun 30-40 nearly lost it.
  • Akbar 56-05, ruling for fifty years (1556–1605), built the machinery of the Mughal State: the Mansabdari System, a military-bureaucracy that decided how the State was administered, how its army was maintained and how the King kept control over a vast and diverse territory.

What is a Mansabdar?

  • Mansab = a post or rank. Mansabdar = the holder of the post.
  • Every Mansabdar had a dual function: military and civil-administrative.
  • The same man who commanded troops also governed territory.
  • That is why it is called a military-bureaucracy.

  • A DM or minister = mansabdar
  • DM-ship or minister-ship = mansab.
  • Mansabdars = the ministers and senior officers of the Mughal State.

Rank: Zat + Sawar

  • Zat = personal rank, governing seniority and status.
  • Sawar = the number of horsemen he must maintain and provide to the King.
  • Net rank = Zat + Sawar. Example: Zat 2,000 + Sawar 3,000 = net rank 5,000.

How Mansabdars were paid: Jagir, Khalisa, Inam

  • A Mansabdar could be paid in cash (a Naqdi Mansabdar) or by a Jagir: a specific piece of land whose land revenue he pocketed directly.
  • This revenue paid his salary and covered the cost of maintaining his army.
  • A Jagir given in lieu of salary = a Tankha Jagir
  • Its holder = a Jagirdar
Handout alert

Iqtadar of the Delhi Sultanate is like the Jagirdar Mansabdar of the Mughals. (Iqta = Jagir in Persian.)

  • Mughal land was divided into these categories:
  • 1. Jagir land: revenue to Mansabdars.
  • 2. Khalisa land: royal land. Revenue goes directly to the King and pays Naqdi Mansabdars plus other State expenses.
  • 3. Inam land: gifted land. Revenue goes to a chosen beneficiary (for example a man of exceptional talent) to sustain himself.
  • 4. Mashrut Jagir: a temporary "top-up" or extra allowance given to an official to cover the extra costs of a specific, difficult assignment.
  • It is revoked automatically when the assignment ends.
  • (Like "base salary" versus "the bonus".)
  • By the early 18th century there were over 8,000 Mansabdars, and four-fifths of all LR came from Jagirs, only one-fifth from Khalisa land.

Three rules for Tankha Jagirs

  • 1. Transferable. The King could rotate Jagirs among Mansabdars, or convert a Jagirdar into a Naqdi Mansabdar at will.
  • 2. Capped extraction. A Jagirdar could collect only the pre-determined LR, not a rupee more.
  • Royal supervision existed to prevent oppression of peasants and zamindars.
  • 3. Confiscation on death. On the death of a Tankha Jagirdar, both the Jagir and all accumulated wealth were confiscated.
  • The predictable result: Mansabdars spent lavishly in their lifetimes, since saving made little sense.

  • Teacher's example split:

Watan Jagir

  • Watan = hereditary and permanent. Unlike Tankha Jagirs, a Watan Jagir could be passed down and was not subject to rotation or confiscation.
  • Given mainly to Rajput chieftains and autonomous rulers who had submitted to the Emperor, so they could collect revenue in their ancestral domains in exchange for military service and loyalty.
  • The holder paid 10% of LR to the King as Peshkash (tribute), acknowledging the King's ultimate authority and compensating him for giving up his right to reclaim the Jagir.

  • Watan is hereditary, given to a Mansabdar doing duty for the King, and passes to heirs.

What held the system together, and its fatal weakness

  • The system ran on a patron-client relationship: the King gave rank and Jagir, and the Mansabdar pledged personal loyalty and maintained the army.
  • This co-opted powerful individuals who might have challenged royal authority.
  • But the loyalty was personal, not institutional.
  • A Mansabdar's loyalty was to the King as a person, not to the State as an institution or to any law.
  • It was transactional and conditional: give and take.
  • If the King could not deliver Mansab and Jagir (land shortage, financial pressure, weak authority), the loyalty dissolved, and dissolved loyalty meant political instability.
  • This differs from a modern State, where bureaucrats and military officers owe their position to merit and service rules: impersonal loyalty to institution and law.
  • Because the whole military strength depended on Mansabdars actually maintaining the Sawar armies, weakened royal supervision meant hollowed-out armies on paper, far fewer men in reality, a silent erosion of State power from within.

Physical collection and the Zabti system

  • Muqaddams collected from cultivators. In zamindari tracts they deposited to Zamindars (who extracted through their own Amlas).
  • Where there were no intermediaries, Muqaddams paid directly to the State.
  • In later periods contractual revenue farmers, the Ijaradars, also collected (covered below).
  • Under Akbar 56-05's Zabti system, the State did not trust Zamindars with autonomous authority.
  • State officials (Amil at pargana level, under the Amalguzar at sarkar level) scientifically measured the land and fixed the exact demand.
  • The land records were archived by the Qanungo at pargana level.
  • LR was calculated as a percentage of gross produce (more LR from more fertile areas).
  • Mughal LR demand was a percentage of Gross Produce, not Net Produce.
  • Subtracting the cost of cultivation before taxing was a British reform, much later, and only after the gross-produce method had caused widespread agrarian ruin.

Revenue farming: Muqatai, Ijaradari, Farming System

  • The logic: instead of collecting through the Zamindar network under State supervision, the right to collect all revenues from an area (land, traders, shopkeepers) was auctioned to the highest bidder, called an Ijaradar or Revenue Farmer.
  • A farmer, in the economic sense, works with a profit motive
  • A peasant works for food security
  • The Ijaradar was not a cultivator but a merchant or moneylender who bid purely for profit. He farmed revenue, not land.
  • The Ijaradar paid the bid amount, fully or partially in advance, to the Jagirdar (on Jagir land) or to the State (on Khalisa land), then collected his investment plus profit from the peasants and traders.
  • Contracts were typically 3 to 5 years.
  • His incentive was to extract as much as possible within that window, with zero interest in irrigation, peasant welfare or long-term growth.
  • If he failed to deliver, the contract was re-auctioned.
  • Ijaradars were urban bankers and merchants with no roots in the countryside: no long-term stake in the land, no relationship with the peasants.

  • Akbar 56-05's approach to religion shaped the political character of Mughal rule for generations.
  • He moved in a clear sequence:
  • 1. Ibadat Khana (1575). A House of Worship at Fatehpur Sikri where scholars from different faiths debated.
  • It exposed him to the diversity of religious thought and to the bitter rivalries among the Ulama.
  • 2. Mahzar, the Infallibility Decree (1579). It gave Akbar 56-05 the authority to adjudicate religious disputes: the Emperor, not the clergy, was the final word on matters of faith.
  • 3. Din-i-Ilahi (1582). A syncretic spiritual order borrowing from Islam, Hinduism, Zoroastrianism and Christianity.
  • It is best understood as a religio-political experiment, not a movement: not many joined, but its real significance lay in the principle it expressed.
  • 4. Sulh-i-Kul: universal peace. The State must treat all religions with equal respect, and no single faith should dominate policy.

9 · Who actually introduced Jizya in India?#

Story link

Because religion shaped policy, here is a side question on Jizya. Ask: who first imposed it, and who institutionalised it?

  • Jizya is a tax on non-Muslims. It is often associated with the Delhi Sultanate, but it predates the Sultanate by several centuries.
  • Muhammad bin Qasim first imposed Jizya in Sindh during the 8th-century Arab conquest.
  • Qutb-ud-din Aibak 06-10 and his successors institutionalised it as a formal instrument of revenue and policy across the Delhi Sultanate from the early 13th century.
  • The distinction matters: Jizya arrived in India with the Arabs, but became a pan-subcontinental administrative practice only under the Sultanate.

11 · The teacher's revision boards on the land revenue (LR) system (Lecture 4)#

Story link

Last scene: the teacher redraws the land revenue machine on four boards. Ask: can you redraw the ladder, the survey, the four steps and the Amlas?

  • At the start of Lecture 4 the teacher redrew the whole LR machinery.
  • These four boards are the best one-page revision of Section 7.
Teacher's board
  • The ladder: Centre → Provinces (Suba) → Districts (Sarkar) → Blocks (Pargana) → Villages.
  • Inside a Jagir the Zamindar has his Milkiyat (his own holding) and the Khudkashts (cultivating peasants) sit on the rest.
  • Board title: auction of the right to collect LR, in Khalisa (KL) or Jagir.
Teacher's board
  • Field survey gathers:
    • (1) past trends of production and LR, area-wise
    • (2) average rainfall, droughts, floods and their frequency
    • (3) land records: which ryot cultivates which field, the size of each field, the LR paid in the past. Maintained by: Patwari (village, non-state actor) and Qanungo (block level).
  • Use of this data: assessment of LR ("how much LR should we demand from which field / ryot") by the Amil (pargana) and the Amalguzar (district).
  • Settlement: a Patta and LR exchanged with the ryot.

Any LR / tax system (teacher's four steps)

  • Assessment (how much, using the field-survey data)
  • Settlement: two documents, the Patta (with the ryot) and the Qabuliyat (with the State), like a rent contract with both parties
  • Collection: mostly by Zamindars (non-state actors), plus supervision / audit by government officers (Amil, Amalguzar)
  • Deposit: to the King (Khalisa) or to the Jagirdar Mansabdar
Teacher's board

Amlas = employees of the Zamindar. The Muqaddam (Mukhiya) collects LR from the villages for him.
