1 · Basics first: power, state, sovereignty#

The teacher opens with vocabulary. Every later chapter uses these words (power, state, sovereignty, centralisation), so this section is worth reading slowly once.

Teacher's board

Source for this course = only the Sarrthi IAS handout (the "book"). After the batch is over, add the new NCERTs (go to the Sarrthi IAS Foundation YouTube channel for those).

Feudal, non-modern and modern

  • Feudal = old in time and regressive. Modern = usually new in time and progressive / better / more efficient.
  • Old is not always feudal or backward. Old can be gold (the handout's example: Lata Mangeshkar). Raja Ram Mohun Roy, the Father of Modern India, wanted a return to the pure past of Vedantic traditions.
  • Teacher's ladder: Feudal → Non-modern (the transition period) → Modern.

Power and its six forms

Power = Economic + Political (decision-making) + Military + Social (influence over people, or awe in their hearts) + Intellectual/Technological + Spiritual.

A rise or fall in one form of power drives a rise or fall in the others. Example from the handout: a social-media influencer's popularity raises his or her economic power, while a scandal can hurt a politician's political power.

Teacher's board

Person-power fit. Think of a joint family. The strong personality of the grandparents holds all the power. After years a weak personality follows, and power gets decentralised.

Teacher's board

Mao: power flows from the barrel of a gun (written beside Military).

Teacher's board

Baba Ramdev example: social power turns into money power (Patanjali, plus coaching). One power rising lifts another.

It is in the nature of power to centralise in a strong personality

  • Power centralises itself in a strong personality. That personality can be a human or an institution.
  • When the personality weakens, power leaves in one of two ways: vertically downwards (decentralisation) or by horizontal displacement (another personality replaces it).
  • Teacher's example of horizontal displacement by an institution: Kodak → smartphones.
Teacher's board

Reading the wave. Till 1707: strong Mughal emperors (all-India power). After 1707: decline of the Mughals, decentralisation of power to regional powers. Then the Maratha empire by the 1750s, broken at the Third Battle of Panipat, 1761, and then the EIC (circled in red) rises.

Country, nation, state

  • Country = territory (Desh).
  • State = the organisation that rules or governs a country and its people (Rajya). To rule = to make laws, implement them and settle disputes (L, E, J) over domestic affairs. That is also called governance.
  • Nation = the summation of people who are one (fraternity), equal (equality) and supreme (liberty). This is the definition given by the French Revolution (1789). The teacher links it to "We the People" in the Preamble = Nation (Rashtra).
Remember

State rules a territory and its people. Country = the territory. Nation = the people. The State talks, trades or fights with the outside world, but it rules only its own country and people.

State structure

  • Teacher's tree: Symbolic sovereign (= President, Head of State) sits over the Legislature, Executive and Judiciary. This structure repeats at the Centre and at the Province.
  • The Executive = the Council of Ministers, which works through military servants and civil servants.
  • The handout says the primary purpose of any constitution is to create the State structure. A modern State structure has separation of power, a well-defined internal structure with clear goals and functions, and mechanisms that keep the whole structure stable from top to bottom. A non-modern State structure has no institutional separation of power and is prone to instability.
From the handout

Student confusion resolved (handout). NCERT says a Modern State has "centralised power exercised over a clearly defined territory". In class we say a Modern State has separation of power. Both are right. A State can be vertically centralised (a strong Union Government with authority over the provinces) and at the same time have horizontal separation of powers at the Union level: Parliament makes laws, the Prime Minister implements them, the Supreme Court resolves disputes. Like parents who centralise power over the home, yet divide it between mother and father.

Teacher's board

The word "province" is better than "state" for the units of a federation, otherwise it causes confusion with State = the ruling organisation.

"Republic of India" and the point of Modern Indian History

  • "Republic of India" = the name of the State (organisation) ruling the Territory and People of India since 26 January 1950.
  • Key goal of the Constitution = to define and create this State (organisation).
  • MIH = the history of the States that ruled the territory of India and its people, for example the Mughal State, the Maratha State, the Awadh State, and the Company State by the EIC.
  • INM (Indian National Movement) = a people's movement. Its goal: end the British colonial State and establish the Nation State (the Republic of India).
Teacher's board

Keep one picture: a State is an organisation. Every dynasty in this course is just one such organisation, and each can be studied by the same questions: who rules, over what territory, with what structure, and why did it weaken?

Sovereignty

Sovereignty = symbolic sovereignty + the power to make laws, implement them and adjudicate disputes (legislative, executive, judicial power), over external and domestic sovereign functions.

AspectMeaning
Symbolic sovereigntyWho is the head of the State
The three powersMake laws, implement them and adjudicate disputes (L, E, J)
External sovereign functionsDefence, Communications and Foreign Affairs: war and diplomacy (including trade)
Domestic sovereign functionsEverything else: Home, Finance, Agriculture, Industry, Infrastructure, Health, Education, Transport and so on
"Rule" / "Govern"Always means exercising those powers over domestic affairs
  • Sovereign functions are also called subjects or affairs. The 7th Schedule of the Constitution (COI 1950) divides the domestic affairs between the Centre and the provinces.
Teacher's board

Symbolic sovereignty: real power = 0. (The head of State is only a symbol.) Second part: the power to do L, E, J on (a) external sovereign functions = D, C, FA and (b) internal / domestic affairs = Home, Finance, Education, Health.

Teacher's board

Affairs = subjects = sovereign functions = the items in the 7th Schedule of the COI.

Teacher's board

External functions map to ministers like this. D (Defence) → Defence Minister → war. C (Communications) and FA (Foreign Affairs) → MEA → diplomacy + trade. Foreign Affairs has an economic and a political side.

Separation of powers in a modern State

  • Modern State structure = symbolic Head of State over L, E and J, at the Centre and provinces. The aim is separation of power, with neither over-centralisation nor an over-weak centre.
  • Horizontal SoP = L / E / J. Vertical SoP = federalism.
  • Inside the executive too: Head of State, PM, Council of Ministers, then the military servants ("gun power") and the civil servants ("political power"). Teacher: notice the separation of powers here also, for example a DM and an SP (more than one civil servant) share district power.
Teacher's board

Non-modern State (example: Mughal). An absolute monarch, extremely powerful, with ministers, aristocrats and mansabdars below him. The mark is excessive centralisation.

Teacher's board

Feudal State. The king is powerless, and feudal lords, ministers or aristocrats are powerful. There is no centralisation of power in the king: a very weak central authority.

How the teacher says to remember battles and wars

  • War = Σ (the sum) of battles. For any named battle or war, hold four things: Geography / place, Duration (example: the Seven Years' War), Participants, and Reason (example: the Austrian War of Succession, 1740–48).

Passages: how regions connect

  • Khyber Pass = India's entry pathway from the North-West.
  • A passage can be natural: on land a pass (Khyber); on water a strait (Gibraltar; the Dardanelles linking the Black and Mediterranean Seas). Or man-made: on land bridges and tunnels; on water canals (the Suez; the Panama Canal).
  • Two places that are strategically important are joined by a passage, and the passage makes them so (teacher's sketch of A and B with a link, natural or man-made).

3 · Mughal take-over from the Lodis (Babur 26-30)#

Who were the Mughals?

  • Babur 26-30 was born in 1483 to Umar Shaikh Mirza, ruler of Fergana, a semi-autonomous State in present-day Uzbekistan, roughly half the size of modern Haryana. Fergana belonged to the Timurid Empire, and Babur 26-30 was a descendant of Timur (Tamerlane).
  • "Mughal" is a Persian corruption of "Mongol". The Mughals were a Turko-Mongol dynasty.
SideAncestorEmpire
MaternalGenghis Khan (1206–27)Mongol Empire (1206–1368)
PaternalTimur (Tamerlane) (1370–1405)Timurid Empire (1370–1507)
Self-identityTimuridsPride in Timur, who had captured Delhi in 1398

They took greater pride in the paternal ancestor, so they called themselves Timurids, not Mughals.

Why Babur 26-30 turned to India

  • Babur 26-30 came to power young but could not hold Fergana; by 1512 his Central Asian ambitions had collapsed.
  • He held Kabul (captured 1504), but Kabul was economically poor.
  • India was wealthy but politically fragile under the Lodi dynasty.
  • With artillery support from the Ottoman Empire (expert gunners), Babur 26-30 began attacking the Delhi Sultanate from 1519.
Teacher's board

Babur 26-30's map: Kabul = "eco" (poor). The Khyber Pass is underlined. The dates 1526 (Panipat), 1528 (Agra) and 1529 (Gwalior and the march east) are underlined.

First Battle of Panipat (1526)

  • Babur 26-30 entered India through the Khyber Pass and met Ibrahim Lodi, the last ruler of the Lodi dynasty (1451–1526), on the plains of Panipat.
  • Babur 26-30's army was smaller (about 12,000 men), but his small bronze cannons were decisive, a technology the Lodis could not match.
  • Lodi was killed, the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) ended and the Mughal Kingdom was born.
  • This was still a kingdom, not yet an empire. It became an empire under Akbar 56-05 and reached its greatest extent under Aurangzeb 58-07.
Teacher's board

Tech power. Babur 26-30's edge over a fragile Lodi Sultanate was technology, not numbers. The teacher underlines "small bronze cannons", "about 12,000" and "Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526)".

Why three great battles happened at Panipat

Three of the most consequential battles in Indian history, 1526, 1556 and 1761, were all fought at Panipat. Geography chose Panipat; the commanders merely followed:

  1. Panipat lay close to the Khyber Pass, the primary invasion route from Central Asia.
  2. It sat in the narrow corridor between the Thar Desert (south) and the Himalayan foothills (north), a natural bottleneck.
  3. The Yamuna to the east acted as a defensive choke-point, ruling out flanking manoeuvres.
Handout alert

The Kala Amb memorial in Haryana commemorates the 1761 Third Battle, not the 1526 one.

4 · Humayun 30-40 and Sher Shah 40-45 Suri 40-45#

  • Babur 26-30 died in 1530; his son Humayun 30-40 succeeded.
  • Sher Shah 40-45 Suri 40-45 was an Afghan-Pathan, born Farid Khan in Bihar, c. 1486. He was no foreign invader: his ancestors migrated to India under the Lodis and settled as Jagirdars in Bihar.
  • He won the title Sher Khan by killing a tiger single-handed while serving Bahar Khan Lohani (Governor of Bihar under the Lodis till 1526, then independent Sultan Md Lohani, who refused Mughal sovereignty).
  • Sher Shah 40-45 defeated Humayun 30-40 at Chausa (1539) and Kanauj (1540), founded the Suri dynasty (1540–55) and pushed the Mughals out of India entirely.
Teacher's board

Two defeats: Chausa (1539) and Kanauj (1540). The Suri interregnum 1540–55 is a gap / intermission in the Mughal movie. Sirhind (1555) is boxed.

Sher Shah 40-45's three reforms

The Suri dynasty lasted only fifteen years, but Sher Shah 40-45's administrative innovations outlasted it:

  1. Revenue. A standardised system of land measurement and revenue assessment, the precursor to Akbar 56-05's Zabti system. Each peasant got a Patta specifying his revenue obligation, and the State took a Qabuliyat, a written agreement, from the peasant. The two-document system gave both State and cultivator an enforceable record.
  2. Currency. The silver Rupiya, a coin of standardised weight and purity, the ancestor of the modern Indian rupee.
  3. Infrastructure. He upgraded the Grand Trunk Road from Peshawar to Sonargaon in Bengal, transforming communication and trade across the subcontinent.
From the handout

Patta and Qabuliyat. The Patta is a document given by the State to the ryot: land details, ryot's name, the LR to be paid. The Qabuliyat is proof that the ryot agreed to pay the LR; it is given by the ryot to the State. Sher Shah 40-45 was the first to introduce this bilateral / mutual agreement system. In ancient times a Patta existed but it was not two-sided: the State gave the Patta (Tamra Patra, a copper inscription).

  • Sher Shah 40-45 died in 1545. The Suri dynasty held Delhi until 1555, when Humayun 30-40 recaptured the throne. Much of what Akbar 56-05 later systematised had its roots in Sher Shah 40-45's innovations.

Humayun 30-40 returns, with Safavid help

To retake his throne, Humayun 30-40 turned to the Safavid dynasty of Iran. The Safavids agreed at a political price: Humayun 30-40 had to

  • adopt Shia practices and outward observances,
  • cede Kandahar to Iran, and
  • promote Persian culture in India.

Whether this was genuine conversion is debated; Humayun 30-40 publicly assumed Shia forms as the price of the alliance. Lasting consequences: Persian language and art became embedded in Mughal court culture, and a distinct Irani group of Mansabdars later emerged in the Mughal administration.

  • With 14,000 Iranian troops, Humayun 30-40 defeated the Suris at the Battle of Sirhind (Punjab, 1555).
  • In January 1556 Humayun 30-40 fell from the stairs of his library, struck his head and died.
  • Hemchandra Vikramaditya (Hemu), from Rewari in present-day Haryana, who had served as chief commander and minister of the Suris, seized Delhi and declared himself Samrat.

  • Hemu was defeated. The Second Battle of Panipat (1556) secured the Mughal State in India for the next 150 years.

6 · How Akbar 56-05 built the Mughal State: the Mansabdari System#

Babur 26-30 founded the Mughal Kingdom. Humayun 30-40 nearly lost it. Akbar 56-05, ruling for fifty years (1556–1605), built the machinery of the Mughal State: the Mansabdari System, a military-bureaucracy that decided how the State was administered, how its army was maintained and how the King kept control over a vast and diverse territory.

What is a Mansabdar?

  • Mansab = a post or rank. Mansabdar = the holder of the post.
  • Every Mansabdar had a dual function: military and civil-administrative. The same man who commanded troops also governed territory. That is why it is called a military-bureaucracy.
Teacher's board

Posts in the State structure. "O" = a post = mansab. A DM or minister = mansabdar; DM-ship or minister-ship = mansab. Mansabdars = the ministers and senior officers of the Mughal State. Five questions the teacher sets for the topic: What is a mansab? What is a mansabdar? Salary? Ranks? How to become a mansabdar?

Rank: Zat + Sawar

Each Mansabdar carried two ranks:

  • Zat = personal rank, governing seniority and status.
  • Sawar = the number of horsemen he must maintain and provide to the King.
  • Net rank = Zat + Sawar. Example: Zat 2,000 + Sawar 3,000 = net rank 5,000.
Teacher's board

No separation of power between civil and military. Zat is like today's rank (A); Sawar = the army, i.e. the horsemen (B); net rank = A + B.

Teacher's board

Eligibility. "Lineage, not merit" in most cases. The primary rule was hereditary, with the exception of Birbal, who was not from an aristocratic family.

Teacher's board

Zat-Sawar graph. Most nobles lie on the rising line (more Zat, more Sawar). Outliers: the favourite of the emperor = "Anshu" and the distrusted = "Piyush". The teacher's two names sit at opposite corners of the graph (Piyush high on Zat, Anshu high on Sawar).

  • Who could become a Mansabdar? Lineage, not merit. If your ancestors had been Mansabdars, you had the opportunity. Talent alone was not enough, not unlike pre-revolutionary France, where only sons of nobles could enter the nobility. This rigidity later became a serious weakness.

How Mansabdars were paid: Jagir, Khalisa, Inam

  • A Mansabdar could be paid in cash (a Naqdi Mansabdar) or by a Jagir: a specific piece of land whose land revenue he pocketed directly. This revenue paid his salary and covered the cost of maintaining his army.
  • A Jagir given in lieu of salary = a Tankha Jagir; its holder = a Jagirdar.
Handout alert

Iqtadar of the Delhi Sultanate is like the Jagirdar Mansabdar of the Mughals. (Iqta = Jagir in Persian.)

Mughal land was divided into these categories:

  1. Jagir land: revenue to Mansabdars.
  2. Khalisa land: royal land. Revenue goes directly to the King and pays Naqdi Mansabdars plus other State expenses.
  3. Inam land: gifted land. Revenue goes to a chosen beneficiary (for example a man of exceptional talent) to sustain himself.
  4. Mashrut Jagir: a temporary "top-up" or extra allowance given to an official to cover the extra costs of a specific, difficult assignment. It is revoked automatically when the assignment ends. (Like "base salary" versus "the bonus".)
  • By the early 18th century there were over 8,000 Mansabdars, and four-fifths of all LR came from Jagirs, only one-fifth from Khalisa land.
Teacher's board

Salary in those times. The key source of power was control over land and its resources (today it is money). Mansabdars were about 4,000 to 5,000 at the peak of Mughal power (17th century); about 80 out of every 100 of LR went to them and about 20 to the King. Therefore the richest single person was the King.

Teacher's board

Worked example for LR: ₹100 of wheat is cultivated by the ryot / peasant / Khudkasht. Tax = LR = 1/3 to 2/3 of gross produce. (For the changing LR share by era, see section 7.)

Three rules for Tankha Jagirs

  1. Transferable. The King could rotate Jagirs among Mansabdars, or convert a Jagirdar into a Naqdi Mansabdar at will.
  2. Capped extraction. A Jagirdar could collect only the pre-determined LR, not a rupee more. Royal supervision existed to prevent oppression of peasants and zamindars.
  3. Confiscation on death. On the death of a Tankha Jagirdar, both the Jagir and all accumulated wealth were confiscated. The predictable result: Mansabdars spent lavishly in their lifetimes, since saving made little sense.
Teacher's board

From a Tankha Jagir the King gets his army. Teacher's example split: LR = ₹30 pays for personal salary; LR = ₹70 must be used to maintain the army as per Sawar. Military corruption (post-1707) = the expected army is not being maintained.

Watan Jagir

  • Watan = hereditary and permanent. Unlike Tankha Jagirs, a Watan Jagir could be passed down and was not subject to rotation or confiscation.
  • Given mainly to Rajput chieftains and autonomous rulers who had submitted to the Emperor, so they could collect revenue in their ancestral domains in exchange for military service and loyalty.
  • The holder paid 10% of LR to the King as Peshkash (tribute), acknowledging the King's ultimate authority and compensating him for giving up his right to reclaim the Jagir.
Handout alert

Do not confuse an ordinary Zamindar's Milkiyat (hereditary personal property within his zamindari tract) with a Watan Jagir (a political grant to a chieftain, carrying sovereign-like privileges). Inam vs Watan: Inam is a pure gift and is confiscated after death like Tankha Jagir. Watan is hereditary, given to a Mansabdar doing duty for the King, and passes to heirs.

In the teacher's picture, a Jagirdar with both gets 90% of the LR of the Watan portion, the king taking 10%; on Tankha land the Jagirdar takes 100% of the LR.

What held the system together, and its fatal weakness

  • The system ran on a patron-client relationship: the King gave rank and Jagir, and the Mansabdar pledged personal loyalty and maintained the army. This co-opted powerful individuals who might have challenged royal authority.
  • But the loyalty was personal, not institutional. A Mansabdar's loyalty was to the King as a person, not to the State as an institution or to any law. It was transactional and conditional: give and take.
  • If the King could not deliver Mansab and Jagir (land shortage, financial pressure, weak authority), the loyalty dissolved, and dissolved loyalty meant political instability.
  • This differs from a modern State, where bureaucrats and military officers owe their position to merit and service rules: impersonal loyalty to institution and law.
  • Because the whole military strength depended on Mansabdars actually maintaining the Sawar armies, weakened royal supervision meant hollowed-out armies on paper, far fewer men in reality, a silent erosion of State power from within.

7 · Mughal land revenue (LR) system#

Usually people think all rights in a property are with the owner. That is not correct. Rights in land belong to four stakeholders:

StakeholderRights
The owner (in the Mughal system, ultimately the King)Right to sell, lease out, rent out, and to get money from these; right to fair tax
The tenant / ryotRight to occupy and cultivate; right to fair rent
The collector of taxRight to collect tax; right to be paid for the service
The StateRight to assess / fix tax, enforce the demand and receive tax

LR was the financial backbone of the State: it paid the Mansabdars, maintained the army and kept the treasury alive.

Teacher's board

Assessors fix the demand using past data of production and of LR, and so assess the LR demand. The two systems, Zabti and Ijaradari, are ticked.

Who collected revenue, and how was it supervised

The State did not collect directly. It relied on Zamindars (non-state actors) in zamindari tracts, who also held police duties.

Remember

Rule of thumb: State actors get salaries. Non-State actors get a commission or profit for service performed for the State.

The revenue hierarchy ran through record-keeping, assessment, settlement, collection and oversight:

  • Patta (the title deed) = the official paper given to the individual peasant by the government. The government used the Patwari's ledger / Khasra to create it. It told the peasant exactly how much land he officially held and how much LR he must pay, and so served as a check against over-extraction.
  • Qabuliyat (the acceptance deed) = the document signed by the peasant and given back to the government, promising to pay the LR written on the Patta.
Teacher's board

A Patta has two pieces of information: which field / plot is cultivated by which ryot and how much LR is to be paid. It is a "safety tool for the Khudkasht / ryot".

Teacher's board

Zamindar = holds the right to collect LR from a land. Jagirdar = holds the right to consume LR from a land.

Physical collection and the Zabti system

  • Muqaddams collected from cultivators. In zamindari tracts they deposited to Zamindars (who extracted through their own Amlas). Where there were no intermediaries, Muqaddams paid directly to the State. In later periods contractual revenue farmers, the Ijaradars, also collected (covered below).
  • Under Akbar 56-05's Zabti system, the State did not trust Zamindars with autonomous authority. State officials (Amil at pargana level, under the Amalguzar at sarkar level) scientifically measured the land and fixed the exact demand. The land records were archived by the Qanungo at pargana level.
  • LR was calculated as a percentage of gross produce (more LR from more fertile areas). Mughal LR demand was a percentage of Gross Produce, not Net Produce. Subtracting the cost of cultivation before taxing was a British reform, much later, and only after the gross-produce method had caused widespread agrarian ruin.
Teacher's board

Mansabdari = a system of the Mughal State structure (king and mansabdars; roles and responsibilities, salaries, hierarchy and ranks). Zamindari / Zabti = a system of collection of LR: assessment (how much LR) by the State, collection by the Zamindar. Don't mix the two.

The zamindar's position

  • The Zamindar was an obedient collection agent under strict supervision, making sure the surplus went to the central treasury, not his pocket.
  • His legally fixed commission, about 10%, was called Nankar or Malikana.
  • His own personal lands were Milkiyat, on which he paid LR like any other landholder.
  • Abwabs = customary tributes, extra demands passed from State to Zamindar to peasant.
  • Intermediary Zamindars = big Zamindars who received LR from Primary Zamindars and collected LR themselves where no Primary Zamindar existed in their assigned land. They passed LR from Khalisa lands to the Imperial Treasury and from Jagir lands to Jagirdars. Sometimes Primary Zamindars sent LR straight to the treasury or the Jagirdar.
Teacher's board

The quantum of LR is decided by the State, not the zamindar. In the teacher's circle (the rural economy): ryots pay LR up to the Zamindar; he sends LR to K (king) and J (jagirdar); he keeps 10% as commission, Abwab, and has police duties.

Teacher's board

Zamindar roles. He also had police duties (the teacher compares it with an SHO's work) and was the traditional leader of ryots. Abwab from State to Zamindar = levies; Abwab from ryot to Zamindar = tribute.

Teacher's board

A Zamindar is a non-state actor: a private person, not a govt employee. Salary is used only for employees. His payment for services to the State (LR collection and police duties) is the 10% of LR.

Revenue farming: Muqatai, Ijaradari, Farming System

The logic: instead of collecting through the Zamindar network under State supervision, the right to collect all revenues from an area (land, traders, shopkeepers) was auctioned to the highest bidder, called an Ijaradar or Revenue Farmer.

  • A farmer, in the economic sense, works with a profit motive; a peasant works for food security. The Ijaradar was not a cultivator but a merchant or moneylender who bid purely for profit. He farmed revenue, not land.
  • The Ijaradar paid the bid amount, fully or partially in advance, to the Jagirdar (on Jagir land) or to the State (on Khalisa land), then collected his investment plus profit from the peasants and traders. Contracts were typically 3 to 5 years. His incentive was to extract as much as possible within that window, with zero interest in irrigation, peasant welfare or long-term growth. If he failed to deliver, the contract was re-auctioned.
  • Ijaradars were urban bankers and merchants with no roots in the countryside: no long-term stake in the land, no relationship with the peasants.
Teacher's board

Why Ijaradari arises: need or greed, for ↑LR. Need = war, bad harvest, LR collection of the State falling short. Greed = for example a Jagirdar who wants to maximise his earnings, and a weak Mughal State.

Teacher's board

The painting analogy. Bidders in the auction are all rich. "HK" (the seller) gets the best price, as a result of competitive bidding, and gets the money in advance. "Bhaskar" (the buyer) then gets the painting. Bhaskar and the other bidders have profit as the motive: he plans to sell it at ₹2 crore, so ₹1 crore is the investment and the return he expects is ₹1 crore. In Ijaradari the right to collect revenue is the painting.

Teacher's board

Who auctions: the Jagirdar (in a Jagir area) or the King/State (on Khalisa lands). They get money in advance (no wait till harvest) and the best price. Participants are men of money: merchants, moneylenders and bankers = Ijaradars. Motive = profit: collect such LR from ryots as covers the bid/investment plus profits. The teacher writes contract time as 3-4 years (the handout says 3 to 5). The Zamindar suffers a temporary loss of his Watan right to collect LR.

Teacher's board

Who wins and loses in Ijaradari: King/Jagirdar: happy (advance, best ₹). Ijaradar: happy (profits). Ryots: unhappy (no Patta). Zamindar: unhappy (lost Watan right and earnings).

Teacher's board

A revolt needs a leader (L) and followers (Fo). If both (Zamindar as leader, ryots as followers) are unhappy: strong revolt. If the leader is unhappy but followers are happy: no revolt. If the leader is happy but followers unhappy: weak revolt.

Teacher's board

Example: Churaman 95-21 Jat = Zamindar-led peasant revolt (~1695); grievance = high LR; it led to the Jat Kingdom. (Taken up in Chapter 5.)

  • Zamindar vs Ijaradar: why it mattered to the peasant. A Zamindar's right was tied to his bloodline; he and his descendants had to live with the peasants forever, so he rarely squeezed them to starvation. His commission was a state-mandated ~10% (Nankar / Malikana). An Ijaradar promised the State a fixed lump sum (say 1 lakh); if he extracted 3 lakh through torture and extortion, he kept the entire 2 lakh profit. The Zamindar's extraction was capped by law; the Ijaradar's was capped only by the peasant's capacity to endure.

The Ijaradari story across history: rise and decline

Auctioning the right to collect tax is ancient, but it was formally institutionalised in the subcontinent during the Delhi Sultanate as the Muqatai system. It lasted until Akbar 56-05 practically abolished it. Ijaradari maps almost perfectly onto the rise and decline of Mughal authority: abolished when the State was strong, revived when it was weak. (Babur 26-30 had no time for revenue reforms in his four-year reign; the decisive break came under Akbar 56-05.)

8 · Akbar 56-05's religious vision: Sulh-i-Kul, Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, Din-i-Ilahi#

Akbar 56-05's approach to religion shaped the political character of Mughal rule for generations. He moved in a clear sequence:

  1. Ibadat Khana (1575). A House of Worship at Fatehpur Sikri where scholars from different faiths debated. It exposed him to the diversity of religious thought and to the bitter rivalries among the Ulama.
  2. Mahzar, the Infallibility Decree (1579). It gave Akbar 56-05 the authority to adjudicate religious disputes: the Emperor, not the clergy, was the final word on matters of faith.
  3. Din-i-Ilahi (1582). A syncretic spiritual order borrowing from Islam, Hinduism, Zoroastrianism and Christianity. It is best understood as a religio-political experiment, not a movement: not many joined, but its real significance lay in the principle it expressed.
  4. Sulh-i-Kul: universal peace. The State must treat all religions with equal respect, and no single faith should dominate policy. It became the ideological foundation of Mughal governance under Akbar 56-05 and influenced Mughal politics long after his death.
Teacher's board

The goal of each step. Ibadat Khana: a mid-path, to reduce conflict. Mahzar: the Emperor is the final word on faith. Din-i-Ilahi: a syncretic religio-political order (a new hybrid religion borrowing from multiple faiths); goal = social peace. Sulh-i-Kul: universal peace via equal respect for all faiths; goal = social peace / order.

9 · Who actually introduced Jizya in India?#

  • Jizya is a tax on non-Muslims. It is often associated with the Delhi Sultanate, but it predates the Sultanate by several centuries.
  • Muhammad bin Qasim first imposed Jizya in Sindh during the 8th-century Arab conquest.
  • Qutb-ud-din Aibak 06-10 and his successors institutionalised it as a formal instrument of revenue and policy across the Delhi Sultanate from the early 13th century.
  • The distinction matters: Jizya arrived in India with the Arabs, but became a pan-subcontinental administrative practice only under the Sultanate.
