Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • The August Declaration (20 August 1917) and its key words gradual and progressive; Report 1918 = blueprint, Reforms = package, Act 1919 = law (2003, 2004, 2015, 2016)
  • First-time features: bicameral Centre (CLA ~145, 3 yrs; CoS ~60, 5 yrs), elected majority, direct elections, adjournment motions, division of subjects, separate budgets; PLC at least 70% directly elected, 3 yrs (2012, 2015)
  • Separate electorates: Muslims from ICA 1909 (not new in 1919), watertight rolls, Sikhs, Anglo-Indians, Christians; Depressed Classes nominated seats (2012, 2026)
  • Women: PLCs may remove the sex disqualification, same property bar as men (husband's property is 1935); no universal adult franchise; VEC convention of 3 Indians in 8
  • Viceroy's powers: veto, approve rejected bills, ordinance for 6 months, restore cuts, 75% non-votable; ICS exam in India 1922; Public Service Commission 1926 (Section 96C, Ross Barker); Narendra Mandal (Royal Proclamation 1921)
  • Dyarchy: Reserved (Police, Justice, Finance, Land Revenue, Irrigation, Control over Officials) against Transferred (health, education, local government, agriculture); Local Self-Government is Transferred (2022); Dyarchy lives inside the province (2017); event order 1905, 1916, 1919 (1995)

Mains hooks

  • Why Britain moved: the WWI democracy slogan and INM pressure; 'gradual and progressive' kept the timeline in British hands
  • Dyarchy as a design to discredit Indian ministers: no money, no officials, the Governor's override
  • Six grounds of INM dissatisfaction: federalism, Dyarchy, franchise, no responsible Centre, seats by importance, no effective self-government
  • Separate electorates as Divide and Rule; Taxation Without Representation at the Centre

1919 was the first step toward responsible government: elected houses and Dyarchy, but power without responsibility at the Centre and responsibility without power in the provinces.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Pressure from WWI and the INM forced Britain to promise gradual responsible government (1917); the Act (1919) gave elected houses and Dyarchy but kept real power: the Centre stayed irresponsible, the Governor held the key subjects, and separate rolls divided voters, so the INM called it a half-step.

  1. The war's slogan comes home (1914-18). WWI is fought by Britain, France and the USA under the banner of democracy and self-determination, and Indians fight and die in large numbers. At home the pressure keeps building: Swadeshi 1905-08, Revolutionaries 1907-15, Ghadar 1913-15, Home Rule Leagues 1916-18 and the Lucknow Pact of 1916. Swaraj now has moral weight.
    → So Britain has to answer: what about democracy in India?
  2. Montagu's promise (20 August 1917). Edwin Montagu, Secretary of State, tells the House of Commons that British policy is the gradual development of self-governing institutions towards the progressive realisation of responsible government within the Empire. Gradual means step by step; progressive means every step toward the goal, with the timeline in British hands. Swaraj is no longer seditious.
    → But a promise needs a plan.
  3. The tour and the Report (1917-18). Montagu personally tours Indian provinces, an unusual step, and writes a joint report with Viceroy Chelmsford 16-21: the Montagu-Chelmsford Report of 1918. It is the blueprint; the Act is the law; together they are the Montford Reforms.
    → Parliament now turns the blueprint into law.
  4. The Centre gets two houses (1919). The Act creates a bicameral Legislature for British India for the first time: the CLA (~145, 3 years) and the CoS (~60, 5 years), with elected majorities and direct elections in CLA, CoS and PLCs (at least 70% directly elected in a PLC). Adjournment motions are allowed; the Gaya 'End or Mend' resolution of 1922 leads the INC into the 1923 elections.
    → But who may vote, and who rules?
  5. Who may vote. The franchise stays tied to income and property; there is no universal adult franchise, though the Lucknow Pact asked for one as broad as possible. Women are not enfranchised directly: PLCs may remove the sex disqualification, but under the same property bar, so under 1% qualify. The husband's-property rule comes only in 1935.
    → And the voters are divided as well.
  6. Separate rolls made watertight. Separate electorates for Muslims continue from 1909 but become watertight: Muslims can no longer vote in unreserved seats. The principle is extended to Sikhs, Anglo-Indians and Christians. Depressed Classes get nominated seats. The British seize on the Lucknow Pact's logic for Divide and Rule.
    → Yet the Centre still answers to no one.
  7. Powers on paper, strings in hand. Subjects are divided between Centre and Provinces for the first time, but with no lists, so the Centre can take any back; Land Revenue goes to Provinces, Customs and Income Tax to the Centre, and provincial budgets are separated. The VEC has no cap, by convention 3 Indians of 8. The Viceroy keeps veto, approval of rejected bills, ordinance for 6 months, restoring cuts and 75% non-votable budget items.
    → Taxation without representation continues.
  8. Services and Princes. The ICS exam is held in India alongside England (first in 1922); Section 96C creates the Public Service Commission (1926, Ross Barker); the ICS splits into All India and Central services; 33% Indians are promised gradually. The Narendra Mandal, recommended by the Report, is constituted by Royal Proclamation in 1921 as a purely advisory body for the Princes.
    → Now the provinces: the most debated feature.
  9. Dyarchy: dual rule in the provinces. Provincial subjects are split into Reserved (Police, Justice, Finance, Land Revenue, Irrigation, Control over Officials) under the Governor, unaccountable to the PLC, and Transferred (health, education, local government, agriculture) under elected ministers responsible to the PLC. The board compares it with ToA 1765 and the 1784 Act: Reserved is the old authority's, Transferred the new one's.
    → But ministers hold the soft half of the bargain.
  10. Why Dyarchy failed. Ministers have no money (Finance is Reserved) and no control over officials; the Governor can overrule them on any matter and take over Transferred subjects on failure of constitutional machinery. When they fail they lose public trust, as the British designed, delaying the demand for full responsible government.
    → So the INM judges the Act.
  11. The verdict of the INM. Six grounds: the division of subjects is not federal; Dyarchy mocks federalism; there is no universal adult franchise; no responsible government at the Centre and only partial in the provinces; seats go by importance not population; there is no effective self-government. Better than 1909's limited self-government, but far from Swaraj.

In simple words#

  • After WWI and the pressure of Swadeshi, Ghadar, Home Rule and the Lucknow Pact, Montagu promised on 20 August 1917 the gradual, progressive growth of responsible government.
  • The Montagu-Chelmsford Report (1918) was the blueprint
  • The Government of India Act 1919 was the law. Together they are the Montford Reforms
  • The Centre got two houses, elected majorities and direct elections, but the Viceroy kept veto, ordinance and the last word on the budget, and had no responsible government.
  • The provinces got Dyarchy: Reserved subjects stayed with the Governor, Transferred subjects went to ministers who lacked money and officials.
  • Separate electorates became watertight and spread to Sikhs, Anglo-Indians and Christians
  • The INM judged the Act a half-step, far from Swaraj

1 · The Montagu Statement, 1917: why did Britain move at all?#

Story link

Because WWI's democracy slogan and the INM's pressure made Swaraj respectable, Britain had to promise something. Ask: who controls the timeline?

  • World War I (1914-19) posed a problem: the Allies (Britain, France, USA) fought under the banner of democracy and self-determination, and Indians had fought and died in large numbers.
  • Political pressure in India had built steadily: the Ghadar movement (1913-15), the Home Rule Leagues (1916-18) and the Lucknow Pact of 1916 (INC and Muslim League together).
  • Swaraj was no longer a fringe demand
  • It had moral weight.
Teacher's board
  • Chain: WWI slogan of democracy → "What about democracy in colonies / India?" → Swaraj has moral weight.
  • Pressure inside India = Swadeshi movement (1905-08) + Revolutionaries 1907-15 + HRL movement 1916-18.
  • Constitutional reform had three drivers: the INM (pressure on Britain), WH (read as world history), and personality (not imp).

1.1 The Montagu Statement / August Declaration, 20 August 1917

  • Issued by Edwin Montagu, Secretary of State for India, in the House of Commons on 20 August 1917.
  • Policy henceforth: the "gradual development of self-governing institutions" with a view to the "progressive realisation of responsible government in India within the British Empire."
  • Key words: "gradual" and "progressive". Britain promised not self-governance but a slow, controlled movement toward it, on British terms.
  • The demand for Swaraj was acknowledged, but the timeline stayed in British hands.
  • GOI 1919 = the 1st step toward the progressive realisation of responsible government.
Teacher's board
  • Swaraj = SG (self-government). "Gradual" = step by step
  • "progressive" = every next step is in the direction of the goal.
  • The Lucknow Pact 1916 had demanded "SG at an early date".
  • After the Statement, the demand for Swaraj is no more seditious: it is "just repeating government policy".
  • The HRL movement 1916-18 was at its peak (arrest of Annie Besant).
Source
  • World History link. WWI propaganda fought absolute monarchies for democracy and self-determination, a moral contradiction while denying freedom in colonies.
  • So the demand for Swaraj was no longer seditious in the post-1917 atmosphere
  • it had legitimacy within the British imperial framework

2 · The Montagu-Chelmsford Report, 1918#

Story link

Because a promise needs a plan, Montagu toured India and wrote the Report. Ask: Report, Reforms or Act: which is the law?

  • In 1917-18 Secretary of State Montagu personally visited India, an unusual step, and toured provinces to assess conditions on the ground.
  • The joint report he co-authored with Viceroy Chelmsford 16-21 was the foundational document of the Act: the Montagu-Chelmsford Report (1918).
  • It directly led to the GOI Act 1919, hence the Act is also called the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms or Montford Reforms.
Handout note

Report, Reforms, Act: not the same thing.

  • RA 1773 to GOI 1935 = all the constitutions of British India (board wording partly unclear).

3 · Provisions of the Government of India Act, 1919#

Story link

Because Parliament turned the blueprint into law, the Centre got two houses and the voters got new rolls. Ask: what was new, and what stayed with the Viceroy?

3.1 Bicameral Legislature at the Centre

  • Bicameral Legislature at the Centre for British India for the first time: the Central Legislative Assembly (CLA), lower house, ~145 members, and the Council of States (CoS), upper house, ~60 members.
  • Elected majority in CLA, CoS and all Provincial Legislative Councils (PLCs) for the 1st time.
  • (Under ICA 1909 a non-official majority existed only in PLCs, not at the Centre.)
  • Direct elections for the first time in CLA, CoS and PLCs.
  • CoS too had direct elections, with higher income criteria for the vote, unusual for an upper house.
  • CLA life:
    • 3 years
    • CoS life: 5 years.
  • In Provinces, PLCs were unicameral with elected majority (1st) and direct elections (1st)
  • At least 70% of members directly elected
  • PLC life 3 years.
  • (Under ICA 1909 PLCs had a non-official majority, but most non-officials were nominated.)
  • Adjournment motions allowed for the 1st time.

  • From RA 1773 to GOI 1919 the constitutions were for British India only.

Bicameralism was only for British India. Only the GOI Act 1935 extended the framework to Princely States in an all-India federation.

Teacher's board
  • Adjournment motions link to the "End or Mend" Councils resolution of the Pro-Changers at the 1922 Gaya Session ("mend" = amend GOI 1919; repeated use of adjournment motions).
  • Change: INC fights the elections of 1923. 1920 elections were boycotted because of the NCM 1920-22.
Teacher's board
  • In practice one British India meant one election, Centre and Provinces together.
  • Post 1947, until 1971, simultaneous elections at Centre and Provinces.

3.2 Narendra Mandal: Chamber of Princes

  • The Narendra Mandal (Chamber of Princes) was created as an advisory body for the Princely States.
  • A key recommendation of the Montagu-Chelmsford Report (1918)
  • formally constituted by Royal Proclamation in 1921, not by the GOI Act itself.
  • The Act gave the framework
  • The Proclamation brought it to life
  • A reward to the Princes for WWI support: the British promised to consult the Princes on policy matters affecting the Princely States.
  • It had no legislative power: purely advisory.
Teacher's board
  • The Narendra Mandal is the Princes' own organisation, a body of the Princely States.
  • Britain will take advice before any change in British policy towards the 565 Princely States.
  • Montford Reforms 1919 = (a) MC Report 1918 → GOI Act 1919, and (b) MC Report 1918 → Narendra Mandal.
  • The Mandal therefore comes from the Report, through the Proclamation of 1921.

3.3 Elections and the right to vote

  • Right to vote linked to strict income/property criteria, as in ICA 1909.
  • Women's franchise: the Act did not directly grant women the vote.
  • It empowered Provincial Legislatures to pass resolutions removing the sex disqualification.
  • Women admitted to vote had to meet the exact same strict property qualifications as men, so less than 1% of women qualified.
  • No Universal Adult Franchise:
    • The Lucknow Pact of 1916 had demanded a franchise as broad as possible
    • not conceded
Handout alert
  • Women's franchise, 1919 against 1935. 1919 did NOT directly enfranchise women
  • it only let Provincial Legislatures remove the sex disqualification, with the same property/income criteria as men and no lower threshold.
  • Voting based on husband's property and a lower income criterion for women came only with the GOI Act 1935.
Teacher's board
  • 1919:
    • Same criteria for both
    • 1935: criteria = husband's property and a lower threshold for women.
  • Random example:
    • Criteria men Rs 400 from property, women Rs 200 from property
    • husband's property gives Rs 300 → the man has no R2V
    • The wife has R2V (1935 logic). Lucknow Pact 1916 demand unmet

3.4 Separate electorates: expanded and made watertight

  • Separate electorates for Muslims continued from ICA 1909, with a critical change.
  • ICA 1909: Muslims could vote in Muslim reserved seats and in unreserved seats.
  • GOI 1919:
    • Muslims could no longer vote in unreserved seats
    • unreserved seats took only voters on the unreserved rolls. The electorates became watertight.
  • Roots in the Lucknow Pact 1916:
    • To forge a united front, the Muslim League gave up its right to vote in general constituencies
    • in exchange the INC accepted separate electorates and a guaranteed, fixed quota of seats for Muslims.
  • The British seized on the communal division logic
  • it dovetailed with Divide and Rule.
  • GOI 1919 created strictly segregated, watertight rolls and extended the separation to communities the Lucknow Pact never contemplated.
  • Extended to Sikhs, Anglo-Indians and Christians, all with watertight rolls: they could vote only in their own reserved constituencies, not in unreserved seats.

3.5 Division of subjects between Centre and Provinces

  • Subjects were divided between Centre and Provinces for the first time.
  • Not a federal distribution: no formal lists (no 7th Schedule of COI 1950).
  • The Centre could still take away any subject from a province or legislate upon it at will.
  • Provincial Budget separated from Central Budget for the first time.
  • Land Revenue (LR) to Provinces
  • Customs and Income Tax retained by the Centre.
  • Seats in CLA and CoS allocated to Provinces not by population but by perceived importance: Punjab weighted for military importance, Bombay for commercial importance.
Teacher's board
  • Subjects = sovereign functions / affairs (initials on the board: D, C, FA, Fin, Rev, Home, Health etc.).
  • Seats by importance, not population = "not in line with democracy".
  • Budget (BD) = statement of income and expenditure.
  • Till 1919 the Centre prepared the BD of the Centre and the BDs of the Provinces of British India
  • from 1919 the Provincial Budget was separate
Teacher's board
  • Board 1: subjects / sovereign functions split into External (always with the Centre) and Domestic (divided between Centre and Provinces).
  • Board 2: federal distribution = COI 1950 7th Schedule: Central List, Concurrent List, State List.
  • Family-property analogy: 1919: on paper all property is of the parents, but in practice some property is with the parents and some with the sons. 1935 and 1950: property is divided legally (in papers) also.
  • Key:
    • Property = Subjects
    • Parents = Centre
    • Sons = Provinces
    • Papers = Lists / COI.

3.6 Viceroy's Executive Council (VEC)

  • The Act removed the statutory maximum limit on ordinary members of the VEC.
  • (ICA 1861: 5 ordinary members; 1874 amendment: 6.)
  • By convention (not statutory): the VEC normally had Viceroy + Commander-in-Chief + 6 ordinary members = 8, of whom 3 of the 6 ordinary members were Indians.
  • A political practice, not a legal requirement.
  • The Lucknow Pact 1916 demanded that half the VEC be Indians
  • 3 of 8 = demand unmet.
Teacher's board

ICA 1909: VEC = GG + CIC + 6 ordinary, of which 1 Indian. 1919: limit of 8 removed; expansion of the Cabinet/VEC possible at the wish of the GG/Viceroy. GOI 1919 did not say that 3 must be Indians; it became a convention on the basis of a British Committee (one initial in its name is unclear: check your notebook).

Handout alert
  • Source of the cap. The handout dates the 6-member cap to the 1874 amendment (5 under ICA 1861)
  • The board writes it under ICA 1909 (GG + CIC + 6, one Indian)
  • Both give 8 in all, and Chapter 31 records the VEC as the "same since 1874"
  • Use the handout's dating and the board's numbers

3.7 Powers of the Viceroy: no responsible government at the Centre

  • Was the VEC responsible to the legislature?
  • No. It was not accountable to the legislature.
  • "Responsibility" has a technical meaning: a Minister must be an elected Member of Parliament and is accountable through the No Confidence Motion.
  • None of this existed for the Central Executive.
  • Morley (on ICA 1909):
    • "There is no intent of Responsible Govt in India." The August Declaration promised "progressive realisation"
    • GOI 1919 was only the 1st step, and the Centre was not made responsible.
  • The Viceroy kept these powers:

3.8 Simultaneous ICS exam and the Public Service Commission

  • The Act mandated that the ICS examination be held simultaneously in India alongside England
  • The first examination in India was held in 1922.
  • Section 96C mandated a Public Service Commission for India
  • set up in 1926 with Ross Barker as first Chairman (direct ancestor of the modern UPSC)
  • A gradual increase to 33% Indians in senior civil service positions.
  • Since the Act divided subjects between Centre and Provinces (Dyarchy), the ICS was split into All India Services (AIS) and Central Civil Services, the structure we see today, continued and modified by the Constitution.

4 · Dyarchy at the provincial level: the most debated feature#

Story link

Because the Centre could not be made responsible yet, the experiment moved to the provinces. Ask: who got which half, and could ministers actually govern?

  • What was Dyarchy? "Dyarchy" = dual rule (Dy = Two; Archy = Top Authority).
  • At the provincial level all subjects were split into two categories, Reserved and Transferred, governed by different authorities.
Dyarchy: two halves of provincial government
Teacher's board
  • Dyarchy = "2 TOP AUTHORITIES" (2 = Dual, Top Auth = Govt).
  • ToA 1765 Dual Government:
    • Bengal's work divided in two under two top authorities: Diwani (Transferred) with the EIC
    • Nizamat (Reserved) with the Nawab.
  • PIA 1784 double government of the EIC's affairs:
    • EIC's work divided in two: Commercial matters (Reserved) with the Court of Directors of the EIC
    • CMR matters (Transferred) with the Board of Control 1784 of the British State.
  • "Reserved" is used for the OLD authority (which had everything earlier)
  • "Transferred" for the NEW authority.
  • See Chapter 10 and Chapter 17.
Teacher's board
  • Subjects = the work of the State ruling British India (illustration: 100 subjects).
  • L = law-making, E = implementing on a subject.
  • Illustration: 100 → 60 to the Centre + 40 to the Provinces = the concept of FEDERALISM.
  • Provinces' 40 subjects are divided into two: Reserved and Transferred.
  • Reserved = OLD authority:
    • The Governor and his officers
    • Executive authority, not answerable to the PLA.
  • Transferred = NEW authority: E = ministers responsible to the PLA.
  • The Governor (E; board shorthand GEC) has ministers of Re and ministers of Tn
  • no CM under 1919 (1935 brings the CM).
Handout alert
  • Reserved executive. The handout says Reserved subjects were run by the Governor and his unelected Executive Councillors
  • the board calls them "ministers of Re" responsible to the Governor, not to the PLA.
  • Same meaning: unelected and unaccountable to the PLC.

4.1 Reserved subjects

  • Politically important:
    • Law &amp
    • Order i.e. Police, Administration of Justice, Finance, Land Revenue, Irrigation, and Control over Officials.
  • The PLC had formal law-making power, but the Governor held absolute veto and certification powers to pass rejected bills.
  • Executive powers lay with the Governor and his officials (unelected Executive Councillors), completely unaccountable to the PLC.
  • Why Irrigation was Reserved: the major canal systems that created the Punjab Canal Colonies were State-funded public works (unlike early railway expansion, financed substantially by private British capital under State guarantees).
  • Irrigation was closely linked to Land Revenue, water rates and rural administration.
  • The British argued the small electorate, dominated by landlords, could not yet be trusted with these rural matters.
  • Keeping it Reserved let British officials retain control over provincial revenue
  • in Punjab it also allowed canal-colony lands to be granted to military veterans and loyal rural elites.

4.2 Transferred subjects

  • Less politically sensitive: health, education, local government, agriculture.

  • Legislative power on Transferred subjects was with the PLC.
  • Administered by elected Ministers responsible to the PLC
  • the Governor had to appoint Ministers from among the elected members of the PLC.

4.3 Why Dyarchy was a problem in practice

  • Indian ministers got the politically less important subjects (health, education, agriculture) but no control over finance or officials.
  • Without money and control over the bureaucracy they could not govern even the Transferred subjects.
  • When they failed to deliver they became unpopular, exactly as the British designed.
  • Goal: hurt the legitimacy of Indian ministers and show Indians could not govern, delaying the demand for full responsible government.
  • On failure of constitutional machinery the Governor could take over executive and legislative powers over Transferred subjects as well.
  • Ministers from the PLC could be overruled by the Governor on any matter.
Teacher's board
  • Examples of "failure of constitutional machinery":
    • No party gets a majority
    • MG's boycott of the Legislature.
  • Contrast: today there are no Reserved subjects, so every minister has to be an MLA first.

4.4 Dyarchy today

Teacher's board
  • Today all subjects at the Province level are Transferred:
    • L = SLA
    • E = ministers responsible to the SLA.
  • Today a form of Dyarchy exists in "UT with Legislature".
  • Difference:
    • Today the LG acts on the aid and advice of the Central Government, which is a fully responsible government
    • not the case in GOI 1919.
