What to prepare

Prelims hooks: which Schedule deals with what (anti-defection, Scheduled Areas, languages, panchayats); Fifth versus Sixth; Ninth Schedule validity; 22 languages; 29 and 18 subjects; first-amendment era of the Ninth Schedule.

Mains hooks: Fifth Schedule and Left Wing extremism (2013 asks the impact of non-implementation); anti-defection merits and flaws; Ninth Schedule and judicial review.

Closing idea: Schedules keep the Articles lean: detailed lists, oaths and arrangements live here and can change without rewriting the main text.

1 · What a Schedule is#

Schedules are annexed lists and tables that support the Articles. They carry operational detail (lists of subjects, forms of oaths, languages, administrative arrangements) so that the Articles stay lean, the detail can be updated by amendment, and Parliament and governments have ready references (who legislates on what, how many Rajya Sabha seats per State, which areas are Scheduled Areas).

Schedules over time

1950
8 Schedules
Later
Four added: the 9th, 10th, 11th, 12th; several existing ones expanded or modified
Today
12 Schedules; each links explicitly to the Articles it supports

Together they support federalism, decentralisation, social justice, linguistic policy and political integrity.

Twelve in a chain
MMOSTTLLSDPM

Map, Money, Oath, Seats, Tribes, Tribes (north-east), Lists, Languages, Shield, Defection, Panchayats, Municipalities. Say: "My Money Oath Seats Tribes Take Lists, Languages Shield Defectors, Panchayats Municipalities."

2 · First to Fourth Schedules#

First Schedule: how the map changes

Parliament
Admits or establishes new States; alters areas, boundaries or names (Arts. 2-4)
On the President's recommendation
After seeking the concerned State legislature's views
Article 4
Done by ordinary law, not treated as an amendment under Art. 368

The First Schedule is the "ledger of India's political map". It confers no powers: it says who the constituent units are and what territory they hold.

Second Schedule: who is covered
PartCovers
APresident of India and Governors (emoluments, allowances, residences)
BOmitted by the 26th Amendment (1971); earlier privy purses of former rulers
CPresiding Officers: Speaker/Deputy Speaker of Lok Sabha, Chairman/Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha, and State counterparts
DJudges of the Supreme Court and High Courts (salaries, leave, pension)
EComptroller and Auditor-General of India
FUnion and State Public Service Commissions (members' pay and service conditions)

It guarantees financial security and dignity of office (a pillar of independence), and Parliament can revise it by ordinary law.

Second Schedule parts
PPJCP

A President and Governors, (B omitted), C Presiding officers, D Judges, E CAG, F PSCs: "Any Court Deserves Even Fees".

Third Schedule

Oaths of Union and State Ministers, candidates, MPs, MLAs/MLCs, SC and HC judges and the CAG are here: an oath of office and, where needed, an oath of secrecy (ministers). The President's and Governors' oaths are NOT here: they are in Art. 60 and Art. 159. The Fourth Schedule fixes the elective Rajya Sabha seats for each State and UT; the 12 nominated seats (appointed by the President) are outside its scope. Reorganisation laws under Arts. 2 or 3 may revise the First and Fourth Schedules by ordinary law (Art. 4), not as amendments under Art. 368.

3 · Fifth and Sixth Schedules (tribal areas)#

Fifth Schedule (Art. 244)

  • Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in most states outside Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram
  • works through the Governor's regulations and the Tribes Advisory Council
  • applies to 10 states

Sixth Schedule (Arts. 244(2), 275)

  • Tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram
  • elected Autonomous District Councils (and Regional Councils) with legislative, executive, limited judicial and fiscal powers
  • grants-in-aid under Art. 275(1)
Fifth Schedule's 10 states
ACGHJMMORT

Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan, Telangana: "A Cool Guy Helps Just My Mother Often Run Tea".

Sixth Schedule's 4 states
AMTM

Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram: "All My Tribal Mates".

Fifth Schedule: four things it does

Four powers of the Fifth Schedule

1 The President declares Scheduled Areas
Declares, alters and re-organises them by public order
2 The Governor reports
Annual report to the President on administration; the President may give directions
3 Tribes Advisory Council (TAC)
Mandatory in every State with Scheduled Areas; so tribal voices are heard in policy
4 The Governor regulates
May direct that Acts of Parliament or the State Legislature do not apply or apply with modification, and make regulations for peace and good government
Tribes Advisory Council and Governor's regulations
PointDetail
TAC sizeUp to 20 members; about three-fourths from ST members of the State Legislative Assembly; if STs have little or no representation, the Governor nominates; not less than half must be ST persons
TAC roleAdvises on welfare and advancement of STs, especially matters the Governor refers
Governor's regulations may(i) prohibit or restrict land transfer by or among STs, (ii) regulate allotment of land to STs, (iii) regulate money-lending to STs
ProcessGovernor must consult the TAC (where it exists); every regulation needs the President's assent

Parliament can change this Schedule by law at any time, and such a law is not an amendment under Art. 368.

Prelims shortcut

Fifth = Governor + TAC + land protection (prevents transfer of tribal land to non-tribals; PYQ 2022 and 2019). Sixth = Councils + taxes + courts (self-government).

Sixth Schedule extras

Councils can set up village courts and District Council courts for certain civil and criminal matters involving tribal parties, apply customary law, and frame rules of procedure and appellate structures, under the supervision of the State High Court. They can levy taxes and fees on land and buildings, entry of goods into markets, animals, vehicles, professions, trade and callings, general services, roads and ferries, and use of water or resources, prepare budgets and keep funds.

4 · Seventh Schedule: who legislates on what#

The Seventh Schedule (Art. 246) distributes subjects between the Union, States and both through three lists: Union, State and Concurrent. National matters needing uniformity sit with Parliament; local matters with States; shared matters in the Concurrent List.

How the lists evolved

42nd Amendment (1976)
Education, forests, wildlife, weights and measures moved from the State to the Concurrent List
101st Amendment (2016)
GST: taxation entries reworked for a unified indirect tax
Entry counts (this handout)
Union 97, State 61, Concurrent 52 (counts change when entries are deleted, merged or modified)
Handout alert

In Part 2 (Centre-State Relations) the handout gives Union 98, State 59 (earlier 66), Concurrent 52 (up from 47). The Union and State figures differ from the 97 and 61 above. Check your class notes before quoting either.

5 · Eighth Schedule: 22 languages#

It recognises languages; it does not declare a national language (India has none). It is read with Arts. 343-351 (official language), especially Art. 351 (develop Hindi) and Arts. 350A-350B (linguistic minorities). Inclusion helps visibility: central recruitment exams, translation facilities in Parliament, schemes through the CIIL and Sahitya Akademi.

How we reached 22

1950
14 languages: Assamese, Bengali, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Malayalam, Marathi, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Urdu
21st Amendment (1967)
+ Sindhi → 15
71st Amendment (1992)
+ Konkani, Manipuri (Meitei), Nepali → 18
92nd Amendment (2003)
+ Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali → 22
Additions
SKMNBDMS

1967 Sindhi; 1992 Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali ("KMN"); 2003 Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali ("BDMS": "Bad Dogs Maul Sheep").

What inclusion does not mean

It does not make a language the "official language of the Union" (Hindi in Devanagari with English as associate/working language under the Official Languages Act) and does not automatically make it a State's official language (States choose under Art. 345).

6 · Ninth Schedule: the protective list#

Art. 31B puts laws in this Schedule beyond challenge: they are "not deemed to be void" for violating Fundamental Rights, retrospectively too. Laws were placed through the 1st Amendment. Original purpose: protect agrarian reform (zamindari abolition, land ceilings). Later additions included tenancy, regulation of industries, reservations and other socio-economic laws. Only a constitutional amendment under Art. 368 can insert or delete items, unlike ordinary Schedules.

The court's journey on the Ninth Schedule

Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965)
Upheld Parliament's wide amending power
Golaknath (1967)
Briefly curtailed that power; clarificatory amendments followed
Kesavananda Bharati (24 April 1973)
Amendments cannot damage the Basic Structure
Waman Rao (1981)
Laws put in the Ninth Schedule after 24-04-1973 are open to basic-structure review
I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)
Settled: no absolute immunity; post-1973 insertions can be struck down if they damage the basic structure
Case order
SSGKWC

Shankari, Sajjan, Golaknath, Kesavananda, Waman Rao, Coelho: "Some Singers Get Kind Words Cheaply".

Prelims trap

"A law in the Ninth Schedule cannot be examined by any court" is wrong. Parliament can place a law there (PYQ 2018: statement 1 true, statement 2 false). The Schedule was introduced under Nehru (1st Amendment; PYQ 2019).

7 · Tenth Schedule: anti-defection#

Inserted by the 52nd Amendment (1985), strengthened by the 91st (2003). Applies to Parliament and State legislatures; it aims to curb instability from opportunistic floor-crossing while keeping party systems intact.

Who is disqualified, and when
MemberDisqualified if
Party member(i) Voluntarily gives up party membership (courts: can be inferred from conduct, not only formal resignation) or (ii) votes or abstains against the party whip without prior permission and the party does not condone within 15 days
Independent memberJoins any political party after the election
Nominated memberJoins a party after six months from taking the seat (within six months is allowed)
Four triggers
GWIN

Gives up membership, Whip defied, Independent joins a party, Nominated joins after six months. "Going With India's Nation".

Who decides and how courts check

Complaint
To the Speaker or Chairman, who decides (once final)
Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992)
Law upheld; the Speaker acts as a tribunal, so decisions are subject to judicial review under Arts. 136, 226, 227, typically after the Speaker decides
Time limit
None in the Schedule; the Court insists on a reasonable period
Advantages and criticisms
AdvantagesCriticisms
Stability: fewer opportunistic toppling of governmentsDissent versus defection: no clear line; curbs conscience and debate; elevates party bossism
Orderly realignment through the merger routeRetail versus wholesale: individual defections banned but group moves legitimised (the "split" route is now deleted)
Lower corruption and cost: fewer transaction-driven politics and frequent electionsOutside the House: no remedy for expulsion over extra-parliamentary acts; House conduct only
Constitutional status for political parties, for the first timeIndependent versus nominated: unequal treatment (nominated can join within six months)
Presiding officer as adjudicator: bias risk (party figure) and lack of judicial training; Speakers Rabi Ray (1991) and Shivraj Patil (1993) questioned it
Four advantages
SOLC

Stability, Orderly merger, Lower corruption, Constitutional status for parties.

The 91st Amendment Act, 2003

What it did
ProvisionRule
Union ministry capMinisters (including PM) not more than 15% of Lok Sabha strength
State ministry capMinisters (including CM) not more than 15% of the Assembly strength, but not less than 12
Ministers barredA member of either House (Parliament or State) disqualified for defection is also disqualified from being a minister

15% and 12: ministers cap and the State floor. One-third split gone, two-thirds merger stays.

8 · Eleventh Schedule: 29 subjects for Panchayats#

Inserted by the 73rd Amendment, 1992; read with Part IX (Arts. 243-243O), especially Art. 243G, which lets State Legislatures give Panchayats powers for plans and schemes on these subjects. It is enabling, not self-executing: real devolution depends on State laws.

9 · Twelfth Schedule: 18 subjects for Municipalities#

Inserted by the 74th Amendment, 1992; read with Part IX-A (Arts. 243P-243ZG), especially Art. 243W, which lets State Legislatures give Nagar Panchayats, Municipal Councils and Municipal Corporations functions for plans and schemes on urban affairs. Like the Eleventh, it is enabling.
