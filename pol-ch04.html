In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • The Schedules are lists and tables attached to the Constitution, so the Articles stay short. The long lists (oaths, languages, who legislates on what) live here.
  • There were 8 in 1950. Four were added later (9th, 10th, 11th, 12th), so there are 12 today.
  • Four carry most exam questions: the Fifth and Sixth (tribal areas), the Ninth (protected laws, Art. 31B) and the Tenth (anti-defection).
  • The Ninth Schedule is not absolute. Laws placed there after 24 April 1973 can be struck down if they damage the basic structure (Waman Rao 1981, Coelho 2007).
  • The Eleventh and Twelfth list the subjects of Panchayats (29) and Municipalities (18)
  • The Eighth recognises 22 languages but declares no national language

Key terms

Schedule
An annexed list or table that supports an Article; it adds detail without lengthening the main text.
Scheduled Area
An area declared under the Fifth Schedule for the administration and control of Scheduled Tribes.
Tribes Advisory Council (TAC)
A body of up to 20 members in each State with Scheduled Areas; it advises on tribal welfare (Fifth Schedule).
Autonomous District Council
An elected council with legislative, executive, limited judicial and fiscal powers in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram (Sixth Schedule).
Art. 31B
The Article that places laws in the Ninth Schedule so they are not deemed void for violating Fundamental Rights.
Defection
Giving up party membership or defying the whip; the ground for disqualification under the Tenth Schedule.
Merger
The only exception left in the Tenth Schedule: two-thirds of a party agree to merge. The one-third 'split' was deleted in 2003.
Concurrent List
The list in the Seventh Schedule on which both the Union and the States can legislate (52 entries).

1 · What a Schedule is#

  • Schedules are annexed lists and tables that support the Articles. They carry operational detail (lists of subjects, forms of oaths, languages, administrative arrangements) so that the Articles stay lean, the detail can be updated by amendment, and Parliament and governments have ready references (who legislates on what, how many Rajya Sabha seats per State, which areas are Scheduled Areas).

Schedules over time

  • 1950: 8 Schedules
  • Later:
    • Four added: the 9th, 10th, 11th, 12th
    • Several existing ones expanded or modified
  • Today:
    • 12 Schedules
    • Each links explicitly to the Articles it supports
  • Together they support federalism, decentralisation, social justice, linguistic policy and political integrity.
  • Memory aid (Twelve in a chain): Map, Money, Oath, Seats, Tribes, Tribes (north-east), Lists, Languages, Shield, Defection, Panchayats, Municipalities. Say: "My Money Oath Seats Tribes Take Lists, Languages Shield Defectors, Panchayats Municipalities."

Also in the handout

  • Therefore, a Schedule is an explanatory note or addendum—an attachment placed at the end of the Constitution where extended detail is needed. Each Schedule explicitly links to the Articles it supports, so readers know exactly which constitutional provisions it elaborates.
  • It lists the names of all States and Union Territories and records their territorial extent, thereby giving constitutional recognition to each unit of the Union. It does not confer powers
  • Instead, it answers two foundational questions: who the constituent units are and what territory they constitutionally occupy at a given time. Its design is deliberately dynamic
  • This mechanism lets India’s political geography evolve without reopening the Constitution’s core text each time a boundary shifts or a name changes.
  • This flexibility allows the map of protected tribal areas to evolve with administrative changes.
  • These changes may add new provisions, modify existing ones, or remove them.

2 · First to Fourth Schedules#

First Schedule: how the map changes

  • Parliament:
    • Admits or establishes new States
    • Alters areas, boundaries or names (Arts. 2-4)
  • On the President's recommendation: After seeking the concerned State legislature's views
  • Article 4: Done by ordinary law, not treated as an amendment under Art. 368
  • The First Schedule is the "ledger of India's political map". It confers no powers: it says who the constituent units are and what territory they hold.

Second Schedule: who is covered

PartCovers
APresident of India and Governors (emoluments, allowances, residences)
BOmitted by the 26th Amendment (1971); earlier privy purses of former rulers
CPresiding Officers: Speaker/Deputy Speaker of Lok Sabha, Chairman/Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha, and State counterparts
DJudges of the Supreme Court and High Courts (salaries, leave, pension)
EComptroller and Auditor-General of India
FUnion and State Public Service Commissions (members' pay and service conditions)
  • It guarantees financial security and dignity of office (a pillar of independence), and Parliament can revise it by ordinary law.
  • Memory aid (Second Schedule parts): A President and Governors, (B omitted), C Presiding officers, D Judges, E CAG, F PSCs: "Any Court Deserves Even Fees".
Handout alert

Third Schedule: Oaths of Union and State Ministers, candidates, MPs, MLAs/MLCs, SC and HC judges and the CAG are here: an oath of office and, where needed, an oath of secrecy (ministers). The President's and Governors' oaths are NOT here: they are in Art. 60 and Art. 159. The Fourth Schedule fixes the elective Rajya Sabha seats for each State and UT; the 12 nominated seats (appointed by the President) are outside its scope. Reorganisation laws under Arts. 2 or 3 may revise the First and Fourth Schedules by ordinary law (Art. 4), not as amendments under Art. 368.

Also in the handout

  • Instead of scattering salary, allowance, leave, pension and service-condition details across Articles, the Constitution groups them here and then cross-references the relevant Articles. Functionally, it serves two purposes: it guarantees financial security and dignity of office (a key pillar of independence and neutrality), and it provides a stable, update-friendly container that Parliament can revise by ordinary law when pay structures are modernized.
  • Instead of scattering the full wording across multiple articles, these forms are placed in one schedule to promote uniformity, solemnity, and legal certainty. Broadly, the Schedule provides (i) an oath or affirmation of office — in which the person promises to faithfully discharge their duties under the Constitution and laws — and, where applicable, (ii) an oath or affirmation of secrecy, which binds certain office-holders (like ministers) not to disclose official matters except as required for duty.
  • A key distinguishing feature from the 5th Schedule is financial autonomy.

3 · Fifth and Sixth Schedules (tribal areas)#

  • Fifth Schedule (Art. 244):
    • Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in most states outside Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram
    • Works through the Governor's regulations and the Tribes Advisory Council
    • Applies to 10 states
  • Sixth Schedule (Arts. 244(2), 275):
    • Tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram
    • Elected Autonomous District Councils (and Regional Councils) with legislative, executive, limited judicial and fiscal powers
    • grants-in-aid under Art. 275(1)
  • Memory aid (Fifth Schedule's 10 states): Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan, Telangana: "A Cool Guy Helps Just My Mother Often Run Tea".
  • Memory aid (Sixth Schedule's 4 states): Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram: "All My Tribal Mates".

Fifth Schedule: four things it does

  • 1 The President declares Scheduled Areas: Declares, alters and re-organises them by public order
  • 2 The Governor reports:
    • Annual report to the President on administration
    • The President may give directions
  • 3 Tribes Advisory Council (TAC):
    • Mandatory in every State with Scheduled Areas
    • So tribal voices are heard in policy
  • 4 The Governor regulates: May direct that Acts of Parliament or the State Legislature do not apply or apply with modification, and make regulations for peace and good government
Four powers of the Fifth Schedule

Tribes Advisory Council and Governor's regulations

PointDetail
TAC sizeUp to 20 members; about three-fourths from ST members of the State Legislative Assembly; if STs have little or no representation, the Governor nominates; not less than half must be ST persons
TAC roleAdvises on welfare and advancement of STs, especially matters the Governor refers
Governor's regulations may(i) prohibit or restrict land transfer by or among STs, (ii) regulate allotment of land to STs, (iii) regulate money-lending to STs
ProcessGovernor must consult the TAC (where it exists); every regulation needs the President's assent
  • Parliament can change this Schedule by law at any time, and such a law is not an amendment under Art. 368.
  • Shortcut: Prelims shortcut. Fifth = Governor + TAC + land protection (prevents transfer of tribal land to non-tribals; PYQ 2022 and 2019). Sixth = Councils + taxes + courts (self-government).
  • Sixth Schedule extras: Councils can set up village courts and District Council courts for certain civil and criminal matters involving tribal parties, apply customary law, and frame rules of procedure and appellate structures, under the supervision of the State High Court. They can levy taxes and fees on land and buildings, entry of goods into markets, animals, vehicles, professions, trade and callings, general services, roads and ferries, and use of water or resources, prepare budgets and keep funds.

Also in the handout

  • It is the core instrument that lets the Union and the States adopt special safeguards for land, livelihoods, custom, and governance where tribal communities are concentrated.
  • [[Additional information]] The Tribes Advisory Council (TAC) is a consultative body meant to keep policy grounded in tribal realities.
  • This fiscal space, though limited, underwrites real decision-making power at the local level.
  • This asymmetry appears illogical in principle.

4 · Seventh Schedule: who legislates on what#

  • The Seventh Schedule (Art. 246) distributes subjects between the Union, States and both through three lists: Union, State and Concurrent. National matters needing uniformity sit with Parliament
  • Local matters with States
  • Shared matters in the Concurrent List

How the lists evolved

  • 42nd Amendment (1976): Education, forests, wildlife, weights and measures moved from the State to the Concurrent List
  • 101st Amendment (2016): GST: taxation entries reworked for a unified indirect tax
  • Entry counts (this handout): Union 97, State 61, Concurrent 52 (counts change when entries are deleted, merged or modified)
Handout alert

Handout alert: In Part 2 (Centre-State Relations) the handout gives Union 98, State 59 (earlier 66), Concurrent 52 (up from 47). The Union and State figures differ from the 97 and 61 above. Check your class notes before quoting either.

Also in the handout

  • The Schedule keeps the federal balance workable:
    • National matters that demand uniformity sit with Parliament
    • Local matters rest with States
    • And shared matters appear in the Concurrent List so both tiers can legislate

5 · Eighth Schedule: 22 languages#

  • It recognises languages
  • It does not declare a national language (India has none). It is read with Arts. 343-351 (official language), especially Art. 351 (develop Hindi) and Arts. 350A-350B (linguistic minorities). Inclusion helps visibility: central recruitment exams, translation facilities in Parliament, schemes through the CIIL and Sahitya Akademi

How we reached 22

  • 1950: 14 languages: Assamese, Bengali, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Malayalam, Marathi, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Urdu
  • 21st Amendment (1967): + Sindhi → 15
  • 71st Amendment (1992): + Konkani, Manipuri (Meitei), Nepali → 18
  • 92nd Amendment (2003): + Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali → 22
  • Memory aid (Additions):
    • 1967 Sindhi
    • 1992 Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali ("KMN")
    • 2003 Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali ("BDMS": "Bad Dogs Maul Sheep")
Handout alert

What inclusion does not mean: It does not make a language the "official language of the Union" (Hindi in Devanagari with English as associate/working language under the Official Languages Act) and does not automatically make it a State's official language (States choose under Art. 345).

6 · Ninth Schedule: the protective list#

  • Art. 31B puts laws in this Schedule beyond challenge: they are "not deemed to be void" for violating Fundamental Rights, retrospectively too. Laws were placed through the 1st Amendment. Original purpose: protect agrarian reform (zamindari abolition, land ceilings). Later additions included tenancy, regulation of industries, reservations and other socio-economic laws. Only a constitutional amendment under Art. 368 can insert or delete items, unlike ordinary Schedules.

The court's journey on the Ninth Schedule

  • Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965): Upheld Parliament's wide amending power
  • Golaknath (1967):
    • Briefly curtailed that power
    • Clarificatory amendments followed
  • Kesavananda Bharati (24 April 1973): Amendments cannot damage the Basic Structure
  • Waman Rao (1981): Laws put in the Ninth Schedule after 24-04-1973 are open to basic-structure review
  • I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007):
    • Settled: no absolute immunity
    • Post-1973 insertions can be struck down if they damage the basic structure
The court's journey on the Ninth Schedule
  • Memory aid (Case order): Shankari, Sajjan, Golaknath, Kesavananda, Waman Rao, Coelho: "Some Singers Get Kind Words Cheaply".
  • Shortcut: Prelims trap. "A law in the Ninth Schedule cannot be examined by any court" is wrong. Parliament can place a law there (PYQ 2018: statement 1 true, statement 2 false). The Schedule was introduced under Nehru (1st Amendment; PYQ 2019).

Also in the handout

  • Article 31B operates with a strong validating clause: any law specified in the Ninth Schedule “shall not be deemed to be void” on the ground that it is inconsistent with any of the Fundamental Rights, and this validation applies retrospectively to past actions under those laws.
  • If the court finds that the law, in its effect, damages or destroys the Basic Structure, it can be struck down notwithstanding its presence in the Ninth Schedule.

7 · Tenth Schedule: anti-defection#

  • Inserted by the 52nd Amendment (1985), strengthened by the 91st (2003). Applies to Parliament and State legislatures
  • It aims to curb instability from opportunistic floor-crossing while keeping party systems intact

Who is disqualified, and when

MemberDisqualified if
Party member(i) Voluntarily gives up party membership (courts: can be inferred from conduct, not only formal resignation) or (ii) votes or abstains against the party whip without prior permission and the party does not condone within 15 days
Independent memberJoins any political party after the election
Nominated memberJoins a party after six months from taking the seat (within six months is allowed)
  • Memory aid (Four triggers): Gives up membership, Whip defied, Independent joins a party, Nominated joins after six months. "Going With India's Nation".

Who decides and how courts check

  • Complaint: To the Speaker or Chairman, who decides (once final)
  • Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992):
    • Law upheld
    • The Speaker acts as a tribunal, so decisions are subject to judicial review under Arts. 136, 226, 227, typically after the Speaker decides
  • Time limit:
    • None in the Schedule
    • The Court insists on a reasonable period

Advantages and criticisms

AdvantagesCriticisms
Stability: fewer opportunistic toppling of governmentsDissent versus defection: no clear line; curbs conscience and debate; elevates party bossism
Orderly realignment through the merger routeRetail versus wholesale: individual defections banned but group moves legitimised (the "split" route is now deleted)
Lower corruption and cost: fewer transaction-driven politics and frequent electionsOutside the House: no remedy for expulsion over extra-parliamentary acts; House conduct only
Constitutional status for political parties, for the first timeIndependent versus nominated: unequal treatment (nominated can join within six months)
Presiding officer as adjudicator: bias risk (party figure) and lack of judicial training; Speakers Rabi Ray (1991) and Shivraj Patil (1993) questioned it
  • Memory aid (Four advantages): Stability, Orderly merger, Lower corruption, Constitutional status for parties.

The 91st Amendment Act, 2003

What it did

ProvisionRule
Union ministry capMinisters (including PM) not more than 15% of Lok Sabha strength
State ministry capMinisters (including CM) not more than 15% of the Assembly strength, but not less than 12
Ministers barredA member of either House (Parliament or State) disqualified for defection is also disqualified from being a minister
Remunerative posts barredA defector cannot hold any remunerative political post: office under the Union or a State paid from public revenue, or under a body wholly or partly owned by them paid by that body, except purely compensatory pay

  • Shortcut: Numbers. 15% and 12: ministers cap and the State floor. One-third split gone, two-thirds merger stays.

Also in the handout

  • Although the Schedule does not prescribe a time limit, the Court has repeatedly stressed that disqualification questions must be decided within a reasonable period, given their bearing on the composition and functioning of the House.
  • Orderly Realignment via Merger: It permits democratic reconfiguration of parties through the merger route, providing a legitimate, rule-bound channel for collective shifts rather than clandestine defections. 14.Lower Political Corruption & Costs: Curbing defections reduces transaction-driven politics and non-developmental expenditure on frequent, irregular elections caused by engineered instability.
  • Wholesale Defections: The distinction drawn historically between individual and group defections proved irrational—banning retail while legitimising wholesale moves through group mechanisms (the ‘split’ route has since been deleted, but the critique highlights the original design flaw and continuing risks via other collective devices).
  • Presiding Officer as Adjudicator (Impartiality & Capacity Concerns): a.
  • Its core thrust was twofold: (i) close loopholes in the Tenth Schedule that enabled mass defections under the guise of “splits,” and (ii) remove office-based inducements (ministerships and other remunerative political posts) that often lay behind defections. It also introduced a numeric cap on the Council of Ministers to discourage oversized ministries used for political accommodation.

Traps#

Trap 1 · Third Schedule oaths The President's and Governors' oaths are not in the Third Schedule. Rule: President is in Art. 60, Governors in Art. 159.
Trap 2 · Ninth Schedule immunity 'A law in the Ninth Schedule cannot be examined by any court' is wrong (PYQ 2018). Parliament can place laws there, but post-24 April 1973 insertions face basic-structure review. Rule: protected, but not absolutely; remember Waman Rao and Coelho.
Trap 3 · Fifth versus Sixth Fifth covers Scheduled Areas in 10 States, run through the Governor's regulations and the TAC. Sixth covers tribal areas of only Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram, run by elected Autonomous District Councils. Rule: Fifth = Governor + TAC + land protection; Sixth = Councils + taxes + courts.
Trap 4 · Tenth Schedule details An independent member is disqualified if he joins a party; a nominated member only if he joins after six months. The one-third split exemption was deleted in 2003; only the two-thirds merger remains. Rule: independent = never, nominated = after six months; split gone, merger stays.

Book edition: simple words and memory words#

In simple words

  • The Schedules are lists and tables attached to the Constitution, so the Articles stay short. The long lists (oaths, languages, who legislates on what) live here.
  • There were 8 in 1950. Four were added later (9th, 10th, 11th, 12th), so there are 12 today.
  • Four carry most exam questions: the Fifth and Sixth (tribal areas), the Ninth (protected laws, Art. 31B) and the Tenth (anti-defection).
  • The Ninth Schedule is not absolute. Laws placed there after 24 April 1973 can be struck down if they damage the basic structure (Waman Rao 1981, Coelho 2007).
  • The Eleventh and Twelfth list the subjects of Panchayats (29) and Municipalities (18); the Eighth recognises 22 languages but declares no national language.

Key terms

Schedule
An annexed list or table that supports an Article; it adds detail without lengthening the main text.
Scheduled Area
An area declared under the Fifth Schedule for the administration and control of Scheduled Tribes.
Tribes Advisory Council (TAC)
A body of up to 20 members in each State with Scheduled Areas; it advises on tribal welfare (Fifth Schedule).
Autonomous District Council
An elected council with legislative, executive, limited judicial and fiscal powers in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram (Sixth Schedule).
Art. 31B
The Article that places laws in the Ninth Schedule so they are not deemed void for violating Fundamental Rights.
Defection
Giving up party membership or defying the whip; the ground for disqualification under the Tenth Schedule.
Merger
The only exception left in the Tenth Schedule: two-thirds of a party agree to merge. The one-third 'split' was deleted in 2003.
Concurrent List
The list in the Seventh Schedule on which both the Union and the States can legislate (52 entries).
