1 · Who the diaspora is#

Class + handout

The Indian diaspora is people whose origins can be traced to India, or Indian citizens settled abroad. It includes Overseas Citizens of India (OCIs), Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) and Non-Resident Indians (NRIs). India has the world’s largest diaspora; it links India with the global community, economically and strategically.

Key concept: diaspora diplomacy (handout)

Building strong ties with citizens and communities living abroad and using their networks, knowledge and influence to support national interests.

About 37 million overseas Indians as of 2026 (MEA). The community includes workers, students, professionals, entrepreneurs and historic groups: large, but not uniform.

Live in the news: the diaspora in the news

In the news · Jul 2026 CA

OCI goes fully digital: the e-OCI card

What happened
The Union Home Minister launched an e-Overseas Citizen of India (e-OCI) card for fully digital OCI services, alongside the FCRA 2.0 portal. The magazine restates the difference: an NRI is an Indian citizen living abroad; an OCI is a foreign citizen of Indian origin with a lifelong visa who can live, work and study in India indefinitely.
Use it in an answer
A small but concrete “Connect” step under the 4 Cs: easier paperwork for the 37-million community. Use it in the way-forward paragraph on digital and consular capacity.
Prelims hook
OCI = foreign citizen of Indian origin; NRI = Indian citizen abroad. FCRA = Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010.
Try first: NRI or OCI: which one is an Indian citizen?

The NRI. An OCI holds a foreign passport with a lifelong visa for India.

Source: Jul 2026 CA (quick updates: FCRA portal and e-OCI card).

3 · Why the diaspora matters for India#

Class + handout
Six dimensions (slides 5-6, handout pp. 73-74)

Brain drain (handout)

  • Skilled and educated people migrate to places with better opportunities, reducing human capital at the origin.

Brain circulation (handout)

  • Skilled migrants return with technology, capital, managerial skills and institutional knowledge.

Live in the news: 2026: the exam used the government’s own phrase

In the news · Mains 2026 + web

“A living bridge”: from a ministry statement to a 15-mark question

What happened
On Pravasi Bharatiya Divas (9 January 2026) the MEA called the diaspora “a living bridge connecting India and the world”. Seven months later GS-II Q19 opened with the same words. The August magazine’s cultural-diplomacy chapter quotes the question as its PYQ and lists Pravasi Bharatiya Divas and the Know India Programme as diaspora programmes.
Use it in an answer
Pattern worth knowing: UPSC borrows official phrases. Read MEA statements for vocabulary. The magazine also gives the soft-power example the handout uses: the diaspora helped push the 2008 India-US civil nuclear agreement in the US Congress.
Prelims hook
Cultural diplomacy runs through the Global Engagement Scheme (Ministry of Culture) and the ICCR (under the MEA).

Source: web: Newsonair, 9 Jan 2026 (collected for the PYQ Lab); Aug 2026 CA §3.7.

In the news · Aug 2026 CA

Mauritius and the Girmitiya memory

Why it matters
The Union Petroleum Minister visited Mauritius, whose Aapravasi Ghat (a UNESCO World Heritage Site) was the immigration depot for the British “great experiment” in indentured labour to replace slave labour.
Use it in an answer
The historic diaspora in one place: the Girmitiya link the handout names is a living asset in Indian Ocean diplomacy (Mauritius also hosts the IORA headquarters).
Prelims hook
Aapravasi Ghat, Port Louis: UNESCO site; the first site of the indentured-labour experiment.

Source: Aug 2026 CA §3.10.1 (high-level visits).

4 · Where the diaspora stops helping#

Class + handout
Seven limitations in leveraging the diaspora (slide 8, handout p. 74)
LimitationConcern
Remittance useMostly consumption, housing, health and education; they do not automatically become productive investment
Institutional trustBonds and investment depend on trust in Indian institutions, stability, regulation and research capacity
Unequal network accessNetworks may stay informal; smaller Indian firms find them hard to use
Political limitsIndian-origin legislators primarily serve host-country constituencies; their positions cannot be assumed to favour India
Soft-power limitsCultural popularity does not automatically translate into diplomatic support
Welfare as a bilateral issueWorkers’ and students’ welfare becomes a bilateral issue during visa changes, discrimination, conflicts or restrictions
Security risksMost mobilisation is peaceful, but small sections may finance separatism, spread violent propaganda, intimidate diplomats or attack missions
Use in the 2026 question

The question asks you to “critically examine” the diaspora as a living bridge. Section 3 is the bridge; this table is the “critically”. A balanced answer gives three of each.

Live in the news: limits, as the magazine sees them

In the news · Aug 2026 CA

“Diaspora 2.0”: from ceremony to structured partnership

Why it matters
The Standing Committee on External Affairs (2022 report, as quoted) found that Indian missions lack a structured mechanism for regular consultation with overseas Indians. The magazine’s fix is “Diaspora 2.0”: move from ceremonial engagement to structured partnership on skills, investment and second-generation cultural retention.
Use it in an answer
It matches this chapter’s limits table: networks stay informal, and remittances do not become investment by themselves. Quote “Diaspora 2.0” as a one-phrase way forward.
Prelims hook
Two committee reports: 2022 (cultural diplomacy, as quoted in the magazine) and 2025 (Indian Diaspora Overseas, as quoted in the handout).

Source: Aug 2026 CA §3.7 (cultural diplomacy).

In the news · Jul 2026 CA

Indian sailors killed at Odesa; US visa rules tighten

What happened
A Russian missile strike on a merchant vessel at Odesa killed ten crew, four of them Indian. The US Department of Homeland Security replaced “Duration of Status” with a fixed authorised stay for F (students), J (exchange visitors and researchers) and I (journalists) visas.
Use it in an answer
Two rows of the challenges table in the news: conflict risk (seafarers caught in wars far from West Asia) and immigration restrictions (students and researchers). A third headline from the same month: India’s Global Passport Index rank fell to 125th.
Prelims hook
Global Passport Index (Global Citizen Solutions) differs from the Henley index (visa-free destinations).

Source: Jul 2026 CA (conflict areas; §3.9.4; §3.9.3).

Model answer to compare (219 words; limit 250). Write yours first.

With about 37 million overseas Indians, India has the world’s largest diaspora. The MEA calls it “a living bridge”; the PM calls its members “Rashtradoot”.

How the bridge works, level by level
  • Household: remittances of US$135.4 billion (FY25), the world’s highest and about 3.8% of GDP, sustain families in Kerala and Punjab.
  • National: Resurgent India Bonds raised US$4.2 billion during the 1998 sanctions; leaders such as Pichai, Nadella and Banga, Silicon Valley start-up links and VAIBHAV, VAJRA and GIAN turn brain drain into brain circulation.
  • Bilateral: the Indian-American community backed the 2008 India-US civil nuclear agreement.
  • Global: yoga, Diwali, cinema and Girmitiya ties with Mauritius, Fiji and the Caribbean convert heritage into soft power.
Where the bridge breaks
  • Remittances fund consumption more than investment.
  • Indian-origin legislators serve host-country voters, and cultural appeal does not guarantee diplomatic votes.
  • Small sections finance separatism and target Indian missions.
  • Welfare crises become bilateral issues: over 400 student deaths (2018-24), H-1B curbs and sailors killed at Odesa (2026).
Way forward

India needs a differentiated policy for workers, students and professionals, a real-time migration database, the pending Overseas Mobility Bill, 2024, and a “Diaspora 2.0” partnership on skills and investment.

Guided by the 4 Cs, India must move from engagement to protection, partnership and productive global networks. Only then will goodwill become lasting strategic leverage.

Mains 2026 (15m, 250w): diaspora as a living bridge, critically examined

Intro: about 37 million overseas Indians, the world’s largest diaspora; the PM calls them “Rashtradoot”.

  • Economic factor: US$135.4 bn remittances (FY25); Resurgent India Bonds (1998); investment and Silicon Valley networks.
  • Knowledge network: professionals and leaders (Pichai, Nadella, Banga); brain circulation; VAIBHAV, VAJRA, GIAN.
  • Cultural heritage into influence: yoga, Diwali, cinema; Girmitiya links; lobbying for the 2008 nuclear deal; Howdy Modi.
  • Critically: remittances go to consumption; Indian-origin leaders serve host voters; culture does not guarantee votes; small sections finance separatism; welfare crises (visas, 400+ student deaths) become bilateral issues.
  • Way forward: differentiated policy, database, worker protection, the Overseas Mobility Bill.

Closing: a living bridge carries traffic both ways; under the 4 Cs it can become strategic leverage, not just goodwill.

Mains 2023 (10m, 150w): economic and political benefits of the diaspora in the West

Intro: the US (6.07 mn), Canada (3.24 mn) and UK (1.28 mn) host large, high-skill Indian communities.

  • Economic: the US alone sends 27.7% of remittances; investment, venture capital, Silicon Valley-India networks; knowledge and technology.
  • Political: Indian-origin legislators and leaders (Sunak, Harris); the 2008 nuclear deal; visibility for Indian concerns.
  • Caveat in one line: they serve host-country voters first.

Closing: benefits grow when India protects and connects (Care, Connect).

Mains 2020 (10m, 150w as printed in the paper): a “decisive” role in American and European politics and economy?

Intro: the diaspora is influential in the West; “decisive” overstates it.

  • For: leaders and legislators; the 2008 nuclear deal lobby; tech leadership (Pichai, Nadella); remittance and investment flows.
  • Against: host-country loyalties; a minority in electorates; culture does not translate into diplomatic votes; visa rules (H-1B) still tighten.

Closing: important and growing, but not decisive.

Mains 2017 (15m, 250w): the diaspora’s role in South-East Asian economies and societies

Intro: thin notes: the chapter does not cover South-East Asia specifically. Use the general frame and add class notes.

  • Segments present: migrant workers and the historic diaspora (handout: “migrant workers: Southeast Asia”).
  • Economy: business networks, trade links; society: culture, festivals, shared Buddhist and Ramayana heritage (Chapter 5, ASEAN).
  • Limits: identity and integration issues; host-country priorities.

Closing: a cultural and commercial bridge that supports Act East.
