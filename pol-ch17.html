In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • The State Legislature (Arts. 168-212, Part VI) makes laws on the State List and Concurrent List. Most states have only the Assembly (unicameral)
  • A few also have a Council (bicameral). The Governor is part of the legislature
  • Vidhan Sabha: directly elected, 500 maximum and 60 minimum seats (30 in Arunachal, Sikkim, Goa; 40 Mizoram; 46 Nagaland), 5-year term, dissolved early by the Governor. Vidhan Parishad: at most one-third of the Assembly and at least 40, permanent, one-third retire every 2 years, so a member serves 6 years.
  • A member must be an Indian citizen aged 25 (Assembly) or 30 (Council) and take the oath. Disqualified for an office of profit, unsound mind, insolvency, loss of citizenship, a Parliament law, or defection (Tenth Schedule; Speaker or Chairman decides).
  • Presiding officers: Speaker and Deputy Speaker (Art. 178), Chairman and Deputy Chairman (Art. 182). Only the Speaker decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill. The Governor summons (no gap above 6 months), prorogues and dissolves the Assembly.
  • Ordinary Bills:
    • The Council can delay only 4 months (3 + 1), there is no joint sitting and the Assembly prevails. Money Bills start only in the Assembly on the Governor's recommendation
    • The Council returns them within 14 days and cannot amend or reject. The Governor can assent, withhold, return or reserve (not return a Money Bill)
  • Members have civil-arrest immunity 40 days before and after a session and free speech under Art. 194(1). Parliament can create or abolish a Council under Art. 169 on the Assembly's resolution, and the Council is far weaker than the Rajya Sabha.

Key terms

Unicameral / Bicameral
One House (Assembly) or two Houses (Assembly + Council)
Continuing chamber
The Council is never dissolved; one-third of its members retire every 2 years
Prorogation
Formally ends a session (Governor notifies); different from adjournment (a pause) and dissolution (ends the Assembly's life)
Dissolution
Ends the life of the Assembly, at the 5-year mark or earlier by the Governor; the Council cannot be dissolved
Quorum
Minimum present to do business: 10 members or one-tenth of the House, whichever is higher (Art. 189)
Casting vote
The presiding officer votes only to break a tie
Suspensive veto
If the Governor returns a Bill and it is passed again, he must give assent
Money Bill
Financial Bill that can start only in the Assembly, on the Governor's recommendation, moved by a minister
Remember it as

Assembly 500/60, age 25, 5 years; Council one-third of Assembly (min 40), age 30, permanent; delay 3 + 1 = 4 months; Money Bill 14 days; no joint sitting.

1 · Structure and organisation#

  • The State Legislature is the law-making authority of the State for subjects in the State List and Concurrent List of the Seventh Schedule, much like Parliament at the Union level but adapted to State needs. Its provisions are in Articles 168 to 212, Part VI.
  • Some States have one chamber (unicameral), a few have two (bicameral): the choice is left flexible for local considerations of representation, cost and governance.

Unicameral against bicameral

  • Unicameral:
    • Legislative Assembly only
    • A passed Bill goes directly to the Governor for assent
  • Bicameral:
    • Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha) plus Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad)
    • A Bill also goes to the second House

Also in the handout

  • This mechanism safeguards against hasty or unrepresentative decision-making and maintains the credibility of legislative proceedings.
  • ## Legislative Procedure in the State Legislature The legislative procedure in a state legislature refers to the systematic process by which laws are proposed, debated, amended, and enacted. The Constitution of India provides a structured mechanism to ensure that law-making is orderly, transparent, and representative. The procedure differs slightly between unicameral and bicameral legislatures, particularly with regard to the role of the Legislative Council, where present.
  • ## Comparative Analysis of Legislative Procedures: Parliament vs State Legislature Understanding the legislative procedures at both the Union and State levels is crucial for grasping the federal structure and law-making in India. While the Constitution provides broadly similar mechanisms, there are important distinctions in the powers, processes, and checks between Parliament and state legislatures, especially in bicameral states.
  • This establishes the Assembly’s dominant role in state law-making.

2 · Governor and the two Houses#

  • The Governor is an integral part of the State Legislature (Art. 168), though in practice the Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister.

Governor's functions in legislative business

Dissolve the Legislative AssemblyArt. 174(2)(b)
Address the LegislatureArt. 176: at the first session after each general election and at the commencement of each year
Assent to BillsGives assent, withholds, returns for reconsideration (Art. 200)

The Council against the Assembly in a bicameral State

  • Money: the Assembly is supreme. A Money Bill originates only in the Assembly and the Council has only recommendatory powers: it cannot reject or amend it.
  • Ordinary Bills:
    • Assembly primacy (Art. 197). The Council can only delay: three months in the first instance and one month if the Bill is returned again
    • After that the Bill is deemed passed by both Houses
  • Accountability:
    • The Council cannot remove the Council of Ministers by a no-confidence motion
    • The executive is collectively responsible to the Assembly alone
  • Memory aid (Governor's links): Summon and prorogue, Address, Dissolve (Assembly only): "Someone Always Directs."

Also in the handout

  • Adjournments ensure that business is managed efficiently while preventing disruption due to lack of quorum or procedural issues.
  • However, the Council has restricted powers.

3 · The Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha)#

  • The Assembly is the main law-making body, directly elected by universal adult franchise from territorial constituencies (Art. 170, with suffrage under Art. 326). Each constituency elects one member, with an approximately uniform population-to-seat ratio.

Delimitation and readjustment

  • Governed by Arts. 170(3) and 327, carried out by Delimitation Commissions set up by Parliament.
  • 42nd Amendment (1976) froze the inter-State allocation of seats so States controlling population growth are not penalised.
  • 84th Amendment (2001) extended the freeze until the first census after 2026, while allowing internal rationalisation within States.

Reserved seats and Anglo-Indian nomination

  • Art. 332: seats reserved for SCs and STs in proportion to their population in the State. Only SC/ST candidates contest, but all eligible voters take part. Reservation was meant to be temporary and has been extended by several amendments.
  • Art. 333:
    • The Governor could nominate one Anglo-Indian member
    • abolished by the 104th Amendment Act, 2019, effective January 2020
  • Memory aid (Assembly numbers): Max 500, Min 60, Small exceptions 30, 40, 46: "More Members, Some smaller."

Also in the handout

  • These differences underline the stronger political role of the directly elected Assembly in representing popular will and controlling the state executive.
  • Delimitation and Readjustment of Seats To ensure fair representation, the Constitution mandates periodic readjustment of constituencies based on census data.
  • This mechanism ensures balance between demographic representation and population policy goals, allowing uniformity without discouraging population control efforts. Reservation of Seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes The Indian Constitution upholds the principle of social justice and inclusivity by reserving seats for Scheduled Castes (SCs) and Scheduled Tribes (STs) in State Legislative Assemblies.
  • Originally intended as a temporary measure, this reservation has been extended through multiple constitutional amendments to ensure continued political representation for marginalized communities.

  • The Council is the upper chamber, a house of revision and review (Art. 171, mixed method of representation).
  • Strength: not more than one-third of the Assembly and not less than 40.

Nature

  • A continuing body:
    • It cannot be dissolved
    • one-third retire every two years
  • Primarily deliberative: reviews, suggests, refines. It can delay non-Money Bills for a maximum of four months.

Who has a Council and who abolished it

  • The handout says Punjab and West Bengal abolished theirs, while Maharashtra, Bihar, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana keep theirs.
  • Art. 169: Parliament creates or abolishes a Council on a State Assembly resolution.

Arguments about Councils

  • In favour:
    • Bring expertise
    • A forum for specialists
    • Revising chamber that improves quality
  • Against:
    • Financial cost
    • Delays in lawmaking
    • Limited democratic legitimacy as many members are indirectly elected or nominated

5 · Duration of the two Houses#

Assembly against Council

  • Legislative Assembly:
    • Not a continuing House
    • five-year term from the first meeting after the general election (Art. 172(1))
    • Can be dissolved early
  • Legislative Council:
    • Permanent, not subject to dissolution (Art. 171(2) as cited)
    • one-third retire every two years
    • six-year term for each member

Assembly

  • Automatic dissolution after five years. Example in the handout: first meeting 1 March 2020 means the term would normally expire on 28 February 2025.
  • Early dissolution by the Governor under Art. 174(2)(b), normally on the Council of Ministers' advice, except in exceptional cases such as breakdown of constitutional machinery (Art. 356).
  • National Emergency (Art. 352): Parliament may by law extend the term by one year at a time, but not beyond six months after the Emergency ends. The 1975-77 Emergency is the handout's example.
  • After the Emergency ends, the Assembly must be re-elected within six months.

Council

  • Vacancies filled by elections and Governor's nominations. Example: in a Council of 90, 30 retire every two years.
  • Retiring members can be re-elected or re-nominated any number of times.
  • Continuity gives institutional memory and policy consistency while the Assembly is dissolved.

Life of an Assembly

  • First meeting after elections: Clock of five years starts (Art. 172(1))
  • Five years end: Automatic dissolution
  • Or Governor dissolves: Art. 174(2)(b), usually on advice
  • Emergency: Extension one year at a time, at most six months past the end of the Emergency
  • Memory aid (Why tenure matters): Democratic accountability, Continuity (Council), Balanced governance, Emergency flexibility: "Don't Cancel Basic Elections."
  • Key Articles: Art. 171(2), 172, 174(2)(b), 352 govern duration and continuity.

Also in the handout

  • This structure allows for stability in legislative functioning and preserves institutional memory.
  • The Constitution provides distinct rules regarding the tenure and continuity of these two houses to maintain a balance between democratic renewal and institutional stability.
  • Similar to the Lok Sabha, it is not a continuing chamber, meaning that it ceases to exist either upon the completion of its five-year term or through early dissolution.
  • Early dissolution allows for fresh elections when the ruling government loses majority support or political stability is disrupted.
  • This provision ensures continuity of governance during crises, while also safeguarding timely restoration of democratic processes once normalcy returns.
  • Each member thus serves a six-year term, providing a balance between legislative experience and regular infusion of fresh members.
  • Democratic Accountability: Regular elections ensure governments are answerable to the electorate.
  • Legislative Continuity: The Council maintains stability and experience in law-making.
  • These roles are critical for maintaining legislative discipline and upholding the constitutional framework.
  • Such a mechanism applies regardless of which House originated the bill.

6 · Membership: qualifications and disqualifications#

  • Citizen of India (Art. 173(1))
  • Takes an oath or affirmation before a person authorised by the Election Commission: bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution and uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India
  • Age: 25 for the Assembly, 30 for the Council.
  • Under the Representation of the People Act, 1951:
    • Assembly candidate must be an elector in any Assembly constituency of the State
    • Council candidate likewise an elector, and a Governor's nominee must reside in the State
    • Reserved-seat candidates must be SC or ST but may also contest general seats

Disqualifications under the Constitution (Art. 191)

  • Holds an office of profit under the Union or State Government (except a minister's office or others exempted by the State Legislature).
  • Unsound mind, declared by a competent court.
  • Undischarged insolvent.
  • Not a citizen, voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship, or owes allegiance to a foreign State.
  • Disqualified under a law made by Parliament.

Disqualifications under the RPA, 1951

  • 1. Conviction for certain election offences or corrupt practices. 2. Conviction for any offence with imprisonment of two years or more (not preventive detention). 3. Failure to file election expense accounts in time. 4. Interests in government contracts, works or services. 5. Director, managing agent or office-holder in a corporation with 25% or more government share. 6. Dismissal from government service for corruption or disloyalty. 7. Conviction for promoting enmity between groups or for bribery. 8. Punishment for practising or preaching social crimes like untouchability, dowry or sati.
  • Who decides: The Governor decides whether a member has become disqualified, but must first seek the Election Commission's opinion (Art. 192 per the Articles table).

Defection (Tenth Schedule)

  • Disqualified if the member defects from the party on whose ticket elected.
  • Assembly: Speaker decides. Council: Chairman decides.
  • The Supreme Court (1992 ruling) held such decisions subject to judicial review.

Two deciders

  • Ordinary disqualification: Governor decides, after the Election Commission's opinion
  • Defection:
    • Speaker (Assembly) or Chairman (Council)
    • Open to judicial review
  • Memory aid (Constitutional disqualifications): Office of profit, Unsound mind, Insolvent, Citizenship lost, Parliament's law: "Our Unruly India Can't Pass."

Also in the handout

  • The trade-off between inclusivity and efficiency has led to varying practices.
  • This system mandates that the government return to the electorate at fixed intervals to seek a renewed mandate.
  • This stability facilitates deliberate law-making and oversight, complementing the Assembly’s role in representing the people’s mandate.
  • Reserved Seats:
    • Candidates for reserved seats must belong to Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes
    • However, they may also contest general seats
  • Disqualifications for Membership The Constitution and statutory laws ensure that certain categories of individuals are prevented from becoming members of the State Legislature to maintain ethical and legal standards.
  • Powers and Responsibilities of the Speaker The Speaker’s role encompasses administrative, procedural, and judicial responsibilities, making the office pivotal for legislative governance.

7 · Oath and vacation of seats#

Oath or affirmation

  • Taken before the Governor or a person authorised by the Governor (Art. 188).
  • Pledge: true faith and allegiance to the Constitution, uphold sovereignty and integrity of India, faithfully discharge duties.
  • Until the oath is taken a member cannot vote or take part in proceedings, and privileges and immunities are not granted.
  • Penalty of Rs 500 per day (Art. 193 per the Articles table) for sitting or voting without the oath, or acting while knowing one is disqualified or prohibited by a law of Parliament or the State Legislature.
  • Salaries and allowances are fixed periodically by the State Legislature.

Vacation of seats

OtherElection declared void by a court; expelled by the House; elected President or Vice-President; appointed Governor of a State

8 · Presiding officers#

  • The Assembly has a Speaker and Deputy Speaker with a panel of chairpersons
  • The Council has a Chairman and Deputy Chairman with a panel of vice-chairmen

  • Shortcut: Resign-to-the-other. Each officer resigns in writing to the other half of the pair: Speaker to Deputy Speaker, Deputy to Speaker, Chairman to Deputy Chairman, Deputy Chairman to Chairman.

Speaker's powers

  • 1. Order and decorum:
    • Regulates debate, authority final and binding. 2. Interprets the Constitution, the Assembly's rules and precedents within the House. 3. Adjourns or suspends in the absence of a quorum. 4. Casting vote only in a tie
    • Otherwise does not vote. 5. Secret sittings on the Leader of the House's request. 6. Money Bill decision is final (Art. 110 as the handout cites, and Art. 199 later). 7. Disqualification cases under the Tenth Schedule. 8. Committees: appoints chairpersons and supervises
    • Chairs the Business Advisory, Rules and General Purposes committees

Deputy Speaker and panel

  • Acts as presiding officer when the Speaker is absent or the office is vacant, with all the Speaker's powers, including committee management when required.
  • The Speaker nominates a panel of chairpersons
  • Any member of the panel presides when both Speaker and Deputy are absent, with the Speaker's powers, until a new panel is nominated

Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Council

  • Chairman's powers resemble the Speaker's: conducts proceedings, interprets Council rules, appoints committee chairpersons.
  • Unlike the Speaker, the Chairman cannot decide whether a Bill is a Money Bill.
  • The Deputy Chairman acts as Chairman when the post is vacant and presides in the Chairman's absence.
  • The Chairman nominates a panel of vice-chairmen to preside in the absence of both.
  • Salaries and allowances of the Speaker, Deputy Speaker, Chairman and Deputy Chairman are fixed by the State Legislature and are charged on the State's Consolidated Fund, so are not voted annually.
  • Memory aid (Speaker's eight): Order, Interpretation, Adjournment, Voting (casting), Secret sittings, Money Bill decision, Disqualification, Committees: "Our Intelligent Assembly Values Silent Major Debates Closely."

10 · Ordinary Bills#

  • An ordinary Bill is neither a Money Bill nor a Constitution Amendment Bill. It can start in either House (or the Assembly in a unicameral State) and can be moved by a minister or private member.

Passage in the first House

  • First Reading: Introduction, no discussion
  • Second Reading: Clause-by-clause detailed debate
  • Third Reading: Final discussion and vote on the whole Bill
  • Sent to the second House: In a unicameral State, straight to the Governor

Council's four options on an Assembly Bill

  • 1. Pass without amendments. 2. Pass with amendments and return it. 3. Reject outright. 4. Take no action and keep it pending.
  • If passed without amendment, or the Assembly accepts the amendments, the Bill is sent to the Governor.
  • If the Council rejects it, suggests unacceptable amendments, or does not act within three months, the Assembly may pass it again. If the Council again rejects or delays (within one month), it is deemed passed by both Houses in the Assembly's form.
  • Delay power: The Council can delay for at most four months (three plus one). There is no joint sitting in a State. A Bill that starts in the Council and is rejected by the Assembly lapses.
The Council's four options on an Assembly Bill
PART
  • PPass the Bill without amendments
  • AAmend and return it to the Assembly
  • RReject the Bill outright
  • TTake no action: keep it pending

The Council plays only a PART in law-making: in the last three cases the Assembly can pass the Bill again and the Council can delay at most 3 + 1 months. The order is rearranged for memory (the options are unordered). (Memory aid, not from the handout.)

Governor's assent (Art. 200): four options

  • Memory aid (Governor's four): Assent, Withhold, Return, Reserve for the President: "Always Weigh Rules Rigorously."
  • If returned and passed again by the House(s) with or without amendments, the Governor must give assent: a suspensive veto, not absolute.

President on reserved Bills (Art. 201)

  • May give assent, withhold assent, or return the Bill for reconsideration.
  • After return the House(s) must pass it again within six months, then it goes back to the President.
  • The Constitution does not clearly say whether the President must assent after reconsideration.
  • Memory aid (President's three): Assent, Withhold, Return: "All Will Return."

  • Assent and Presidential/Gubernatorial Oversight: After passage, the bill is presented to the President at the Centre or the Governor in states.

13 · Position of the Legislative Council#

  • The Council is largely advisory and subordinate: equal to the Assembly in a few areas, unequal in most.

Areas of parity

  • 1. Ordinary Bills: takes part, but the Assembly prevails on disagreement. 2. Ordinances (Art. 213): ordinances promulgated by the Governor must be approved by both Houses. 3. Ministers (Art. 164): ministers, including the Chief Minister, can be members of either House, yet remain responsible only to the Assembly. 4. Reports of the State Finance Commission, State Public Service Commission and the CAG are reviewed. 5. PSC jurisdiction: can discuss proposals to enlarge the State PSC's powers.

Areas of subordination

  • 1. Money Bills:
    • Only suggestions, return within 14 days. 2. Money Bill status: the Speaker decides (Art. 199). 3. Ordinary Bills: Assembly can override
    • Delay up to four months
    • The Council is not a revising chamber unlike the Rajya Sabha. 4. Finance: can discuss the budget but cannot vote on Demands for Grants. 5. Control over government: no no-confidence motion
    • Ministers answer only to the Assembly. 6. Bills from the Council: lapse if the Assembly rejects them. 7. Elections: no part in electing the President or the State's Rajya Sabha members. 8. Constitution Amendments: the Assembly's will prevails in ratification. 9. Existence: Parliament can abolish the Council under Art. 169, but only on the Assembly's recommendation
  • Memory aid (Nine weaknesses): Money Bills, Speaker decides status, Ordinary Bills overridden, Finance (no vote on grants), Control (no no-confidence), Bills from Council lapse, Elections (President, RS), Amendments, eXistence (Art. 169): "My Small Old Father Can't Beat Every Angry X-ray."

Also in the handout

  • This ensures that proposals affecting state finances are carefully controlled and introduced by the executive responsible for the state budget.
  • Its powers and relevance can be examined under two broad categories: areas where it is equal to the Assembly and areas where it is subordinate or unequal.

14 · Privileges of the State Legislature#

  • Special rights and immunities for the Houses, committees and members, to protect independence, authority and dignity. They extend to State ministers and the Advocate-General, but not to the Governor, though the Governor is part of the legislature.

Collective and individual

  • Collective (the House):
    • Publish reports, debates and proceedings and stop others from publishing
    • Exclude outsiders and hold secret sittings
    • Make rules and adjudicate breaches
    • Punish members and outsiders for contempt (reprimand, admonition, imprisonment, suspension, expulsion)
    • Be told at once of a member's arrest, detention, conviction, imprisonment or release
    • Inquire, summon witnesses, call for documents
    • Courts cannot question proceedings
    • No arrest or legal process within the precincts without the presiding officer's permission
  • Individual (members):
    • Freedom from arrest in civil cases during the session and 40 days before and after (not criminal cases or preventive detention)
    • freedom of speech in the House and committees (Art. 194(1))
    • Exempt from jury service
    • May refuse to appear as a witness in courts during sessions
  • Memory aid (Individual privileges): Arrest immunity (civil, 40 days), Speech (Art. 194(1)), Jury exemption, Witness refusal: "All Speakers Joke Wisely."
  • Rationale: These immunities let legislators work without fear of intimidation, litigation or outside pressure and reinforce separation of powers between legislature and judiciary.

Also in the handout

  • These provisions ensure that legislators are eligible, competent, and free from conflicts of interest, thereby upholding the integrity of the legislative process.
  • However, they do not have voting rights, preserving the principle of elected representation. This provision allows ministers to explain government policies, introduce bills, respond to questions, and engage in debates relevant to their departments, thereby promoting accountability and transparency.
  • They also protect legislators in performing their duties, ensuring that they can discharge their responsibilities freely and effectively.
  • The Houses are also entitled to receive immediate information regarding the arrest, detention, conviction, imprisonment, or release of any member, reinforcing legislative oversight over potential infringements on members’ freedoms. They can institute inquiries, summon witnesses, and call for relevant documents, ensuring robust investigative powers.

  • Council history:

Recall sheet#

Cover the right column and answer. Same questions as the flashcards.

What do Arts. 168 to 212 deal with?
Part VI provisions on the State Legislature: organisation, powers, privileges and functioning.
Who is part of the State Legislature besides the Houses?
The Governor (Art. 168).
What are the Assembly's maximum and minimum strengths?
Maximum 500, minimum 60 (30 for Arunachal, Sikkim, Goa; 40 Mizoram; 46 Nagaland, as the handout states).
What did the 42nd and 84th Amendments do for delimitation?
42nd (1976) froze inter-State seat allocation; 84th (2001) extended it to the first census after 2026, allowing internal rationalisation.
What does Art. 332 give?
Reserved seats for SCs and STs in proportion to their population in the State.
What ended Anglo-Indian nomination?
The 104th Amendment Act, 2019, effective January 2020 (Art. 333).
What is the Council's size rule?
Not more than one-third of the Assembly and not less than 40 members.
How is the Council composed?
Graduates 1/12; teachers 1/12; local authorities nearly 1/3; Assembly members nearly 1/3; remainder nominated by the Governor.
How long is a Council member's term?
Six years; one-third retire every two years; the Council cannot be dissolved.
Who can abolish a Council?
Parliament, under Art. 169, on a State Assembly resolution.
Which States abolished Councils per the handout?
Punjab and West Bengal.
How long is an Assembly's term and which Article?
Five years from the first meeting after the general election (Art. 172(1)).
How can an Assembly's term be extended?
By Parliament during a National Emergency, one year at a time, not beyond six months after it ends (Art. 352).
Minimum ages for membership?
25 for the Assembly, 30 for the Council.
Who decides ordinary disqualification and defection?
Ordinary: the Governor after the Election Commission's opinion; defection: Speaker (Assembly) or Chairman (Council), subject to judicial review.
Which disqualifications are in Art. 191?
Office of profit, unsound mind, undischarged insolvency, citizenship loss or foreign allegiance, disqualification by Parliament's law.
What is the penalty for sitting or voting without taking the oath?
Rs 500 per day.
Who administers the oath?
The Governor or a person authorised by the Governor (Art. 188).
What happens if a member is absent 60 consecutive days?
The House may declare the seat vacant.
How is a Speaker removed?
By a resolution passed by a majority of all Assembly members after 14 days' notice.
Which committees does the Speaker chair?
Business Advisory, Rules and General Purposes.
Can the Chairman decide Money Bill status?
No: only the Speaker of the Assembly can.
What is prorogation?
The formal end of a session by the Governor's notification; adjournment is only a temporary break.
What is the quorum and its Article?
Ten members or one-tenth, whichever is higher (Art. 189).
What are the Council's four options on an Assembly Bill?
Pass it, pass with amendments, reject it, or keep it pending.
What are the Governor's four options under Art. 200?
Assent, withhold, return for reconsideration, reserve for the President.
What happens when a Money Bill reaches the Council?
It can only recommend and must return the Bill within 14 days; the Assembly may accept or reject the recommendations.
Why is there no joint sitting in a State?
The Assembly prevails after a second passage; a Bill from the Council rejected by the Assembly lapses.
Name two things the Council cannot do.
Vote Demands for Grants; move a no-confidence motion; take part in electing the President or State's Rajya Sabha members.
What are the individual privileges of members?
Civil-arrest freedom for the session and 40 days either side, freedom of speech (Art. 194(1)), jury exemption, refusal to appear as witness during sessions.
Do privileges extend to the Governor?
No: privileges cover Houses, committees, members, ministers and the Advocate-General, not the Governor.
Mains 2021: Constitutional provisions on Legislative Councils and their working?
Art. 169 (Parliament creates or abolishes on an Assembly resolution), Art. 171 (strength and five categories), permanent house with one-third retiring every two years; delay power of four months; no vote on grants, no no-confidence, subordinate to the Assembly; handout lists Punjab and West Bengal abolishing, and six Councils in its table.
Mains 2023: Role of Presiding Officers in order, impartiality and democratic practice?
Speaker and Chairman regulate debate, interpret rules, adjourn without quorum, casting vote only in a tie, decide Money Bill status (Speaker) and Tenth Schedule cases, chair key committees; independence supported by Consolidated Fund salaries and removal only by majority of all members on 14 days' notice.
Mains 2023: Reduced use of Article 356 since the mid-1990s?
Not covered in this handout beyond Art. 356 appearing as the exceptional ground for dissolution of an Assembly without the Council of Ministers' advice; build this answer from the Centre-State and Governor chapters.
Mains 2001: Why is it said that the centre has absolute veto over State Legislature

Traps#

Trap 1 · Is the Council a revising House? The handout introduces the Council as a house of revision and review, yet its list of subordinate areas says the Council is not a revising chamber like the Rajya Sabha. It can only delay an ordinary Bill by up to four months. Rule: in the exam call it a delaying and advisory House: 3 months + 1 month, then the Assembly prevails.
Trap 2 · How many states have a Council The text names Maharashtra, Bihar, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, but its strength table also gives Uttar Pradesh a Council of 100. Rule: six states: AP 58, Bihar 75, Karnataka 75, Maharashtra 78, Telangana 40, UP 100.
Trap 3 · Removal of the Speaker: which majority The voting section says a special majority is needed to remove the Speaker or Chairman, but the Speaker section says a resolution passed by a majority of all members after 14 days' notice. Rule: write majority of all members of the House with 14 days' notice.
Trap 4 · Money Bill article numbers The handout names Art. 199 for origin of Money Bills and the Speaker's decision, but also Art. 110 in the Speaker's powers; its Key Articles table gives Art. 198 as the special procedure and Art. 199 as the definition. Rule: states = Art. 198 (procedure) and Art. 199 (definition); Art. 110 is the Union version.
