1 · THE President · Nominal HEAD · Discretion · Election · Usa-india-france#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 2-4. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The President is the de jure head, acting on binding ministerial advice (Art 74). Real discretion exists mainly in hung Parliaments, plus one return of advice or bills. The balanced view: neither a rubber stamp nor an active ruler.

Union Executive AND THE President

Union Executive

  • Part V, Chapter I, Arts 52-78: President, Vice-President, Council of Ministers headed by the PM, Attorney-General
  • Art 52 office of President
  • Art 53 executive power vested in the President

Why indirect election

  • parliamentary logic: a nominal head needs no direct mandate
  • avoids rivalry with the PM over popular mandate
  • cost and complexity of a national poll
  • federal representation through MPs and MLAs (Arts 54, 55)

Three roles

  • Head of State (first citizen)
  • Supreme Commander of the armed forces
  • Nominal/constitutional head (de jure executive)

Binding Advice (article 74)

  1. Art 74(1)
    President acts on aid and advice of the Council of Ministers; the advice is binding
  2. 1976
    42nd Amendment: made binding advice explicit
  3. 1978
    44th Amendment: President may return advice once for reconsideration; reconsidered advice is binding
  4. Art 74(2)
    courts cannot inquire into the advice; S. R. Bommai (1994): advice is non-justiciable but the material behind it can be examined

Why a nominal head

  • continuity when governments fall
  • non-partisan symbol
  • separates Head of State from Head of Government
  • limited discretion in crises such as a hung Parliament

Discretionary Powers

Situations

  • choosing a PM when no party has a clear majority
  • dismissing a PM who lost the Lok Sabha majority but will not resign (Art 75(2))
  • suspensive veto: return a bill once
  • refuse dissolution advice from a PM who has lost the majority
  • act until a new PM is found if the PM dies suddenly
  • pocket veto: keep a bill pending without return or assent
Note

K. R. Narayanan, 1997: returned the Cabinet's advice to impose President's Rule in Uttar Pradesh, using moral authority.

Rubber stamp

  • Art 74 binds the President
  • wide powers exercised on advice
  • no formal override ever

More than a stamp

  • suspensive and pocket veto (Zail Singh)
  • hung Parliament choices matter
  • Narayanan 1997
  • non-partisan legitimising check
  • R. Venkataraman (My Presidential Years): right to ask questions and seek reconsideration
Way forward / key point

Balanced view: neither a mere rubber stamp nor an active ruler; real role in hung Parliaments, instability and emergencies.

Presidential Election

Criticism of the system

  • highly complex
  • value of MLA votes differs by State
  • it is neither true PR nor plain STV: one post cannot be divided proportionally, so it is really an alternative vote style, calling it PR is a "constitutional misnomer"

2 · Ordinance-making Power · Article 123 · Court Cases · Debate#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 4-7. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Under Art 123 the President (on advice) can issue an ordinance when a House is not in session. It has the force of an Act but lapses six weeks after Parliament reassembles. Courts forbid repeated re-promulgation (Wadhwa, Krishna Kumar Singh).

Article 123: Conditions

When

  • Either House (or both) not in session
  • If both are sitting, no ordinance
  • Same force as an Act but temporary
  • Usable in peacetime (not an Emergency power)
  • Rare in democracies
  • UK and USA have none
  • Ambedkar: to meet a "situation which may suddenly and immediately arise"

Conditions and limits

  • President satisfied of immediate necessity (acts on Art 74 advice)
  • 38th Amendment 1975 made satisfaction final
  • 44th Amendment 1978 removed that, restoring review for mala fide or irrelevant material
  • subject matter:
    • Union and Concurrent List
    • Can amend or repeal laws, retrospective subject to Art 20(1)
  • cannot violate Fundamental Rights or amend the Constitution (Art 368)

Duration

  • laid before both Houses on reassembly
  • ceases six weeks after reassembly (from the later date) unless replaced by an Act
  • ends earlier if both Houses pass disapproving resolutions
  • maximum normally six months + six weeks (Art 85(1) interval)
  • may be withdrawn anytime
  • Lok Sabha Rule 71(1): replacing Bill needs a statement of why urgent
Memory aid · SIX-6-6

Duration: 6 weeks after reassembly; at most 6 months gap + 6 weeks. (own mnemonic)

Judicial Control

  1. 1970
    R. C. Cooper: ordinance power has constitutional limits; challengeable for violating rights
  2. 1982
    A. K. Roy: ordinance-making is a legislative power with constitutional limits; judicial review
  3. 1987
    D. C. Wadhwa v. Bihar: Bihar promulgated 256 ordinances (1967-81), some kept up to 14 years; re-promulgation is a "fraud on the Constitution"
  4. 2017
    Krishna Kumar Singh v. Bihar (7 judges): repeated ordinances impermissible; laying is mandatory; rights under an ordinance do not automatically survive expiry (except where reversing is impossible, impractical or against public interest); courts check for misuse

Issues

  • separation of powers blurred
  • bypasses debate and committees
  • fait accompli (asset transfers, restructuring)
  • federal: Governors have Art 213 power

H. M. Seervai defends

  • Strong judicial review can strike a law at once
  • Ordinance prevents a legal vacuum in recess

Counter

  • USA has equally strong review but no decree power
  • Uses emergency sessions

Way forward / key point

Way forward: convene Parliament within one or two weeks of major ordinances; President to use the proviso to Art 74(1) to return questionable advice; keep actions reversible (Krishna Kumar Singh).

3 · President: India VS Germany · Vice-president · Prime Minister#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 7-10. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • India and Germany both have nominal Presidents, but India's has wider formal powers (ordinances, assent, veto types). India's VP chairs the Rajya Sabha and has no executive role, unlike the US VP. The PM is the real executive, unlike the US President who is both Head of State and Government.

4 · Parliamentary System · Features · Merits · Limits · India VS Britain#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 10-12. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • India follows the parliamentary (cabinet, Westminster) model at Union (Arts 74, 75) and State (Arts 163, 164) levels. Its merits are harmony and accountability; its demerits are instability and cabinet dictatorship. India differs from Britain in being a republic with a written constitution and judicial review.

Features

Meaning

  • executive politically accountable to the legislature:
    • India, Britain, Japan, Canada
    • Presidential (fixed tenure): USA, Brazil
  • also: Cabinet / Responsible / Westminster model
  • PM seen as primus inter pares
  • Crossman, Mackintosh: "prime ministerial government"

Merits AND Limits

Advantages

  • harmony of executive and legislature
  • responsible government (Question Hour, committees, no-confidence)
  • checks on arbitrary power
  • alternative government without fresh polls (Jennings: Leader of Opposition = alternative PM)
  • broad representation

Limitations

  • instability (Charan Singh, V. P. Singh, Chandra Shekhar, I. K. Gujral)
  • policy discontinuity (Janata 1977-79)
  • cabinet dictatorship (Laski, Muir; Emergency 1975-77)
  • fusion of powers (Bagehot: "hyphen that joins the buckle")
  • non-expert ministers
  • coalition compulsions

Why India chose it

  • familiarity: GoI Acts 1919 and 1935
  • Ambedkar:
    • Balance stability and responsibility
    • Preferred accountability
  • avoid deadlock
  • accommodate diversity
  • flexibility
  • Swaran Singh Committee 1976 retained it

5 · Council OF Ministers · Cabinet · Committees · Cabinet Secretariat · PMO · Secretariat#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 13-18. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Council of Ministers has three ranks (Cabinet, MoS, Deputy) and is capped at 15% of the Lok Sabha (81) by the 91st Amendment. The Cabinet is the real policy body, supported by committees, the Cabinet Secretariat and the PMO. Concerns: PMO centralisation and weak scrutiny.

Council OF Ministers

Ranks (by convention)

  • Cabinet Ministers: key portfolios (Defence, Home, Finance, External Affairs), chaired by the PM
  • Ministers of State: attached to a Cabinet Minister, or Independent Charge (may attend Cabinet when invited)
  • Deputy Ministers: assist, no independent charge, rarely used now
Note

Size cap: 91st Amendment, 2003, Art 75(1A): ministers including the PM not over 15% of Lok Sabha: with 543 seats, 81.

Smaller council

  • clear decisions
  • lower expenditure
  • no rewarding of allies
  • accountability

Larger council

  • scale needs coverage
  • regional/community representation
  • coalition partners
  • specialised portfolios
Value addition

First ARC (1966): Cabinet about 16 including PM; Council about 40, never above 45; a Deputy PM; a Cabinet Committee on Administration.

Cabinet

Status

  • Ramsay Muir: "steering wheel of the ship of State"
  • Word "Cabinet" entered the Constitution via the 44th Amendment 1978
  • Art 352(3): Emergency needs the Cabinet's written decision
BasisCouncil of MinistersCabinet
Compositionall ministersPM + Cabinet Ministers
Sizelargersmaller
Roleimplements; executive responsibilityprincipal policy-making
Meetingsrarely as a wholeregularly
Articles74, 75352(3)

Functions

  • policy formulation
  • legislative agenda
  • financial direction (Budget)
  • administrative coordination
  • appointments and crisis management

Checks

  • judicial review
  • Parliament (questions, motions, committees)
  • EC, CAG and other bodies
  • public opinion, media, opposition
Value addition

"The creation dominates the creator": Cabinet drawn from Parliament but controls its agenda. Kitchen Cabinet: informal inner group (e.g. P. N. Haksar, D. P. Dhar under Indira Gandhi); faster, secret, but dilutes the Cabinet.

Big four committees: Political · Economic · Security · Appointments. (own mnemonic)

Issues

  • irregular meetings
  • junior ministers have limited role
  • political stature over expertise
  • PM chairs many, centralisation
  • weak scrutiny by the full Cabinet

Cabinet Secretariat, PMO, Secretariat

Cabinet Secretariat

  • Under the PM
  • Chief coordinating agency
  • agendas, minutes, follow-up of decisions
  • ensures Transaction of Business and Allocation of Business Rules, 1961
  • Committees of Secretaries for inter-ministerial issues
  • Crisis coordination

Cabinet Secretary

  • Senior-most civil servant
  • Ex officio Chairman of Civil Services Board
  • Buffer between political executive and civil services
  • "conscience keeper"
  • N. R. Pillai first
  • T. V. Somanathan current (verify)
  • tenure up to four years, plus up to 3 months extension

PMO

  • Non-constitutional
  • Prime Minister's Secretariat 15 Aug 1947, renamed PMO 28 March 1977
  • policy advice, coordination, monitoring, departments in PM's charge (Atomic Energy, Space), NITI Aayog, public relations
  • over-centralisation: weak Cabinet deliberation, ministers as implementers, parallel unelected power, delays, States sidelined
  • work through established systems

Central Secretariat

  • headquarters of ministries and departments
  • split system:
    • Policy in Secretariat
    • Implementation by attached/subordinate offices and field agencies
  • policy, legislative drafts, Budget, supervision, coordination (CAG, UPSC), institutional memory
  • issues: file layers, generalist bias, technology gaps, overlap, weak coordination with States
  • reforms:
    • Second ARC flatter structure
    • Lateral entry
    • E-Office
    • Merge ministries
    • Mission Karmayogi

6 · 130th Amendment BILL 2025 · Removal OF Ministers IN Custody · Basic Structure#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 18-20 (current affairs, verify). Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The Bill (introduced 20 Aug 2025, referred to a JPC) would remove a PM, CM or minister who stays in custody 30 consecutive days for an offence punishable with 5+ years. Supporters cite criminalisation of politics; critics cite presumption of innocence, federalism and parliamentary democracy.

WHAT THE BILL DOES

Note

Current-affairs item. Status per the handout: under parliamentary examination (JPC); not law. Verify latest status.

Provisions

  • Introduced in Lok Sabha on 20 August 2025 by the Home Minister
  • Referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee
  • covers PM, Union Ministers, CMs, State/UT ministers detained 30 consecutive days for an offence punishable with 5 years or more
  • Ministers:
    • President (on PM's advice) or Governor (on CM's advice) must remove them by the 31st day
    • Otherwise they cease to hold office from the 32nd day
  • PM and CMs: must resign by the 31st day, else cease from the 32nd
  • reappointment allowed after release
  • Companion Bills for Puducherry and J&K
  • Delhi via amendment to Art 239AA
Memory aid · 30-31-32

30 days custody → advice by 31st day → office ends on 32nd. (own mnemonic)

Rationale

  • no separate criminal standard for ministers
  • RPA 1951 Section 8: disqualification only after conviction, which takes years
  • avoid "governing from jail"

Basic Structure Concerns

Parliamentary democracy

  • P. V. Narasimha Rao v. State (1998):
    • Parliamentary democracy is part of basic structure
    • Art 75(3)
  • Manoj Narula (2014):
    • PM/CM prerogative in choosing ministers
    • Automatic removal restricts it
  • investigations could decide tenures without a no-confidence vote

Federalism

  • S. R. Bommai (1994): federalism is basic structure
  • arrest by ED, CBI or NIA could remove a State CM (Union interference)
  • State police against Union ministers or another State's CM: confrontation

Rule of law and innocence

  • BNSS Section 35:
    • Arrest on reasonable suspicion
    • Not proof of guilt
  • BNSS Section 187:
    • Default bail after 60/90 days
    • 30 days may pass before charges
  • strict bail in PMLA Section 45 and UAPA Section 43D(5)
  • removal before a judicial finding: arbitrariness, due process, Arts 14 and 21
