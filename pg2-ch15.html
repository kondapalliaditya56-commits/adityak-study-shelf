1 · THE President · Election · Nominal HEAD · Binding Advice · Discretion · Rubber Stamp Debate#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 2-4. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The President is the head of State and the nominal (de jure) executive, while the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers are the real (de facto) executive. The President must act on their advice (Article 74), but can send it back once. In a hung Parliament and in a few other situations the President has real discretion. So the office is neither a rubber stamp nor an active ruler.

THE Union Executive AND THE President

Union Executive (Part V, Chapter I, Articles 52 to 78)

  • The Union Executive is the branch that implements laws and runs the day-to-day administration of the country.
  • It consists of: (1) the President, (2) the Vice-President, (3) the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, and (4) the Attorney-General of India.
  • Among these the President holds the highest constitutional position. He is the head of the State and all executive actions of the Union are taken in his name.

Why is the President elected indirectly? The Constituent Assembly debated this deeply before finalising Articles 54 and 55.

  • Parliamentary system logic: since the President is only a nominal head and not the real executive, a direct election like that of the US President seemed unnecessary.
  • Avoiding political rivalry: a direct national election could create conflict between the President and the Prime Minister, both claiming a popular mandate.
  • Cost and complexity: a direct election over so large an electorate would be slow and expensive, especially for a largely ceremonial office.
  • Federal representation: including elected members of both Parliament and the State Assemblies means the President represents both the Union and the States.

Position AND ROLE: REAL HEAD vs Nominal HEAD

Constitutional position

  • Article 52 creates the office of the President. Article 53 vests the executive power of the Union in the President, to be used directly or through subordinate officers, in accordance with the Constitution.
  • The President holds three roles:
    • Head of State (first citizen and symbol of national unity)
    • Supreme Commander of the Armed Forces
    • And constitutional or nominal head, the de jure executive but not the real executive

Binding nature of ministerial advice (Article 74)

  • Article 74(1): the President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, and this advice is binding.
  • 42nd Amendment (1976) made it explicit that the President must act on ministerial advice, removing any doubt about discretionary power.
  • 44th Amendment (1978) allowed the President to send the advice back once for reconsideration. But if the Council reconsiders and repeats the advice, it is binding.
  • Article 74(2) bars courts from inquiring into what advice ministers gave the President. But in S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) the Supreme Court clarified that while the advice itself is non-justiciable, the material on which it is based can be examined by courts.

Why have a nominal head at all?

  • Continuity and stability: the President stays in office even when a government falls, so there is no constitutional vacuum.
  • Non-partisan head: a symbol of national unity above party politics.
  • Prevents concentration of power: separates the Head of State from the Head of Government.
  • Constitutional safeguard: can return advice for reconsideration and use limited discretion, for example during a hung Parliament.
  • The difference between the de jure executive (President) and the de facto executive (PM and Council of Ministers) is a defining feature of India’s parliamentary democracy.

Discretionary Powers OF THE President

Situations where the President can act without being bound by the Council’s advice

  • Appointing the PM when no party has a clear majority in the Lok Sabha: the President uses personal judgment to invite the leader most likely to command confidence.
  • Dismissing a PM who has lost the Lok Sabha majority but refuses to resign (Article 75(2): ministers hold office during the President’s pleasure).
  • Returning a Bill for reconsideration (suspensive veto): can be done once.
  • Refusing dissolution of the Lok Sabha if a PM who has lost the majority advises it.
  • If the PM dies suddenly the Council of Ministers is automatically dissolved
  • The President can act until a new PM is formed and then appoint a new PM who can command a majority
  • Pocket veto: keeping a Bill pending without formal return or assent.
Note

K. R. Narayanan (1997): he returned the Cabinet’s advice to impose President’s Rule in Uttar Pradesh. This is an example of the President using moral authority even without formal legal power.

Assessment: Rubber Stamp OR Constitutional Guardian?

Arguments: rubber stamp

  • Article 74 makes Cabinet advice binding
  • The President “acts on advice” with no formal right to deviate
  • Vast executive, legislative, financial and military powers are used entirely on ministerial advice.
  • India has no history of a President formally overriding a Cabinet decision.

Arguments: more than a rubber stamp

  • The suspensive veto lets the President send a matter back once, forcing the government to rethink.
  • The pocket veto (unique to India) allows indefinite delay, as shown by President Zail Singh.
  • Discretion in hung-assembly situations is real: choosing who forms the government is not trivial.
  • Narayanan’s return of the UP President’s Rule advice shows moral authority can be used within conventions.
  • In a majoritarian democracy the President’s non-partisan role is a legitimising check on arbitrary parliamentary action.
  • R. Venkataraman in My Presidential Years noted that the ability to ask questions, point out concerns and require reconsideration, even once, gives the office real constitutional weight.
Way forward / key point

Balanced view: the President is neither a mere rubber stamp nor an active ruler. Normally he acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, but in crises (hung Parliament, unstable government, emergency) he can play an important role through limited discretion and moral authority to protect the Constitution.

Presidential Elections

Criticism of the election system

  • It is a highly complex system.
  • The value of an MLA’s vote differs from State to State.
  • It is neither true proportional representation nor a plain single transferable vote system. Calling it “proportional representation” is a constitutional misnomer because a single office cannot be divided proportionally, so the process is really an alternative vote and not a true PR-STV system.

2 · Ordinance-making Power (article 123) · Conditions · Limits · Cases · Trends · Examples#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 4-7. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Under Article 123 the President can issue an ordinance when Parliament is not in session and immediate action is needed. It has the force of an Act but is temporary: it must be laid before both Houses and lapses six weeks after Parliament reassembles unless replaced by an Act. Courts have said repeated re-promulgation is a fraud on the Constitution (D. C. Wadhwa) and that the power is open to judicial review.

WHAT IS THE Ordinance Power?

Meaning

  • The ordinance-making power is an exceptional feature of the Indian Constitution. Under Article 123, the President can promulgate an ordinance when either House or both Houses of Parliament are not in session (for example when the Lok Sabha is prorogued or dissolved while the Rajya Sabha is sitting, or vice versa).
  • Ordinary legislation needs the approval of both Houses. An ordinance lets the Executive take immediate legislative action when Parliament cannot make a law through the normal process.
  • Legal status: it has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament, but it is a temporary law.
  • Not an Emergency power: ordinances can be issued in peacetime, without declaring an Emergency under Part XVIII.
  • Global comparison: such power is unusual in major democracies. The UK and the USA have no equivalent presidential ordinance-making power.
  • Constituent Assembly view: Dr. B. R. Ambedkar defended it as a way to deal with “a situation which may suddenly and immediately arise” when existing laws are not enough and Parliament is not in session.

Constitutional Conditions AND Limitations

1. Parliament must not be in session

  • An ordinance can be issued only when either or both Houses are not in session. If both are in session, an ordinance cannot be promulgated.
  • It is an interim legislative power, not a parallel substitute for Parliament.

2. Immediate necessity must be satisfied

  • The President must be satisfied that circumstances require immediate action, and he acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74).
  • 38th Amendment (1975) made the President’s satisfaction final, conclusive and non-justiciable. 44th Amendment (1978) removed this protection, bringing back judicial review.
  • Judicial scrutiny today: courts can examine whether the satisfaction rests on relevant material or is tainted by mala fide intent or an improper purpose. They do not judge the wisdom of the policy or the adequacy of the material.

3. Legislative competence and subject matter

  • The ordinance power is co-extensive with Parliament’s ordinary legislative power, subject to constitutional limits.
  • Subject matter: generally the Union List and Concurrent List, plus matters on which Parliament can legislate under special provisions.
  • Effect: it can amend or repeal existing laws and operate retrospectively, subject to limits like Article 20(1).
  • Limits: it cannot violate Fundamental Rights or other constitutional restrictions. It cannot amend the Constitution, which needs the Article 368 procedure.

4. Parliamentary approval and duration

  • Mandatory laying: every ordinance must be placed before both Houses after they reassemble.
  • Duration: it ceases to operate six weeks after Parliament reassembles, unless replaced by an Act.
  • Earlier end: it ends sooner if both Houses pass resolutions disapproving it.
  • If the Houses reassemble on different dates, the six weeks are counted from the later date.
  • Maximum life: normally six months and six weeks, because the gap between two sessions can be at most six months (Article 85(1)).
  • Withdrawal: the President can withdraw it at any time on ministerial advice.
  • Procedural safeguard: under Rule 71(1) of the Lok Sabha Rules, a Bill replacing an ordinance must come with a statement explaining why immediate legislation was needed.
Memory aid · SINE-LAPSE

Ordinance checklist: Session off · Immediate need · No amendment of the Constitution / no FR violation · Effect like an Act · Laid before both Houses · Approval or lapse in 6 weeks · President may withdraw · Expiry max 6 months + 6 weeks. (own mnemonic)

Judicial Control AND Lapsed Ordinances

CaseWhat the Court held
R. C. Cooper v. Union (1970)The ordinance power is subject to constitutional limits. Ordinances can be challenged in court for violating Fundamental Rights or other constitutional provisions.
A. K. Roy v. Union (1982)Ordinance-making is a legislative power, subject to constitutional limits. An ordinance has the same force and effect as parliamentary legislation and is subject to judicial review, especially when it violates constitutional rights.
D. C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987)Between 1967 and 1981 Bihar re-promulgated 256 ordinances, keeping some alive for up to 14 years without legislative consideration. The Court held that repeated re-promulgation to bypass the legislature is a “fraud on the Constitution” and that the Executive cannot replace the regular legislative process with ordinances.
Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017, seven-judge bench)Bihar repeatedly issued ordinances to take over private Sanskrit schools. When they expired, the question was whether rights created under them continued. The Court held: re-promulgation without legislative scrutiny is impermissible; every ordinance must be laid before the legislature (failure is a serious violation); rights and benefits created under an ordinance do not automatically continue after it expires, although some effects may continue if reversing them is impossible, impractical or against compelling public interest; and courts can examine whether the power was used for a genuine purpose or misused.

History AND Trends

Evolution

  • Nehru era (1952-64): 66 ordinances during the first three Lok Sabhas.
  • 1978: demonetisation was carried out through the High Denomination Bank Notes (Demonetisation) Ordinance, 1978.
  • 1990s: coalition politics and legislative delays increased reliance on ordinances.

Critical Debates AND Issues

Constitutional principles at stake

  • Separation of powers: ordinances let the Executive temporarily exercise legislative power, which upsets the institutional balance.
  • Parliamentary accountability: they bypass debate, committee scrutiny and public consultation.
  • Fait accompli: the Executive can take irreversible decisions through an ordinance (asset transfers, restructuring) that Parliament may find hard to undo later.
  • Federal balance: Governors have a similar power under Article 213, which raises concerns over executive misuse and Centre-State relations.

The Seervai debate

  • H. M. Seervai’s defence: the ordinance power is essential because Indian courts have strong judicial review and can strike down an existing law immediately. In such a case an ordinance avoids an administrative or legal vacuum while Parliament is in recess.
  • Counter-argument:
    • The USA has equally strong judicial review without giving the President decree powers
    • It relies on emergency legislative sessions instead

Way forward: strengthening safeguards

  • Timely parliamentary scrutiny: a convention to convene Parliament within one or two weeks of promulgating a major ordinance, so debate happens early.
  • More assertive Presidential oversight: use the proviso to Article 74(1) to return questionable ordinance recommendations for Cabinet reconsideration.
  • Reversible actions: as far as possible, actions under an ordinance should be reversible if Parliament does not pass the replacing law, in line with Krishna Kumar Singh (2017).
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: ordinance-making is a necessary mechanism for urgent legislation but must remain the exception. Parliamentary scrutiny, judicial review and responsible executive conduct are needed to prevent misuse.

4 · Parliamentary System · Features · vs Presidential · Merits AND Limits · WHY India Chose IT · India vs Britain#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 10-12. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • India follows a parliamentary system at both Union (Articles 74 and 75) and State (Articles 163 and 164) level: the executive is accountable to the legislature, with a nominal head and a real head. It gives harmony, accountability and flexibility but can bring instability and cabinet dominance. India chose it because of historical familiarity and the wish for responsible government. It differs from Britain in being a republic with a written constitution and judicial review.

WHAT IS A Parliamentary System?

Meaning

  • The Indian Constitution sets up a parliamentary system at the Union level (Articles 74 and 75) and the State level (Articles 163 and 164).
  • Parliamentary system: the executive is politically accountable to the legislature. Examples: India, Britain, Japan, Canada.
  • Presidential system: the executive has a fixed tenure and does not depend on legislative confidence. Examples: USA, Brazil.
  • It is also called Cabinet Government, Responsible Government or the Westminster Model. Ivor Jennings called it a “cabinet system” because the Cabinet holds real executive authority.
  • Traditionally the PM was “primus inter pares” (first among equals). But the PM’s growing dominance led scholars like Crossman and Mackintosh to call it “prime ministerial government”.

KEY Features IN India

Eight features

  • 1. Nominal and real executive. The President is the nominal (de jure) executive and Head of State
  • The PM is the real (de facto) executive and Head of Government. Under Article 74 the President acts on aid and advice but may seek reconsideration once
  • 2. Majority party rule. The President appoints as PM a leader who can command majority support in the Lok Sabha. In a hung Parliament, a party or coalition that can secure a majority forms the government.
  • 3. Collective responsibility (Article 75(3)). The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. A successful no-confidence motion forces the entire Council to resign.
  • 4. Political cohesion. Ministers generally follow common policies and collective decisions. In coalitions, unity depends on cooperation and consensus.
  • 5. Dual membership. Ministers belong to both the executive and the legislature, a fusion of powers. Under Article 75(5) a non-MP minister must become a member of either House within six months.
  • 6. Leadership of the PM. The PM heads the Council, coordinates policy and is the central link between the President, Cabinet and Parliament.
  • 7. Dissolution of the lower House (Article 85). The President may dissolve the Lok Sabha, ordinarily on the advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the PM, allowing fresh elections before the term ends.
  • 8. Secrecy (Article 75(4)). Ministers take an oath of office and of secrecy, administered by the President. Cabinet discussions stay confidential, so there can be free debate and collective decisions.

Advantages (merits)

Five advantages

  • 1. Executive-legislative harmony: fusion promotes coordination and smoother lawmaking.
  • 2. Responsible government: Article 75(3) ensures accountability through Question Hour, debates, committees and no-confidence motions.
  • 3. Checks on arbitrary power: collective responsibility and parliamentary scrutiny help prevent misuse of executive authority.
  • 4. Alternative government: another party or coalition can form a government without fresh elections if it can win a majority. Jennings: the Leader of the Opposition serves as an “alternative Prime Minister”.
  • 5. Broad representation: the Council of Ministers can include different regions, communities and political groups, reflecting India’s diversity.

Limitations (demerits)

Six limitations

  • 1. Political instability: coalition disputes and loss of majority can cause frequent changes. Examples: the short-lived governments of Charan Singh, V. P. Singh, Chandra Shekhar and I. K. Gujral.
  • 2. Lack of policy continuity: a change of government may reverse policies. Example: the Janata Government (1977-79) reversed several policies of the earlier Congress government.
  • 3. Cabinet dictatorship: a strong majority lets the PM and Cabinet dominate Parliament and weakens scrutiny. H. J. Laski and Ramsay Muir warned of the “dictatorship of the cabinet”. Example: concentration of power during the Emergency (1975-77).
  • 4. Fusion of powers: close links weaken checks and balances. Walter Bagehot: “the cabinet is a hyphen that joins the buckle that binds the executive and legislative departments together.”
  • 5. Governance by non-experts: ministers are chosen mostly on political considerations, so technical expertise may be limited.
  • 6. Coalition compulsions: policy compromises, conflicting priorities and slower decisions.

WHY India Adopted THE Parliamentary System

Six reasons

  • 1. Historical familiarity:
    • India had experience with parliamentary institutions under British rule
    • The Government of India Acts of 1919 and 1935 gradually introduced responsible government, especially in the provinces
  • 2. Preference for responsible government: B. R. Ambedkar stressed balancing stability and responsibility. The Constituent Assembly preferred more executive accountability over the fixed tenure of a presidential system.
  • 3. Preventing deadlocks: the fusion of executive and legislature was expected to reduce conflict and ease lawmaking.
  • 4. Accommodating diversity:
    • The system allows regions, communities and parties to be represented in the executive
    • Coalitions can accommodate regional interests
  • 5. Flexibility: a government can be replaced without fresh elections if another leader gets a majority.
  • 6. Swaran Singh Committee (1976): recommended keeping the parliamentary system rather than shifting to a presidential form.

Way forward / key point

Conclusion: India’s parliamentary system combines executive accountability, political flexibility and representative governance. Its success depends on strong parliamentary scrutiny, collective responsibility and respect for constitutional institutions.

5 · Council OF Ministers · Cabinet · Committees · Cabinet Secretariat · PMO · Central Secretariat#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 13-18. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The Council of Ministers is the full ministry (Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State, Deputy Ministers), capped at 15% of the Lok Sabha by the 91st Amendment. The Cabinet is its small inner core that takes the real policy decisions. It is supported by Cabinet Committees, the Cabinet Secretariat (headed by the Cabinet Secretary), the PMO and the Central Secretariat. Each is useful but also raises issues like centralisation and delays.

Council OF Ministers: Composition AND SIZE

Composition

  • The Council of Ministers is a large body that includes all ministers of the Union Government. Its composition is decided by the Prime Minister, who considers seniority, political experience, regional balance and coalition needs.
  • The Constitution does not rank ministers, but three broad categories have grown by convention.

  • It introduced a ceiling: under Article 75(1A) the total number of ministers, including the PM, cannot exceed 15% of the Lok Sabha’s strength.

First ARC (1966) recommendations

  • The Union Cabinet should normally have 16 ministers including the PM.
  • The Council of Ministers should normally have 40 members and never exceed 45.
  • A Deputy Prime Minister could help with coordination and administrative reform.
  • A Cabinet Committee on Administration, with support in the Cabinet Secretariat, should help in reforms.

THE Cabinet

What it is

  • The Cabinet is the smaller and more powerful body inside the Council of Ministers and the principal policy-making and decision-making authority of the Union Government. Ramsay Muir called it the “steering wheel of the ship of the State”.
  • Constitutional status: the word “Cabinet” was formally put into the Constitution by the 44th Amendment, 1978. Under Article 352(3), a National Emergency cannot be proclaimed unless the written decision of the Union Cabinet is communicated to the President. The Cabinet consists of the PM and ministers of Cabinet rank as specified under Article 352.

Functions of the Cabinet

  • Policy formulation: the main policy-making body of the government.
  • Legislative management: decides the legislative agenda, major Bills and proposals about parliamentary sessions.
  • Financial functions: directs Budget preparation, expenditure and economic policy.
  • Administrative coordination: coordinates ministries and settles major policy differences.
  • Appointments and crisis management: oversees major appointments and takes key decisions in national emergencies and crises.

Cabinet dominance and checks on it

  • Because the Cabinet comes from Parliament yet strongly influences its agenda, it is said that “the creation dominates the creator”. A strong majority can lead to Cabinet dominance and weaker scrutiny.
  • Judicial checks: judicial review of executive actions.
  • Legislative checks: questions, debates, motions and committees.
  • Institutional checks: oversight by the Election Commission, CAG and other independent bodies.
  • Political checks: public opinion, media scrutiny, opposition parties and pressure within the party.

Kitchen Cabinet (Inner Cabinet)

  • A smaller, informal group of the PM and a few trusted colleagues. It discusses sensitive or urgent political matters before formal Cabinet meetings.
  • It brings speed and confidentiality, but may dilute the authority of the formal Cabinet.
  • Example: during Indira Gandhi’s leadership, advisers like P. N. Haksar and D. P. Dhar had considerable influence.

Cabinet Committees

What they are — small groups of ministers formed to deal with specific policy areas or administrative functions. They reduce the Cabinet’s workload by examining issues in detail before decisions are taken.

  • Extra-constitutional: not mentioned in the Constitution. They work under the Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961, framed under Article 77(3).
  • Formation: their number, names and composition are decided by the Prime Minister.
  • Membership:
    • Generally senior Cabinet Ministers
    • Ministers of State may be included or invited
  • Chair: the PM or a designated senior minister.
  • Types:
    • Standing committees work on a continuing basis
    • Ad hoc committees are formed for specific purposes

Functions and significance

  • Efficient decisions: smaller committees examine proposals and decide faster.
  • Specialisation: ministers with relevant experience go into details.
  • Inter-ministerial coordination: resolve disputes and prevent delays.
  • Collective responsibility: encourages consultation and limits arbitrary decisions by individual ministers.
  • PM leadership: helps the PM supervise important policy areas.

Issues with Cabinet Committees

  • Irregular meetings in some committees.
  • Limited participation: junior ministers can be included but may have little say.
  • Political considerations: membership can reflect political stature rather than expertise.
  • Centralisation: the PM chairing several key committees may reduce independent ministerial deliberation.
  • Weak Cabinet scrutiny: committee decisions may get limited discussion in the full Cabinet.

Cabinet Secretariat AND Cabinet Secretary

Cabinet Secretariat

  • The organisation created specifically to support the Cabinet, Cabinet Committees and the Prime Minister. It works directly under the PM and is the chief coordinating agency of the Government of India.
  • 1. Secretarial assistance:
    • Prepares and circulates agendas and background papers for Cabinet meetings
    • Records and circulates minutes
    • Monitors implementation of Cabinet decisions
  • 2. Implementing the Rules of Business: the Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961 and the (Allocation of Business) Rules, 1961.
  • 3. Inter-ministerial coordination:
    • Resolves differences between ministries
    • Promotes policy coordination and prevents duplication
    • Uses Committees of Secretaries for inter-ministerial issues
  • 4. Administrative monitoring: collects information on ministries’ major activities, monitors decisions and implementation, and coordinates responses in major crises.
  • 5. Policy support: promotes new policy initiatives and gives administrative support for coherent governance.

Cabinet Secretary

  • Heads the Cabinet Secretariat and is regarded as the senior-most civil servant in the Government of India.
  • Head of civil services: senior-most administrative coordinator and ex-officio Chairman of the Civil Services Board.
  • Adviser: gives high-level administrative advice to the PM and Cabinet.
  • Coordinator: resolves disputes among Secretaries.
  • Link: a buffer between the political executive and the civil services, protecting both from undue interference.
  • Leader: often called the “conscience keeper of the civil services” because his conduct influences the whole system.
  • Facts: N. R. Pillai was the first Cabinet Secretary of independent India. T. V. Somanathan is the current Cabinet Secretary as per the handout (verify). Extensions may be given in public interest under service rules, generally within a four-year ceiling, with a possible further extension of up to three months.

Prime Minister’s Office (PMO)

What it is — a non-constitutional administrative body that gives secretarial assistance to the Prime Minister.

  • Evolution:
    • Set up as the Prime Minister’s Secretariat (PMS) on 15 August 1947
    • Renamed the Prime Minister’s Office (PMO) on 28 March 1977. It has become a major centre of policy coordination and monitoring
  • Functions:
    • Policy advice (briefing notes, analysis, recommendations)
    • Coordination between ministries and an interface between PM and bureaucracy
    • Monitoring of major programmes and decisions
    • Direct responsibilities such as helping the PM in departments under his charge (Atomic Energy and Space) and NITI Aayog
    • Public relations, important correspondence and diplomatic engagements

Why a strong PMO is needed

  • Continuity of administrative support and advice to the PM.
  • Coordination of issues involving many ministries.
  • Policy monitoring of important decisions and implementation.
  • International engagement: helps coordinate communication with foreign governments and leaders.

Centralisation of power in the PMO

  • It has grown from a secretariat-style support body into a major coordinating centre of governance, especially under politically strong PMs. Reasons: strong electoral mandates demanding coordination
  • Need for a single point of inter-ministerial coordination
  • Leadership styles that prefer central control
  • Concerns:
    • (1) weak Cabinet deliberation, as major decisions may be taken in the PMO before they reach the Cabinet
    • (2) reduced ministerial autonomy, so ministers become implementers
    • (3) a parallel power structure of unelected officials with influence but no parliamentary accountability
    • (4) delays from too many approvals moving upwards
    • (5) weak federal consultation, States feeling sidelined
  • Way forward:
    • The PMO must work through the established administrative system and not bypass it
    • Strong coordination should strengthen, not weaken, collective Cabinet responsibility

Central Secretariat

What it is — the headquarters of Union ministries and departments, which assists the political executive in policy formulation, legislation, budgeting and administrative supervision.

  • Split system: the Government of India broadly follows a split between policy formulation (mainly the Secretariat and ministries) and policy implementation (attached offices, subordinate offices and field agencies). Example: the Health Ministry frames policy, while implementing agencies and field institutions carry out programmes.

Functions of the Central Secretariat

  • Policy formulation: gives ministers background information, facts, data and advice, and examines the legal and administrative impact of proposals.
  • Legislative functions:
    • Prepares draft Bills, rules and regulations
    • Helps ministers answer parliamentary questions and debates
  • Financial functions: prepares Budget proposals and monitors departmental spending.
  • Administrative supervision: general supervision over implementation, ordinarily without day-to-day field work.
  • Coordination: a channel between ministries, Union and State governments, and bodies like CAG and UPSC.
  • Institutional memory: keeps records of earlier decisions for continuity and consistency.

Issues and reforms

  • Issues:
    • Administrative delay (too many file layers)
    • Generalist bias (limited domain expertise)
    • Technology gaps
    • Overlapping functions
    • Weak coordination with State governments
  • Reforms:
    • The Second ARC recommended flatter structures, fewer levels and more delegation
    • Strengthen domain expertise, including lateral entry where suitable
    • Expand e-Office and digital file movement
    • Merge or reorganise ministries with overlapping functions
    • Mission Karmayogi to build the capacity and skills of Secretariat staff
  • The aim: a leaner, more specialised, digital and better-coordinated Secretariat that makes policymaking faster and citizen-focused.

6 · Constitution (130th Amendment) BILL, 2025 · Removal OF Ministers · Basic Structure Questions#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Executive and Legislature handout pp. 18-20. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The 130th Amendment Bill (introduced 20 August 2025, sent to a Joint Parliamentary Committee) would remove the PM, Union Ministers, CMs and State ministers who stay in custody for 30 consecutive days for offences punishable by 5 years or more. Supporters see it as closing a gap and stopping “governing from jail”. Critics say it clashes with presumption of innocence, federalism and parliamentary democracy. It has not become law.

THE BILL

Basics

  • The Constitution (One Hundred and Thirtieth Amendment) Bill, 2025 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 20 August 2025 by the Minister of Home Affairs and referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC).
  • It proposes the removal of the Prime Minister, Union Ministers, Chief Ministers and State/UT Ministers who remain in custody for 30 consecutive days in connection with serious criminal offences.
  • Status: the Bill remains under parliamentary examination and has not become law (current affairs; verify).

Key provisions

  • Threshold: applies to ministers accused of offences punishable with imprisonment that may extend to five years or more, who stay arrested and detained for 30 consecutive days.
  • Union and State ministers: the President (on the PM’s advice) or the Governor (on the CM’s advice) must remove the minister. Advice must be given by the 31st day of custody. Without advice the minister automatically ceases to hold office from the 32nd day.
  • PM and CMs:
    • Must resign by the 31st day of custody
    • If not, they automatically cease to hold office from the 32nd day
  • Reappointment: a removed minister, PM or CM may be reappointed after release from custody.
  • Companion legislation: similar provisions are proposed for Puducherry and Jammu and Kashmir through separate amendment Bills. Delhi is covered by the proposed amendment to Article 239AA.

Rationale

Why the Bill is proposed

  • Closing a constitutional gap: the Constitution has no separate general criminal disqualification standard for ministers beyond existing constitutional and legislative rules.
  • Criminalisation of politics: under Section 8 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, criminal disqualification generally follows conviction, which can take years.
  • Administrative continuity: to stop ministers from continuing in executive office during long custody, addressing the concern of “governing from jail”.

Constitutional Challenges: Basic Structure

The Bill raises concerns under the Basic Structure doctrine (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973) because removal can happen before conviction or even before charges are framed.

  • 1. Parliamentary democracy. In P. V. Narasimha Rao v. State (1998) the Supreme Court recognised parliamentary democracy as part of the basic structure, and under Article 75(3) the Council of Ministers is responsible to the Lok Sabha. In Manoj Narula v. Union of India (2014) the Court recognised the constitutional prerogative of the PM and CM to select ministers, which automatic removal may restrict. Concern: investigative action could end the tenure of elected governments without a no-confidence vote.
  • 2. Federal balance. Under S. R. Bommai (1994) federalism is part of the basic structure. Central agencies such as the ED, CBI or NIA arresting a State CM could trigger removal, raising fears of Union interference. State agencies acting against Union ministers or another State’s CM could cause inter-governmental confrontation.
  • 3. Rule of law and presumption of innocence. Under Section 35 of the BNSS, 2023, arrests can be made on grounds including reasonable suspicion, subject to safeguards
  • Arrest does not prove guilt. Section 187 BNSS provides default bail after 60 or 90 days, so 30 days of custody can pass before charges are framed. Strict bail conditions in PMLA Section 45 and UAPA Section 43D(5) apply in specified cases. Concern: removal before a judicial finding on the merits raises questions of arbitrariness, due process and presumption of innocence under Articles 14 and 21
