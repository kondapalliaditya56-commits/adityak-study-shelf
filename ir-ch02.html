1 · Understanding major powers#

Class + handout

“International politics, like all politics, is a struggle for power.” (Hans J. Morgenthau, quoted in the handout.)

A major power is a state with significant economic, technological and military capabilities and the strategic intent to shape the international order. Unlike regional powers, major powers influence multiple regions and global institutions through deterrence, agenda-setting and economic interdependence. The USA, China and Russia qualify because they combine strong military, economic and technological capabilities with the intent to shape the global order.

How other states respond to a major power (slide 2)

Major-power rivalries and alliances shape the global balance of power, which decides whether other states balance, bandwagon or pursue strategic autonomy.

Key concept: strategic entente

An informal, cooperative diplomatic understanding between nations. They agree to consult or align their policies for mutual benefit or to manage a common rival, without necessarily being a formal alliance. (Handout p. 4.)

Class + handout

The US-China rivalry is the main strategic competition in the current world order. The handout reads it as a power transition leading to the Thucydides Trap.

Two key concepts (handout p. 5)
ConceptMeaning

Thucydides TrapThe risk of conflict when a rising power threatens the position of an established power

6 · Russia-West confrontation#

Class + handout

“The supreme art of war is to subdue the enemy without fighting.” (Sun Tzu, quoted in the handout.) The Russia-West confrontation is a major fault line in the current world order. It intensified after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

Mains 2026 (15m, 250w): BRI and South Asia as a theatre of great-power competition

Intro: the BRI (2013) uses ports, railways and corridors (CPEC, Gwadar) to deepen China’s regional centrality, and South Asia has become a space where China, India and the US compete.

  • Security: CPEC through PoK is a sovereignty objection; China-Pakistan defence cooperation complicates two-front planning; Gwadar adds a potential maritime dimension (do not call it a naval base).
  • Indian Ocean: String of Pearls (Gwadar, Hambantota, Kyaukpyu), research vessels, naval reach; India’s response: maritime domain awareness, neighbourhood partnerships, SAGAR/MAHASAGAR, Quad.
  • Regional influence: Chinese infrastructure money competes with Indian connectivity; India must offer faster, reliable projects (way forward).
  • Critical view: “theatre of competition” is partly true, but South Asia states also hedge; India is more than a reactor (Quad, Act East, partnerships).

Closing: BRI raises the stakes for India’s security and influence; the answer is delivery, partnerships and deterrence, not rejection alone.

Mains 2024 (10m, 150w): the West fostering India as an alternative to China

Intro: US-China rivalry and Russia-West confrontation have pushed the West to look for trusted partners and diversified supply chains.

  • Supply chains: China+1 and friend-shoring open opportunities for India in electronics, semiconductors, pharmaceuticals; the EU lists semiconductors, pharma, medical devices, critical minerals; Australia, France, Japan on minerals and manufacturing.
  • Strategic ally: Quad and Malabar, India-US defence framework and iCET/TRUST, India-France co-production, Japan’s JPY 10 tn target.
  • Limits: dependence on Chinese inputs (rare-earth magnets 90.4%), Russia ties and strategic autonomy, trade frictions (tariffs, CBAM), execution gaps (Japan).

Closing: the West sees India as a partner, not a proxy; India accepts it on its own terms.

Mains 2023 (15m, 250w): NATO expansion and a stronger US-Europe partnership: good for India?

Intro: the Ukraine war expanded NATO (Finland 2023, Sweden 2024) and tightened US-Europe coordination. For India the effect is mixed.

  • Gains: West looks to India for supply chains and technology; EU FTA, TTC; France and US defence co-production; India gets scope for diversification away from Russia.
  • Costs: sanctions on Russia hit India’s defence spares and energy; Graham Act 2026; India-Russia-China triangle can reduce India’s space in Eurasia; pressure on strategic autonomy.
  • India’s response: dialogue and diplomacy on Ukraine, no automatic alignment, multi-alignment, defence diversification and indigenisation.

Closing: only partly: it widens India’s options but also raises costs on Russia ties; autonomy decides the net effect.

Mains 2021 (15m, 250w): AUKUS and existing partnerships (thin notes: AUKUS is only named)

Intro: AUKUS is listed among US minilateral groupings that strengthen regional deterrence. (The notes give no more detail: add AUKUS facts after class before using this in the exam.)

  • Context from the notes: the US builds flexible coalitions (NATO, Quad, I2U2, AUKUS) to prevent China’s regional dominance; alliances with Japan, South Korea, Australia and the Philippines.
  • Existing partnerships: Quad and Malabar for maritime security and technology; India-Australia cooperation (Malabar, AUSINDEX, PACTS); minilateralism supplements, not replaces, existing groupings (Chapter 1).
  • Verdict frame: AUKUS adds a layer; it does not supersede the Quad or ASEAN-centred mechanisms.

Closing: another coalition layer in a multipolar churn, not a replacement.

Mains 2020 (15m, 250w): Indo-US over Indo-Russian defence deals and the Indo-Pacific

Intro: defence ties with the US are moving from arms purchases to interoperability, co-production and technology; Russia remains a legacy and maintenance pillar.

  • US: LEMOA 2016, COMCASA, BECA 2020, 2+2, 2025 ten-year framework, defence trade about $25 bn by end-2023; Quad and Malabar for Indo-Pacific stability.
  • Russia: Su-30MKI, T-90, S-400, AK-203, INS Vikramaditya; MRO; shift to licensed production; but S-400 delays, spares, payment and sanctions risks.
  • Indo-Pacific stability: US partnership supports freedom of navigation and a balance against China; Russian systems are continental in focus (the handout).
  • India’s approach: diversify suppliers; CAATSA objection; no forced abandonment of Russia.

Closing: significance of US deals lies in interoperability and technology; Russia stays a legacy pillar; India diversifies.

Mains 2020 (15m, 250w): Quad, from military alliance to trade bloc?

Intro: the Quad is a minilateral grouping for a free, open and rules-based Indo-Pacific, not a treaty alliance.

  • Test the claim: the notes link the Quad with Malabar, maritime security, resilient supply chains and critical technologies. No trade bloc features are named.
  • India avoids “a formal anti-China military bloc”, “treaty alliance” and “automatic war commitments” (India seeks v avoids).
  • Wider footing: IPEF and friend-shoring (US), SCRI (India, Japan, Australia), PACTS and the Critical Minerals Corridor (Australia).

Closing: the Quad is a multi-issue minilateral: security plus supply chains and technology; “trade bloc” overstates the shift.

Mains 2019 (15m, 250w): why friction between India and the US

Intro: ties moved from Cold War mistrust (1971) to strategic partnership, but a “partnership, not alliance” leaves gaps in expectations.

  • The statement’s core: the US expects closer alignment; India wants issue-based cooperation, autonomy and recognition of its ambitions.
  • Examples: Russia (S-400, CAATSA, oil); tariffs and GSP removal (2019); Pakistan (re-hyphenation, mediation); H-1B and student safety; defence technology limits; values and sovereignty.
  • Counter-evidence: Nuclear Agreement 2005-08, LEMOA/BECA, iCET, Quad.

Closing: friction comes from different expectations of India’s role, but converging interests keep the partnership growing.

Mains 2019 (10m, 150w): India and Japan, a global and strategic partnership

Closing: significance for Asia and the world is real; the test is turning convergence into projects.
