1 · Local Self-government · Significance · Evolution OF Panchayats#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Local Self-Government module pp. 3-6. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Local self-government is the oldest and the closest form of government, yet the name is a misnomer: self-government exists at higher levels too, and local bodies do not hold full authority over their area. It matters because it builds grassroots democracy, trains leaders, is economical and brings real participation. Its evolution runs from Lord Ripon (1882) through the Community Development Programme (1952), the Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957) and a series of later committees, until the 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) gave it constitutional status.

WHAT Local Self-government IS

Oldest and closest form of government

  • Oldest form: when human settlements began, whether small villages or rudimentary towns, communities were managed by local institutions. National and State Governments came much later with the emergence of the modern nation-state.
  • Closest form: it is the level of government closest to the people. Its activities and decisions can be observed directly by the people, unlike those of the Union and State Governments.

Why "Local Self-Government" is a misnomer

  • 1. Self-Government: self-government exists at higher levels as well, since Union and State Governments are also elected. The term originated during the British period when limited self-government was introduced mainly at the local level, especially in municipalities.
  • 2. Government: the word "government" is also a misnomer because local bodies do not possess comprehensive authority over all subjects within their territorial jurisdiction.
  • At the State level the government is a composite entity responsible for law and order, public order and various developmental activities.
  • At the district level administration is fragmented. The District Magistrate/Collector heads the district administration, while Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) and Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) perform specified developmental and civic functions.
  • There is no single comprehensive district government. Various institutions perform overlapping functions, coordinated and controlled to varying degrees by the district administration.
  • Thus local bodies are not all-powerful institutions within their territorial areas. Their powers and responsibilities are divided among different administrative authorities.

Local Government can be defined as

  • Administration of a small locality by a body representing local inhabitants.
  • Enjoying a fairly large degree of autonomy.
  • Raising at least a part of its revenue locally.
  • Spending it on local services, such as sewage, sanitation, water supply, roads, bridges, street lighting and maintenance of the local area.

Significance OF Local Self-government

1. Grassroot Democracy

  • According to Jawaharlal Nehru, democracy at the top cannot succeed unless it is built on a strong foundation from below.
  • Local bodies help people develop political consciousness.
  • They provide the foundation for the entire superstructure of democracy.

2. School of Democracy

  • Local Self-Government is the best school of democracy.
  • Regular elections, debates, dissent and voting behaviour help people learn the practice of democracy.
  • It is also a training ground for future leaders, who learn to articulate and represent democratic ideas at the local level.

3. More Capable Level of Government

  • Local bodies are more capable of addressing certain local problems because of:
  • Local Interest: people directly experience local problems, such as potholes, water shortages and poor sanitation, and have an interest in resolving them.
  • Local Knowledge: people living in an area possess better knowledge of its problems, resources and requirements.

4. More Economical

  • Local Self-Government can be a more economical form of government.
  • Local expenditure takes place under the direct observation of inhabitants.
  • Greater public vigilance can ensure better utilisation of resources and reduce unnecessary expenditure.

5. Reduces the Burden on Higher Governments

  • Effective local bodies reduce the burden on Union and State Governments.
  • Local institutions can deal with smaller and local issues.
  • Higher governments can focus on broader policies and larger developmental concerns.
  • However, this requires effective devolution of powers, responsibilities, authority and funds.

6. Two-Way Channel of Communication

  • Local bodies act as a two-way channel of communication between the people and higher levels of government.
  • Bottom-Up: local representatives communicate the problems and demands of the community to higher authorities.
  • Top-Down: government schemes and programmes are implemented and monitored through local institutions.

7. Vital for National Progress

  • Development requires recognition and utilisation of locally available resources.
  • Village Panchayats and local bodies are better placed to identify human and material resources.
  • They help in identifying and utilising locally available talent and development opportunities.

8. Indispensable for Decentralised Planning and Development

  • Local problems and priorities can be identified more accurately.
  • Development programmes can be prepared according to the actual needs of the community.

9. Real Participation of People

  • Real participation of people is possible most effectively at the local level.
  • Local institutions allow citizens to participate directly in public affairs.
  • The Gram Sabha provides a platform for direct democracy at the village level.
  • Urban neighbourhood-level institutions can similarly strengthen citizen participation.
Way forward / key point

Significance: Local Self-Government brings democracy closer to the people and makes governance more participatory and accountable.

Evolution OF THE Panchayats

Lord Ripon: Resolution of 1882

  • Lord Ripon was interested in the democratisation of local bodies and tried to make them effective institutions of self-government.
  • His Resolution of 1882 is referred to as the Magna Carta of Local Self-Government.
  • He emphasised greater participation of elected non-official members in local bodies.
  • He is regarded as the Father of Local Self-Government in India.

Original Constitution on Local Self-Government

  • The original Constitution contained two important provisions concerning local government:
  • 1. Article 40 (DPSP): directs the State to organise village Panchayats and endow them with necessary powers and authority to function as units of self-government.
  • 2. Entry 5, State List: local government is a State subject.
  • Significance: the original Constitution gave constitutional importance but not constitutional status to local bodies. Constitutional status came later through the 73rd and 74th Amendments, 1992.

Community Development Programme (CDP), 1952

  • The Government of India launched the CDP in 1952 for comprehensive rural development and reconstruction, with a focus on agriculture and overall development.
  • The programme drew on community development and extension-service approaches and received support from the Ford Foundation.
  • Under CDP, Blocks emerged as territorial units of development, while districts, subdivisions and tehsils continued as field units of revenue administration.
  • The programme was based on people's participation rather than bureaucracy alone.

Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957)

  • The National Development Council (NDC) appointed the Committee in 1957 to evaluate the working of CDP and the National Extension Service.
  • It found that CDP was only a partial success because of inadequate people's participation and the absence of an institutional framework at the grassroots level.
  • 1. Three-Tier Panchayati Raj System:
    • Gram Panchayat (village level)
    • Panchayat Samiti (block level, the principal executive tier)
    • Zila Parishad (district level, with a coordinating and supervisory role)
  • 2. Vertical Representation: Gram Panchayats should have directly elected representatives, while higher tiers would be constituted mainly through indirect representation.
  • 3. Regular Elections: elections should be held every five years.
  • 4. Representation of Legislators: MPs, MLAs and MLCs should be associated with Zila Parishads.
  • 5. District Collector: the Committee envisaged an important coordinating role for the Collector in the district-level structure.
  • 6. Political Parties: favoured keeping political parties outside Panchayat elections.
  • 7. Genuine Devolution: adequate powers, finances and taxation powers should be transferred to local bodies.
  • Implementation: the recommendations were accepted in 1958, and democratic decentralisation began in 1959. Rajasthan became the first State to introduce Panchayati Raj at Nagaur on 2 October 1959. Subsequently, different States adopted different structures, including two-tier, three-tier and four-tier arrangements.

Ashok Mehta Committee (1977)

  • In 1977 the Janata Government appointed a committee on PRIs under the chairmanship of Ashok Mehta. It submitted its report in 1978.
  • 1. Two-Tier System: Zila Parishad (district level, the principal executive body) and Mandal Panchayat (a group of villages with a population of 15,000-20,000).
  • 2. Political Parties: should be allowed to contest Panchayat elections, in contrast to the Balwant Rai Mehta Committee.
  • 3. Regular Elections: premature dissolution should be followed by elections within six months.
  • 4. Reservation: seats should be reserved for SCs and STs.
  • 5. Separate Ministry: a separate Ministry of Panchayati Raj should be established in State Governments.

G. V. K. Rao Committee (1985)

  • Also known as the Committee to Review the Existing Administrative Arrangements for Rural Development and Poverty Alleviation Programmes (CAARD).
  • 1. Zila Parishad: should be the principal body to manage rural development programmes at the district level.
  • 2. District Development Commissioner (DDC): a senior official should function as the Chief Executive Officer of the Zila Parishad.
  • Significance: sought to make PRIs central to rural development administration rather than bypassing them through bureaucratic agencies.

L. M. Singhvi Committee (1986)

  • 1. Constitutional Status: PRIs should be given constitutional status.
  • 2. Nyaya Panchayats: separate Nyaya Panchayats should be established for a cluster of villages.
  • 3. Panchayati Raj Judicial Tribunals: should be established in States to adjudicate disputes relating to Panchayat elections, dissolution and functioning.

Thungon Committee (1988)

  • Constitutional recognition of PRIs.
  • Fixed five-year tenure and regular elections.
  • Zila Parishad as an important institution for district planning.

Gadgil Committee (1988)

  • Constitutional status for PRIs.
  • Regular elections and a stronger democratic structure.
  • Adequate taxation and financial powers.
  • Establishment of State Finance Commissions and State Election Commissions.

Constitutionalisation

  • The Rajiv Gandhi Government introduced the 64th Constitutional Amendment Bill in July 1989 to give constitutional status to PRIs and make them more powerful and broad-based.
  • The Bill was passed by the Lok Sabha in August 1989 but was not approved by the Rajya Sabha.
  • The Opposition argued that it could strengthen centralisation in the federal system.
  • Eventually, after modifications and consultations with States, the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, 1992 were passed.

This phase is often referred to as the New Magna Carta Phase of Local Self-Government. Part IX contains three types of provisions: (1) Mandatory Provisions; (2) Mandatory Provisions with Flexibility in Implementation; (3) Optional Provisions.

2 · PART IX OF THE Constitution · Eleventh Schedule · PESA ACT, 1996#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Local Self-Government module pp. 6-9. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The 73rd Amendment added Part IX (Articles 243 to 243-O) and the Eleventh Schedule with 29 subjects. Its provisions are mandatory, mandatory with flexibility, or optional. The structure is largely mandatory, but real functional and financial empowerment still depends on State governments. Article 243M excludes Fifth and Sixth Schedule areas and a few others, and PESA 1996 extended Part IX to Fifth Schedule areas with a strong role for the Gram Sabha.

73RD Amendment ACT, 1992: PART IX

What it did

  • The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs).
  • Added Part IX (Articles 243 to 243-O) to the Constitution.
  • Added the Eleventh Schedule, containing 29 subjects.
  • Came into force on 24 April 1993.

Mandatory AND Optional Provisions

1. Mandatory Provisions

  • Establishment of Gram Sabha.
  • Three-tier Panchayati Raj System, with an exception for States having a population not exceeding 20 lakh.
  • Direct elections to all seats in Panchayats.
  • Reservation for SCs, STs and women.
  • Five-year tenure and timely elections.
  • Establishment of State Election Commission (SEC).
  • Constitution of State Finance Commission (SFC) every five years.

2. Mandatory with Flexibility in Implementation

  • Composition of Panchayats: States determine the detailed composition, subject to constitutional requirements.
  • Election of Chairpersons: States decide the manner of election at the village level. Chairpersons at intermediate and district levels are elected indirectly.
  • Reservation: States determine the manner of allotment and rotation within the constitutional framework.
  • Accounts and Audits: States determine the detailed procedures through legislation.

3. Optional (Voluntary) Provisions

  • Specific powers and functions of Gram Sabha.
  • Reservation for Backward Classes.
  • Representation of MPs, MLAs and other specified members in higher-tier Panchayats.
  • Devolution of Funds, Functions and Functionaries.
  • Authority to levy and collect taxes, duties, tolls and fees.
Value addition

Key Issue: the constitutional structure of Panchayats is largely mandatory, but actual functional and financial empowerment remains substantially dependent on State Governments.

Important Articles AND Exceptions

Article 243B: Three-Tier Panchayati Raj System

  • Village level: Gram Panchayat. Intermediate/Block level: Panchayat Samiti. District level: Zila Parishad.
  • Exception: States having a population not exceeding 20 lakh may dispense with the intermediate tier.

Article 243C: Composition of Panchayats

  • All seats at the three levels are filled through direct elections.
  • The Gram Panchayat Chairperson is elected in the manner prescribed by State law.
  • Chairpersons at the intermediate and district levels are elected indirectly by and from the elected members.
  • States may provide representation for MPs, MLAs and other specified representatives in higher-tier Panchayats.

Article 243D: Reservation of Seats

  • SCs and STs: reservation in proportion to their population in the Panchayat area.
  • Women: at least one-third of directly elected seats, including SC/ST women.
  • Chairpersons: reservation for SCs, STs and at least one-third for women at each level.
  • Backward Classes: reservation may be provided by State law.
  • Rotation: reserved seats and Chairperson offices are allotted according to the constitutional framework.
  • Important: many States have increased women's reservation to 50%, beyond the constitutional minimum.

Article 243E: Duration of Panchayats

  • Every Panchayat has a five-year tenure from its first meeting.
  • Elections must be completed before the expiry of its tenure.
  • In case of premature dissolution, elections must ordinarily be held within six months.
  • If the remaining original tenure is less than six months, a fresh election for that short period is not necessary.
  • A Panchayat reconstituted after premature dissolution serves only the remaining original tenure.

Article 243K: State Election Commission

  • The State Election Commissioner is appointed by the Governor.
  • Cannot be removed except in the same manner and on the same grounds as a High Court Judge.
  • Service conditions cannot be changed to the Commissioner's disadvantage after appointment.
  • The Governor must provide necessary staff when requested by the SEC.
  • Significance: provides constitutional protection for regular and independent local body elections.

Article 243M: Exceptions to Part IX

  • Part IX does not ordinarily apply to:
    • Fifth and Sixth Schedule Areas, subject to the relevant constitutional provisions
    • Nagaland, Meghalaya and Mizoram
    • Specified hill areas of Manipur
    • Specified provisions relating to the Darjeeling hill areas
  • Arunachal Pradesh: the constitutional requirement of SC reservation under Article 243D does not apply.
  • Important: Parliament may extend Part IX to Scheduled and tribal areas with suitable modifications under Article 243M(4)(b).

Eleventh Schedule: 29 Subjects

Important subjects

  • Agriculture and Land Improvement.
  • Minor Irrigation and Water Management.
  • Animal Husbandry, Fisheries and Social Forestry.
  • Rural Housing and Drinking Water.
  • Roads, Bridges and Rural Electrification.
  • Education, Health and Sanitation.
  • Women and Child Development.
  • Poverty Alleviation Programmes.
  • Public Distribution System.
  • Maintenance of Community Assets.
Value addition

Important: inclusion of a subject in the Eleventh Schedule does not automatically transfer that function to Panchayats. Article 243G leaves devolution substantially to State legislation.

PESA ACT, 1996

Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996

  • PESA extended Part IX, with modifications, to Fifth Schedule Areas.
  • Based on the recommendations of the Dilip Singh Bhuria Committee (1995).
  • Enacted under Article 243M(4)(b).
  • Recognises tribal customs, traditional institutions and community resource management.
  • Gives an important role to the Gram Sabha in tribal self-government.

Major provisions

  • 1. Village Definition: recognises a habitation, hamlet or group of hamlets managing affairs according to traditions and customs.
  • 2. Gram Sabha Powers: safeguards traditions, customs, cultural identity, community resources and customary dispute resolution.
  • 3. Development Planning: Gram Sabha approves village-level development plans and identifies beneficiaries.
  • 4. Natural Resources: provides for ownership of minor forest produce, management of village markets and minor water bodies through the applicable institutional framework.
  • 5. Tribal Protection: safeguards against land alienation, exploitative moneylending and unregulated intoxicant sales.
  • 6. Land Acquisition: requires consultation with the Gram Sabha or Panchayat at the appropriate level before land acquisition and rehabilitation.
  • 7. Minor Minerals: requires prior recommendation of the Gram Sabha or appropriate Panchayat for specified mining leases and concessions involving minor minerals.

Implementation and problems

  • PESA applies to notified Fifth Schedule Areas in 10 States. According to the Ministry of Panchayati Raj (April 2026): nine States had notified PESA Rules
  • Odisha had not yet notified its final PESA Rules. (verify)
  • Major problems:
    • Inadequate implementation of Gram Sabha powers
    • Weak control over natural resources
    • Delays in aligning State laws with PESA
    • Bureaucratic dominance and limited awareness among tribal communities
Way forward / key point

Significance: PESA seeks to protect tribal self-government while integrating Scheduled Areas into the constitutional framework of democratic decentralisation.

3 · Significance OF THE 73RD Amendment ACT · GRAM Sabha#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Local Self-Government module pp. 9-10. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The 73rd Amendment gave local bodies new respect, regular elections, their own finance and election commissions, a separate functional domain and reservation that widened the social base of politics. The Gram Sabha, made up of all registered voters of a village, is the platform for direct democracy, but it suffers from size, irregular meetings, caste-class-gender divides and weak participation.

Significance OF THE 73RD Constitutional Amendment ACT

1. New Respectability

  • The Amendment brought new respectability to local bodies by giving them constitutional status. It is often referred to as the New Magna Carta of Local Self-Government.

2. Regular Elections

  • Elections are held at intervals of five years, as it is now a constitutional requirement. Supersession of Panchayats (premature termination) has become difficult.

3. Separate Finance Commission and State Election Commission

  • State Finance Commission (SFC): reviews the financial position of local bodies and recommends the devolution of resources.
  • State Election Commission (SEC): conducts local body elections, which were earlier controlled by State Governments.

4. Separate Functional Domain

  • The Constitution recognises a separate functional domain for local bodies through the Eleventh Schedule (29 subjects) and Twelfth Schedule (18 subjects).

5. Provision for Weaker Sections

  • The Amendment expanded the social base of politics in India by providing reservation for SCs, STs and women. This has been described as the Rise of the Newly Enfranchised and a Silent Revolution.

6. India's Claim to Be the Biggest Democracy Acquired Substance

  • The scale of democratic representation increased considerably with the addition of millions of elected representatives at the local level.
  • According to the Ministry of Panchayati Raj (2026), women constitute approximately 49.75% of elected Panchayat representatives. (verify)
  • This has strengthened grassroots democracy and political participation.

GRAM Sabha Functions

What the Gram Sabha is

  • The Gram Sabha is a constitutional body consisting of persons registered in the electoral rolls of a village.
  • Its powers and responsibilities vary across States, as Article 243A allows State Legislatures to determine its functions.

Common functions

  • 1. Annual Budget: considers the annual budget of the Gram Panchayat.
  • 2. Annual Audit Report: examines the Panchayat's audit report.
  • 3. Annual Administration Report: reviews the activities and performance of the Panchayat.
  • 4. Social Audit: performs social audits of government schemes and programmes.
  • 5. Identification of Beneficiaries: identifies eligible beneficiaries under welfare schemes in States where such powers are provided.
  • 6. Selection of Schemes: participates in selecting development schemes and fixing local priorities.
Value addition

Important: these functions depend on the applicable State laws and programme guidelines. They are not uniformly binding powers of Gram Sabhas across India.

Areas Where GRAM Sabha CAN BE Effective

Six areas

  • 1. Social Audit: performs the social audit of government schemes to ascertain whether the schemes have delivered the intended benefits to the people for whom they were launched.
  • 2. Creating Awareness about Government Programmes: schemes and programmes can be made known to eligible beneficiaries through the Gram Sabha.
  • 3. Identification of Beneficiaries: eligible beneficiaries can be identified with the help of the Gram Sabha, using its local knowledge.
  • 4. Improving Transparency and Accountability: regular meetings and participation of members increase transparency and accountability.
  • 5. Fixation of Development Priorities: the Gram Sabha can have a say in development priorities by taking the opinions of its members.
  • 6. Strengthening Direct Democracy: if Gram Sabhas function effectively, they strengthen direct democracy at the grassroots level.

Problems IN THE Working OF GRAM Sabha

Six problems

  • 1. Large and Unwieldy Size: some Gram Sabhas have populations of almost 10,000 people, making it difficult to conduct meetings effectively.
  • 2. Lack of Appreciation of the Nature of the Body: villagers may lack adequate knowledge about its powers and functions. Meeting procedures and agendas are often not properly followed, leading to disorder and chaos.
  • 3. Prolonged Meetings: meetings are sometimes prolonged and endless, discouraging public participation.
  • 4. Irregular Meetings: although State Panchayat Acts prescribe the periodicity of meetings, they are often irregular. In some cases, fictitious meetings are recorded on paper without actually taking place.
  • 5. Poor Participation of Women: generally low, although it has improved in several areas where Panchayats are headed by women.
  • 6. Class, Caste and Gender Divides: meetings are often characterised by class, caste and gender divisions, which restrict the effective participation of marginalised communities.

Suggestions

Five suggestions

  • 1. Involve Credible NGOs: credible NGOs can assist in organising and managing Gram Sabha meetings, ensuring that meetings are held effectively and regularly.
  • 2. Mandatory Provisions for Holding Meetings: although State Panchayat Acts prescribe meeting requirements, an effective mechanism is needed to ensure implementation and prevent fictitious meetings.
  • 3. Information Flow from Gram Panchayat to Gram Sabha: proper management of information flow, ensuring that budgets, expenditure and development activities are not hidden.
  • 4. Redesign the Gram Sabha-Gram Panchayat Relationship: along the lines of the Cabinet-Parliament relationship, with the Gram Sabha exercising greater scrutiny over the elected Panchayat.
  • 5. Strengthen Ward Sabhas: smaller Ward Sabhas can improve participation and make discussions more manageable, particularly where Gram Sabhas are large and unwieldy.

4 · State Control OVER Panchayats · Problems OF PRIs · Panchayat Devolution Index · Women · Developmental Impact#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Local Self-Government module pp. 11-15. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • States control PRIs for good reasons (they create and fund them, and must prevent a parish-pump attitude) but often use a sledgehammer where a spur and rein are needed. PRIs face political and bureaucratic resistance, a hostile social landscape, restrictive qualifications, corruption, parallel bodies, criminality, poor devolution of the 3Fs and weak capacity. The Panchayat Devolution Index 2024 shows wide State differences. Women have gained voice but face proxy rule; political development has been better realised than socio-economic development.

NEED FOR AND Means OF State Control

Need for State control over PRIs

  • 1. Legal Creation of the State: PRIs are legally constituted, funded and empowered by State Governments. Hence, States must ensure that PRIs function properly.
  • 2. Balanced Regional Development: States must ensure balanced development and prevent a Parish Pump Attitude, where Panchayats focus only on their own needs without considering neighbouring villages and areas.
  • 3. Administrative and Technical Assistance: State Governments provide funds, administrative support and technical assistance to PRIs. Hence, reasonable supervision is necessary.

Means of State control

  • "Where the spur and rein are required, the State Government uses a sledgehammer." Instead of limited encouragement and reasonable supervision, State Governments often exercise excessive and intrusive control over PRIs.
  • 1. Appointment of Key Officials:
    • Critical staff of local bodies are appointed and controlled by State Governments
    • Through these employees, States can influence the functioning of PRIs
  • 2. Superseding Power: States can dissolve Panchayats prematurely under provisions of their respective Panchayat Acts, subject to constitutional safeguards.
  • 3. Suspension and Removal of Members: States may suspend or remove elected members under specified circumstances. The L. M. Singhvi Committee recommended Panchayati Raj Judicial Tribunals to adjudicate such disputes.
  • 4. Suspension of Resolutions: States may suspend or annul resolutions passed by PRIs under the applicable State laws.
  • 5. Vertical Budgetary Approval: in some States, Panchayat budgets require approval from higher authorities, reducing financial autonomy and delaying expenditure.
  • 6. Binding Directions: States may issue binding directions to local bodies under their respective laws.
  • 7. Audit and Enquiries: State authorities can audit PRI accounts and order enquiries into their functioning.
  • 8. Devolution of Powers: State Governments determine the extent of powers and responsibilities devolved to local bodies.
  • 9. Tied Grants: States provide grants that specify where the money must be spent, restricting local financial autonomy.

Evaluation OF THE Working OF PRIs

Why a pan-India judgement is difficult

  • 1. Different Levels of Empowerment: powers, duties, responsibilities and finances of PRIs are determined by respective State Panchayat Acts, so variation across States is considerable.
  • 2. Not Completely Autonomous: functional and financial powers largely depend on State legislation.
  • 3. Multiple Objectives: Political Development (deepening political consciousness and democratisation) and Socioeconomic Development (education, health, employment and other sectors).

Problems OF PRIs

1. Lack of Adequate Politico-Bureaucratic Will or Vision

  • The 73rd Amendment led to the rise of a new power structure at the grassroots level.
  • Political resistance: State-level politicians may view the rise of local leaders as a threat to their authority.
  • Bureaucratic resistance: more powers to local bodies mean fewer decision-making powers with the bureaucratic apparatus.
  • Significance: if this problem is addressed, many other obstacles to decentralisation can be reduced.

2. Social Landscape

  • The social landscape of rural India is not always conducive to the effective growth of local bodies.
  • Patriarchy: restricts women's independent participation and leadership.
  • Feudalism: landowners and economically powerful groups may dominate local institutions.
  • Casteism: caste hierarchies restrict the effective empowerment of backward classes, particularly Dalits.
  • Caste-related problems:
    • Rotational Policy (frequent rotation of reserved seats prevents representatives from developing lasting ties)
    • Elite Capture (better-off and influential sections secure a disproportionate share of benefits)
    • Proxy Candidates (the practice of Sarpanch Pati weakens genuine women's representation)
    • Violent Backlash (representatives from disadvantaged communities may face intimidation and violence, sometimes described as Panchayat Martyrs)

3. Restrictive Qualifications

  • Some States have imposed additional qualifications for candidates contesting Panchayat elections.
  • Rajbala v. State of Haryana (2015):
    • The Supreme Court upheld certain restrictions under the Haryana Panchayati Raj law. These included: minimum educational qualification
    • A functional toilet at the candidate's residence
    • No specified arrears of cooperative loans
    • No specified electricity bill arrears
  • Criticism: such requirements disproportionately affect the poor, women and disadvantaged communities, particularly where low educational attainment or financial hardship arises from structural inequalities.
  • Javed v. State of Haryana (2003): the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of a statutory two-child norm for Panchayat candidates.
  • Constitutional issue: whether such restrictions improve the quality of elected representatives or unnecessarily limit democratic participation.

4. Massive Corruption

  • Improper maintenance of accounts and weak auditing can contribute to corruption in local bodies.
  • Misuse of development funds.
  • Irregularities in contracts and beneficiary selection.
  • Poor financial transparency and accountability.

5. Existence of Parallel Bodies and Programmes

  • District Administration: every district is a Mini-Capital, as most State departments are represented through their field formations. Examples: District Transport Officer (DTO), District Health Officer (DHO), District Education Officer (DEO). These may perform functions that overlap with those assigned to PRIs.
  • Parastatals: bodies such as the District Rural Development Agency (DRDA) and other specialised district agencies perform development functions outside the direct control of elected Panchayats.
  • Parallel programmes: MPLADS (1993) and State-level MLALADS operate alongside local body development responsibilities. The 2nd ARC recommended abolition of MPLADS and MLALADS and favoured integrating parallel development agencies with elected local bodies.
  • Additional parallel agencies: some States have created Water User Associations and other specialised institutions whose responsibilities overlap with those of PRIs.
  • NGOs: operate in areas such as health, sanitation, nutrition and education. A cooperative framework for convergence is necessary between NGOs and PRIs.
  • Khap Panchayats: customary caste-based bodies in parts of northern India may influence or interfere with elected local institutions. They are distinct from constitutionally recognised Panchayats.

6. Increasing Criminality and Money and Muscle Power

  • A lot of violence and rivalry is reported from the villages where these elections are held. Criminality has increased in local bodies' elections. The journey from Panchayat Swabhiman (Pride) to Panchayat Cynicism has indeed been very short.
  • Impact: criminalisation weakens public confidence and discourages genuine democratic participation.

7. Domination of Senior Politicians

  • Part IX permits States to provide representation for MPs and MLAs in Panchayats above the village level. Their presence may weaken the initiative and leadership of elected local representatives.
  • 2nd ARC: recommended discontinuing such representation to protect the autonomy of local bodies.

8. Inadequate Organisational Structure

  • The responsibilities and functional profile of Panchayats have increased, but this has not been matched by adequate organisational development.
  • Inadequate Panchayat office buildings
  • Shortage of trained staff
  • Lack of technical and administrative infrastructure
  • In some villages, official work is conducted from the residence of the Panchayat Chairperson.
Key point

9. Poor Devolution of Funds, Functions and Functionaries (3Fs)

  • Even if the 3Fs are properly devolved, the notion of Functionality becomes important.
  • Panchayats may lack the capacity to use the devolved powers properly. Training, technical expertise and institutional support are often inadequate.
  • Key issue: genuine decentralisation requires Funds, Functions, Functionaries and Functionality.

Panchayat Devolution Index 2024

Panchayat Devolution Index 2024

  • The Ministry of Panchayati Raj released the Panchayat Devolution Index 2024 in February 2025.
  • It measures devolution through six dimensions: Framework, Functions, Finances, Functionaries, Capacity Enhancement and Accountability.
  • Findings: overall devolution increased from 39.9% to 43.9% between the study periods 2013-14 and 2021-22. Karnataka ranked first with 72.23, followed by Kerala (70.59) and Tamil Nadu (68.38). Maharashtra ranked fourth and Uttar Pradesh fifth.
  • Significance: considerable differences continue between States regarding the actual empowerment of PRIs.

Overall Assessment AND Election-related Inadequacies

Overall assessment

  • Even after three decades of the 73rd Amendment, local bodies have not fully developed into effective institutions of self-government. They are often considered shadowy outliers in the system of government.
  • 1. Inadequate Capacities: local bodies lack trained staff and institutional resources.
  • 2. Holding Together Federation: India is a Holding Together Federation, where substantial authority has historically been concentrated at higher levels of government.
  • 3. Acceptance of Centralised Power: the historical preference for centralised administration has limited the growth of local autonomy.
  • 4. Weak Public Pressure for Decentralisation: citizens often demand changes in Union or State Governments but place less pressure on them for genuine local decentralisation.
  • 5. Narrow Focus on Service Delivery: local bodies are frequently treated as channels for implementing government schemes rather than as independent institutions of self-government.

Election-related inadequacies

  • Postponement of elections: State Governments sometimes postpone local body elections by citing incomplete delimitation of constituencies, pending reservation matrix, and natural calamities and administrative difficulties.
  • Weakening of State Election Commissions: the SEC's ability to prevent postponement may be weakened by appointment of pliant officers, denial of adequate funds, and failure to provide necessary security personnel.
  • Kishansing Tomar v. Municipal Corporation of Ahmedabad (2006): the Supreme Court emphasised that elections to local bodies must be held within the constitutional time frame.

Women AND Developmental Impact

Impact of the 73rd Amendment on women's empowerment (factoids)

  • Women constitute approximately 49.75% of elected Panchayat representatives (MoPR, 2026). (verify)
  • Participation of women in Gram Sabhas has increased, particularly in women-headed Panchayats.
  • Women-headed Panchayats have shown greater attention to women's welfare, drinking water and other local priorities in several studies.
  • Women representatives have contributed to addressing social evils such as child marriage and alcoholism.
  • Women are gradually beginning to discover their feet and voice in Panchayats. Their social status has improved, with a positive impact on gender relations.

Problems faced by women

  • 1. Dual Responsibility: household responsibilities along with political duties.
  • 2. Lack of Security: criminalisation and violence in politics discourage many women from entering local body elections.
  • 3. Lack of Information and Knowledge: inadequate education, training and administrative knowledge may make elected women dependent on second-hand information.
  • 4. Panchayat Pati: women may act as proxy representatives while their husbands exercise actual decision-making powers.
  • 5. Lack of Adequate Exposure: patriarchal social restrictions may prevent women from presenting their views forcefully and assertively in public meetings.
  • 6. Restrictive Qualifications: educational requirements and two-child norms may disproportionately affect women.
  • 7. Limited Election from General Seats: women are frequently elected through reserved constituencies, while their representation from general seats remains limited.
  • 8. Impact of Rotational Policy: frequent rotation of reserved seats may prevent women from developing lasting political ties.
  • Suggestion: rotation of reserved seats may be considered after two election cycles (10 years) instead of one, to provide continuity in political leadership.

Developmental impact of PRIs

  • The developmental impact has been uneven across India, with considerable variation among States.
  • 1. Agriculture: in some States, such as Karnataka, agricultural and animal husbandry programmes were transferred to Panchayats without adequate technical expertise, affecting implementation. In contrast, PRIs in Gujarat and Tamil Nadu have contributed to agricultural development. Key issue: transfer of functions must be accompanied by the transfer of technical expertise and functionaries.
  • 2. Weaker Section Development: reservation has expanded the social base of politics (Rise of the Newly Enfranchised), but political representation has not always translated into socioeconomic empowerment. The Darji Committee (Gujarat) and Sadiq Ali Committee (Rajasthan) highlighted concerns about the development of weaker sections.
  • 3. Education: in many States, responsibilities relating to primary and secondary education have been devolved to local bodies, but results remain uneven due to inadequate staff, finances and administrative authority.
Way forward / key point

Overall assessment: between political development and socioeconomic development, the former has been better realised than the latter. The 73rd Amendment has considerably expanded democratic representation, but developmental performance remains constrained by incomplete devolution, weak institutional capacity and State control.

5 · Local Self-government: Finances · State Finance Commission · OSR · LOW Equilibrium TRAP#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Local Self-Government module pp. 16-17. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Local bodies have little revenue potential, hesitate to tax, depend on uncertain, irregular and tied State grants, and cannot borrow. The State Finance Commission, meant to fix this, is among the weakest links. Around 95% of Panchayat revenue receipts are grants (RBI 2024), and low own-source revenue creates a low equilibrium trap.

Problems OF Local Government Finances

1. Inelastic Taxes

  • The taxes assigned to local bodies often have very little revenue potential, such as taxes and fees on boats, rickshaws, tea stalls and vehicles.
  • As a result, local bodies have inadequate resources to meet their developmental responsibilities.

2. PRIs Do Not Make Use of Limited Taxation Powers

  • PRIs often do not utilise even their limited taxation powers due to the fear of backlash from people who are closely in contact with the community.
  • Other reasons include inadequate tax records, poor collection mechanisms and limited administrative capacity.

3. State Grants Are Uncertain, Irregular and Tied

  • Uncertain: local bodies cannot accurately estimate future grants.
  • Irregular: delayed releases affect development programmes.
  • Tied: funds can be used only for specified purposes, not necessarily according to local needs.

4. No Market for Loans

  • PRIs have very restrictive borrowing powers, which generally require State Government approval.
  • Their limited revenue and weak creditworthiness further restrict access to institutional borrowing.

State Finance Commission (SFC)

What the SFC is

  • The SFC has been one of the weakest links in fiscal decentralisation.
  • Under Article 243I, the Governor constitutes an SFC every five years to review Panchayat finances and recommend revenue sharing, grants and measures to improve their financial position.
  • Article 243Y extends its role to Municipalities.

Problems with State Finance Commissions

  • 1. Periodicity Not Maintained: the constitutional requirement to constitute SFCs every five years has not been followed regularly by several States.
  • 2. Inadequate Survey of Local Finances: SFCs conduct inadequate surveys due to the large number of local bodies and the absence of proper maintenance of accounts.
  • 3. Poor Quality of Members: qualifications are determined by State legislation. Commissions may lack adequate representation of experts in public finance and local governance.
  • 4. Fate of Recommendations: SFC recommendations are advisory. Many are rejected, partially accepted or not implemented by State Governments.
  • 5. No Synchronisation with Union Finance Commission (UFC): SFCs and UFCs are constituted at different times. Delayed SFC reports weaken their use by the UFC while recommending grants for local bodies under Article 280.
  • 6. Poor Quality of Reports: some SFC reports lack reliable data and recommendations consistent with their objectives.
  • 7. Delay in Reports and Action Taken Reports (ATRs): delays in submitting reports and placing ATRs before State Legislatures weaken their relevance and accountability.
  • 8. Global Sharing Formula Not Used: a Global Sharing Formula, similar to tax sharing between the Union and States, could be adopted between States and local bodies. Instead, revenue sharing is often selective and discretionary.
Note

Recent update: the Ministry of Panchayati Raj released the Report of the Committee on Datasets for State Finance Commissions in June 2026. It emphasised reliable, timely and standardised financial data for strengthening SFC recommendations. (verify)

TIED Nature OF Funds

Tied funds

  • Although funds are available to local bodies, many are tied funds, meaning they can be used only for specified purposes.
  • As a result, PRIs often become instruments for implementing Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) and State Sponsored Schemes (SSS) rather than independent institutions of self-government.
Tied grantsUntied grants
Used only for specified activities.Used for local priorities within applicable guidelines.
Restrict expenditure flexibility.Strengthen local financial autonomy.

Value addition

Important: the old estimate that 93% of Panchayat funds were tied should not be confused with the share received as government grants. According to the RBI Report on Finances of Panchayati Raj Institutions (2024), around 95% of Panchayat revenue receipts came from grants provided by higher governments. (verify)

OWN Source Revenue (OSR)

Own Source Revenue

  • OSR is revenue raised directly by local bodies through taxes, fees, user charges and income from their assets.
  • Major problems:
    • Inadequate Taxation Powers (States have not devolved sufficient revenue-raising powers)
    • Poor Tax Collection (Panchayats often fail to realise their full revenue potential)
    • Political Resistance (local representatives hesitate to impose taxes and fees)
    • Weak Enforcement (inadequate records and enforcement allow defaults to continue)
  • Latest government data (MoPR, March 2026): PRIs in 30 States/UTs collected around Rs 25,595 crore in OSR during 2017-18 to 2021-22. The national average per capita OSR was approximately Rs 59 annually. (verify)
  • Significance: local revenue generation remains very limited despite substantial developmental responsibilities.

LOW Equilibrium TRAP

Key point

Low Equilibrium Trap (Economic Survey 2017-18)

The vicious circle

Low tax collection
Inadequate funds
Poor public services
Low willingness to pay taxes
Continued low tax collection (back to start)

Reading the trap

  • Weak revenue collection results in poor public services.
  • Poor services reduce people's willingness to pay local taxes.
  • Political representatives become reluctant to impose or increase taxes.
  • Local bodies remain dependent on higher government grants.
  • Way forward: strengthen own source revenue, financial accountability and service delivery to break this cycle.

Suggestions

Six suggestions

  • 1. Strengthen SFCs: ensure timely constitution, expert membership and implementation of recommendations.
  • 2. Improve Own Source Revenue: strengthen tax assessment, collection and enforcement.
  • 3. Adopt Global Sharing Formula: provide local bodies with a predictable share of State revenues.
  • 4. Increase Untied Grants: allow greater flexibility to address locally identified priorities.
  • 5. Improve Financial Accountability: maintain proper accounts, conduct regular audits and publish financial information.
  • 6. Strengthen Fiscal Autonomy: match local responsibilities with adequate financial resources and revenue-raising powers.
Way forward / key point

Key Issue: without financial decentralisation, local bodies cannot function as genuine institutions of self-government.

6 · THE Municipalities · 74TH Amendment · Twelfth Schedule · DPC · MPC · Urban Problems#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Local Self-Government module pp. 18-22. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Urban governance has long been neglected. The 74th Amendment (1992) added Part IX-A (Articles 243P-243ZG) and the Twelfth Schedule. Municipalities are Nagar Panchayats, Municipal Councils and Municipal Corporations, with no hierarchy among them. District and Metropolitan Planning Committees consolidate rural and urban plans but are weak. Municipal government follows the Bombay (Mayor weak, Commissioner strong) or Howrah/Calcutta (Mayor-in-Council) pattern, and cities face an institutional jungle of overlapping bodies.

PART IX-A: 74TH Constitutional Amendment ACT, 1992

Background

  • Urban space management (towns and cities) has historically been neglected by both the Union and State Governments.
  • Urban areas are the principal drivers of economic growth, yet urban governance has received inadequate attention.
  • Committees and commissions examining urban development have also been fewer compared with those dealing with rural India.
  • The National Commission on Urbanisation (1985), headed by Charles Correa, was an important step towards improving urban governance.

What the 74th Amendment did

  • Gave constitutional status to Urban Local Bodies (ULBs).
  • Added Part IX-A (Articles 243P to 243ZG).
  • Added the Twelfth Schedule (18 subjects).
  • Came into force on 1 June 1993.

How the category is decided

  • The Governor specifies the category of urban area, considering population, population density, revenue generation, non-agricultural employment and economic importance.
  • Important: unlike PRIs, there is no hierarchy among the three types of Municipalities, as each administers a different urban area.

Industrial Townships

  • Under Article 243Q, the Governor may notify an Industrial Township where an industrial establishment provides or proposes to provide municipal services.
  • Concern: such arrangements may deny residents an elected municipal institution, raising questions of democratic representation and accountability.

Rural-to-urban transition

  • An area should be classified as a Nagar Panchayat with utmost caution because:
  • Cost of administration in urban areas is higher.
  • Rates of taxes and user charges may increase.
  • Some ongoing rural programmes may cease to apply.
  • The Gram Sabha ceases to function under the ordinary rural Panchayati Raj framework.

Other important provisions

  • 1. Composition (Article 243R): members are directly elected from Wards. States may also provide representation for MPs, MLAs, MLCs and experts. Nominated experts do not have voting rights in municipal meetings.
  • 2. Reservation (Article 243T): SC/ST reservation is proportionate to population, while at least one-third of directly elected seats are reserved for women. States may also provide OBC reservation.
  • 3. Duration (Article 243U): five-year tenure. After premature dissolution, elections must ordinarily be completed within six months.
  • 4. Powers (Article 243W): States may devolve responsibilities relating to economic development, social justice and the 18 subjects of the Twelfth Schedule.
  • 5. Finances (Articles 243X-243Y): States determine municipal taxation powers. The SFC reviews their financial position.

Twelfth Schedule: 18 Subjects

Important subjects

  • Urban Planning and Regulation of Land Use.
  • Roads, Bridges and Water Supply.
  • Public Health, Sanitation and Solid Waste Management.
  • Fire Services and Environmental Protection.
  • Slum Improvement and Urban Poverty Alleviation.
  • Urban Amenities, Parks and Playgrounds.
  • Street Lighting and Public Conveniences.
  • Registration of Births and Deaths.
Value addition

Important: inclusion in the Twelfth Schedule does not automatically transfer these functions to Municipalities.

Planning Committees: DPC AND MPC

District Planning Committee (DPC): Article 243ZD

  • Consolidates plans prepared by Panchayats and Municipalities and prepares a draft development plan for the district as a whole.
  • At least four-fifths of members must be elected from the district-level Panchayat and Municipalities.
  • Representation is based on the rural-urban population ratio.
  • Considers spatial planning, water resources, integrated infrastructure, environmental conservation and available resources.

Metropolitan Planning Committee (MPC): Article 243ZE

  • Prepares a draft development plan for the metropolitan area as a whole.
  • A Metropolitan Area has a population of 10 lakh or more, subject to constitutional notification.
  • At least two-thirds of members are elected from Municipalities and Panchayat Chairpersons in the metropolitan area.
  • Considers coordinated spatial planning, infrastructure, natural resources and environmental conservation.
Way forward / key point

Significance: DPCs and MPCs seek to achieve integrated rural-urban planning and development.

Problems with grassroot planning bodies

  • 1. Delays in Setting up DPCs: several States delayed the constitution of DPCs. For example, Gujarat constituted its DPC framework in 2006, long after the 74th Amendment.
  • 2. Defunct Entities: in some States, planning bodies exist largely on paper. Meetings are rarely held, making them defunct and moribund.
  • 3. Lack of Expertise: DPCs and MPCs often lack adequate technical expertise in planning, infrastructure and finance.
  • 4. State-Level Ministers as Chairpersons: State-level political leaders may dominate these grassroots planning bodies.
  • 5. Lack of Financial Decentralisation: planning bodies often lack adequate financial resources. In the absence of financial decentralisation, planning decentralisation becomes a hollow exercise.
  • 6. Rural-Urban Integration Not Achieved: coordination remains weak between small towns, surrounding villages and rural areas, particularly in infrastructure and resource management.

Types OF Municipal Government IN India

1. Bombay Pattern

  • Most States follow variations of the Bombay Pattern, based on the Executive-Deliberative Dichotomy.
  • The Mayor is primarily the political head and presiding officer of the Municipal Corporation.
  • The Municipal Commissioner exercises substantial executive and administrative powers.
  • The appointment, posting and transfer of the Commissioner are generally controlled by the State Government.
  • Problem: the elected Mayor may have limited executive authority, while real administrative power rests with the State-appointed Commissioner.

2. Howrah/Calcutta Pattern

  • Associated with the Mayor-in-Council system.
  • The Mayor exercises real executive powers, assisted by elected members in the Mayor-in-Council.
  • The Municipal Commissioner performs administrative responsibilities within this framework.
  • Significance: strengthens democratic political accountability by giving greater executive authority to elected municipal leadership.
  • Reform: the 2nd ARC recommended a directly elected Mayor with real executive powers.

Problems OF Urban Administration

Fourteen problems

  • 1. Haphazard and Unplanned Urbanisation: migration (rural to cities), overcrowding, crippling infrastructure (housing, water supply, sanitation, electricity), increasing slums.
  • 2. Increasing Health-Related Problems:
    • Recurring public health problems such as dengue outbreaks in Delhi
    • Poor sanitation and drainage
    • Greater dependence on private healthcare raises costs for the urban poor
    • Community health often receives inadequate attention
  • 3. Increasing Urban Poverty: high living costs, insecure employment, inadequate housing and poor access to basic services.
  • 4. Traffic Congestion:
    • Increasing population and vehicle ownership. Possible solutions: Pedestrianisation (restrict vehicles in selected areas, e.g. Chandni Chowk, Delhi)
    • Congestion Levy (charge vehicles entering heavily congested areas)
    • Public Transportation over private vehicles
    • Higher Parking Fees
  • 5. Pollution of All Kinds: air, water, land degradation and noise pollution.
  • 6. Power and Water Shortages: inadequate infrastructure and rising demand.
  • 7. Rising Crimes, Especially against Women:
    • Street crime, unsafe public spaces and inadequate infrastructure
    • Women's safety remains an important issue
  • 8. Massive Corruption: in municipal contracts, building permissions and public works.
  • 9. Poor Devolution of the 3Fs: excessive State control and inadequate devolution of Funds, Functions and Functionaries.
  • 10. Inadequate Capacity Building: elected and appointed functionaries often lack training, technical expertise and administrative capacity.
  • 11. Absence of Urban Way of Life:
    • Weak civic awareness and participation
    • Citizens may not adequately demand accountability
    • Poor civic behaviour affects sanitation and maintenance. Key issue: the absence of effective public pressure reduces incentives for better municipal performance
  • 12. Lack of Quality Leadership: in many municipalities the Mayor has limited executive powers, weakening leadership and political accountability.
  • 13. Focus Shift towards Rural Areas: political leadership has traditionally focused more on rural development, while urban administration has received inadequate institutional attention.
  • 14. Institutional Jungle: many parallel bodies operate at the municipal level with overlapping functions.

Example of the institutional jungle: Delhi

  • Municipal Corporation of Delhi (MCD): provides local civic services.
  • Government of NCT of Delhi: controls several urban administrative functions.
  • Union Government: exercises authority over subjects such as land, police and public order under the constitutional framework.
  • Delhi Development Authority (DDA): performs important functions relating to land development and planning.
  • Update: the three earlier Municipal Corporations were reunified into a single MCD on 22 May 2022.
  • Key issue: multiple institutions create confusion, overlapping responsibilities and weak public accountability.

WARD Committees, AREA Sabhas, Smart Cities

Ward Committees and Area Sabhas

  • Ward Committees (Article 243S) are mandatory in municipalities with populations of three lakh or more. They provide a mechanism for neighbourhood-level participation.
  • Area Sabhas: smaller participatory forums proposed to improve citizen involvement in urban governance, similar in purpose to rural Gram Sabhas.
  • Problems: inadequate powers, irregular meetings and weak public participation.
  • Reform: strengthen Ward Committees and provide meaningful opportunities for citizen participation and local accountability.

Smart Cities Mission and municipal autonomy

  • The Smart Cities Mission (2015) was implemented through Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) in 100 selected cities.
  • Positive aspects:
    • Professional project management
    • Faster implementation of infrastructure projects
    • Greater use of technology in urban administration
  • Concerns:
    • SPVs may bypass elected Urban Local Bodies
    • Important decisions may shift towards appointed officials
    • Weakens the spirit of democratic decentralisation under the 74th Amendment
  • Way forward: specialised agencies should support elected Municipalities rather than replace them.
Way forward / key point

Overall assessment: the 74th Amendment strengthened the constitutional position of Municipalities, but urban governance remains constrained by weak municipal leadership, incomplete devolution, inadequate capacity and institutional fragmentation. Central issue: ULBs must develop into genuine institutions of self-government rather than mainly service delivery agencies of State Governments.
