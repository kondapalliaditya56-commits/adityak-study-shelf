1 · The judicial system and the Supreme Court#

India has a single, unified judicial system that brings all levels of courts under one framework, unlike many federal nations with separate state and federal courts.

The pyramid

Supreme Court
apex; guardian of the Constitution and final interpreter of law
High Courts
state level
District and subordinate courts
below the High Courts
Control

Lower courts work under the supervision and control of the higher courts, so laws are interpreted and applied uniformly across the country.

  • The Supreme Court was established on 28 January 1950.
  • It protects fundamental rights, settles disputes between governments and maintains the rule of law.

Composition and strength (Article 124)

  • Art. 124 provides for the establishment and composition of the Court.
  • The Court has a Chief Justice of India (CJI) and as many other judges as Parliament decides by law, so Parliament can revise strength with workload and population.
  • Present strength: 1 CJI + 33 other judges = 34.
  • The number was raised by the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019, to meet rising cases and reduce pendency.
Strength

1 + 33 = 34; set by Parliament; last raised in 2019.

2 · Appointment of judges and the Collegium#

Under Art. 124(2) the President appoints every Supreme Court judge after consultation with such Supreme Court and High Court judges as he deems necessary. What "consultation" meant became a long debate.

Early practice (1950 to early 1980s)

  • Appointments were made by the President on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers, after consulting the CJI.
  • The CJI's opinion was respected but not binding, so the Executive had a major role.

The cases

Judges Cases and the collegium
StageCaseWhat it held
First Judges Case (1982)S.P. Gupta v. Union of India"Consultation" is not "concurrence"; the President is not bound by the CJI's advice; primacy to the executive. Criticised as a threat to independence
Second Judges Case (1993)Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (constitutional bench)Reversed it: "consultation" means "concurrence"; primacy to the judiciary; CJI recommends in consultation with the two senior-most judges: basis of the collegium
Third Judges Case (1998)President's opinion sought under Art. 143Collegium expanded to five: CJI + four senior-most judges; if even two judges disagree, the CJI should not send the recommendation forward
Judges Cases
GAC

Gupta 1982 = executive, Advocates-on-Record 1993 = judiciary, Collegium of five 1998 (Art. 143 opinion): "Government Asks, Court decides."

Case years

82 - 93 - 98: Executive wins, Judiciary wins, Collegium grows from 3 to 5.

  • The 1998 collegium is the present system for appointing judges to the Supreme Court and High Courts.

The attempted reform: NJAC

  • Parliament passed the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014, creating the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) with members from both judiciary and executive, for transparency and accountability.
  • In 2015 the Supreme Court struck it down: it violated the independence of the judiciary, part of the basic structure.
  • So the collegium continues.

3 · Appointment of the Chief Justice of India#

The CJI is appointed by the President under Art. 124(2); the process evolved through convention and judicial practice.

CJI appointment through time
PeriodPractice
1950-1973The senior-most judge was conventionally appointed CJI: an unwritten rule of seniority protecting the judiciary from political influence
1973Convention ignored: Justice A. N. Ray appointed CJI, superseding three senior judges (Shelet, Hegde and Grover)
1977Convention ignored again: Justice M. H. Beg superseded Justice H. R. Khanna
Since 1977Senior-most judge strictly appointed; now a well-established constitutional custom
  • Both supersessions drew wide criticism as executive interference.
  • The Constitution does not explicitly require seniority.
  • Formally the President appoints, on the recommendation of the outgoing Chief Justice, after consultation within the judiciary.
Supersession
RB

Ray 1973 (over three), Beg 1977 (over Khanna): "Rules Broken twice".

Qualifications (Art. 124(3))

A person must be a citizen of India and satisfy any one of:

Any one condition
RouteRequirement

Qualifications
JAJ

Judge 5 years, Advocate 10 years, Jurist by President's opinion: "5, 10, Jurist".

Oath or affirmation (Art. 124(6))

  • Made and subscribed before entering office, administered by the President or a person he authorises.
  • The judge swears: true faith and allegiance to the Constitution; uphold sovereignty and integrity of India; perform duties faithfully, without fear or favour, affection or ill-will; uphold the Constitution and the laws.

Salaries (Art. 125)

  • Salaries are fixed by Parliament by law; until then as in the Second Schedule.
  • Allowances, privileges, leave and pension also by Parliamentary law, till then as in the Second Schedule.
  • Protection clause: after appointment, salary, allowances or pension cannot be reduced to the judge's disadvantage.

5 · Tenure and removal#

Tenure and removal are in Arts. 124(2)-(4) and aim at security of office and independence.

Tenure

  • A judge holds office until age 65 (Art. 124(2)).
  • A judge may resign by writing to the President, or vacate office on appointment to another constitutional post (for example President or Governor).
  • Any age question is decided by an authority and manner provided by Parliament.

Removal (Art. 124(4))

  • Only by the President, on a motion passed by both Houses.
  • Only two grounds: proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
  • Special majority in each House: majority of total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting.

Procedure in practice: Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968

8 · Original jurisdiction (Art. 131)#

The Court's jurisdiction is set out mainly in Arts. 131 to 139A. Original jurisdiction means cases are heard directly, without filing first in a High Court or lower court.

Exclusive original jurisdiction: disputes

1. Between the Government of India and one or more States; or 2. Between the Government of India and one or more States on one side and one or more other States on the other; or 3. Between two or more States, if and only if the dispute involves a question (of law or fact) on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends.

9 · Writ jurisdiction (Art. 32)#

Under Art. 32 every citizen can approach the Supreme Court directly to enforce Fundamental Rights. Dr B. R. Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul of the Constitution". The Court may issue directions, orders or writs, including five:

Five writs
WritMeaningUse
Habeas Corpus"to have the body"Produce an illegally detained person so the court can examine detention and order release; most powerful weapon for personal liberty
Mandamus"we command"Directs a public authority (officer, agency, lower court) to do a public or statutory duty; cannot be issued against the President, Governors or private individuals
Prohibition(none given)Higher court tells a lower court or tribunal to stop proceedings beyond its jurisdiction or against natural justice; preventive, before judgment
Certiorari"to be certified"Higher court quashes an order of a lower court or tribunal that acted without jurisdiction or against due process; preventive and corrective
Quo Warranto"by what authority"Inquires into legality of a claim to public office; removes an unlawful holder
Writs
HMPCQ

Habeas, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo warranto: "Hot Masala Puffs Cost Quite a lot".

Prohibition vs Certiorari

Prohibition = before judgment (stop); Certiorari = quash (preventive and corrective).

10 · Appellate jurisdiction (Arts. 132-136)#

The Court is the final court of appeal in constitutional, civil and criminal matters, under Arts. 132 to 136.

134CriminalSee below

CCC

Constitution 132, Civil 133, Criminal 134: "Constitution, Civil, Criminal: one, two, three after 131."

Criminal appeals (Art. 134)

Appeal lies when the High Court: 1. Reverses an acquittal and sentences the accused to death; 2. Withdraws a case from a subordinate court, tries it and convicts; 3. Certifies the case fit for appeal.

  • In the first two categories appeal is of right: no High Court certificate needed.
  • If the High Court reverses a conviction and acquits, there is no automatic right of appeal.
1970 expansion

Parliament widened criminal appellate jurisdiction. Appeals also lie where the High Court (1) reverses an acquittal and sentences to life imprisonment or at least ten years, or (2) takes over a case, convicts, and sentences to life or ten years or more.

Special Leave to Appeal (Art. 136)

  • Grants special leave from any judgment, decree or order of any court or tribunal in India (except military courts).
  • Used where there is gross injustice or violation of due process.
Four features of special leave
FeatureMeaning
DiscretionaryNot an appeal as of right; the Court decides whether to entertain it
Any stageAgainst a final decision or an interlocutory (interim) order
Any subjectConstitutional, civil, criminal, tax, labour, revenue, professional conduct or other questions of law
Any court or tribunalNot only High Courts (other than military courts)
  • The Court calls it an exceptional, residual power to be used sparingly: grave miscarriage of justice, glaring error of law or question of general public importance.

11 · Advisory jurisdiction (Art. 143)#

The President may seek the Court's opinion on legal or constitutional questions of great public importance, making the Court a constitutional adviser to the executive.

Two clauses
ClauseSubjectCourt's duty
143(1)Any question of law or fact of public importance, or a substantial question on interpreting the ConstitutionCourt may tender or may refuse its opinion; opinion not binding but carries high authority and is generally accepted
143(2)Disputes from pre-Constitution treaties, covenants or agreements outside Art. 131Court must tender its opinion (investigation and report)
143

(1) = may, (2) = must. Pre-Constitution disputes are barred from Art. 131, so they come through 143(2).

12 · Court of Record and contempt (Art. 129)#

A Court of Record preserves its proceedings and judgments as permanent evidence. Two powers follow. 1. Preservation of records: judgments, orders and acts are recorded for perpetual memory and serve as precedents; accepted as authentic evidence and cannot be questioned in any court; they form part of the doctrine of precedent. 2. Power to punish for contempt: any act lowering the Court's dignity or obstructing justice. In 1991 the Court ruled this covers contempt of itself, High Courts, subordinate courts and tribunals across the country.

Contempt of Courts Act, 1971

  • Enacted on the recommendations of the H. N. Sanyal Committee (1961).

Civil contempt

  • Wilful disobedience of a court's order, judgment or undertaking

Criminal contempt

  • Publication or act that scandalises or lowers a court's authority
  • prejudices or interferes with judicial proceedings
  • obstructs the administration of justice
Rules under the Act
PointRule
Not contemptFair and accurate reporting, innocent publication, reasonable criticism of judicial acts
PunishmentSimple imprisonment up to six months, or fine up to Rs 2,000, or both
LimitationNo proceedings after one year from the alleged act
ExemptionDoes not apply to Nyaya Panchayats or village courts
Contempt numbers
SOT

Six months, One year, Two thousand: "Silence Often Tranquil".

13 · Judicial review, review and curative petitions#

Judicial review

  • Power to examine the constitutionality of laws passed by the legislature and executive orders, at the Centre and in the States.
  • An inconsistent law or order is declared ultra vires (beyond power), null and void, and cannot be enforced.
  • It makes the Court guardian of the Constitution, ensures no organ exceeds its authority, and is the cornerstone of constitutional democracy.

Review (Art. 137)

  • The Court may review any judgment or order passed by it, to correct its own errors.
  • Filed within 30 days; heard by the same bench or judge.
  • Grounds: new and important evidence; error apparent on the face of the record; any other sufficient reason.
  • The Court may dismiss, or issue notice to the opposite party.

Curative petition

  • Even after a review is dismissed, the Court may reconsider through a curative petition, evolved in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002).

14 · Constitutional interpretation: 13 doctrines#

As final authority the Court determines the true meaning, intent and scope of the Constitution, using doctrines it has evolved.

Doctrines and landmark cases

EclipseA law inconsistent with FRs is not nullified but inoperative ("in eclipse") until the inconsistency is removedBhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P. (1955)

16 · Independence of the Supreme Court#

The Court guards the Constitution and Fundamental Rights, so it must be free of executive and legislative influence.

Seven safeguards
SafeguardHow it protects
1. Mode of appointmentPresident in consultation with senior judges (collegium): limits executive discretion
2. Security of tenureRemoval only by impeachment under Art. 124(4) for proved misbehaviour or incapacity; not at the President's pleasure
3. Fixed service conditionsSet by Parliament; cannot be changed to disadvantage, except during a Financial Emergency
4. Expenditure chargedSalaries, pensions and administrative expenses are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India: non-votable in Parliament
5. Conduct not discussedNeither Parliament nor State Legislatures may discuss judges' conduct, except in impeachment
6. Ban on post-retirement practiceRetired judges cannot practise or appear before any court or authority in India
7. Jurisdiction cannot be curtailedParliament cannot reduce powers, but may expand them under Arts. 138 and 139
Seven safeguards
ASFECBJ

Appointment, Security of tenure, Fixed conditions, Expenditure charged, Conduct not discussed, Ban on practice, Jurisdiction not curtailed: "A Safe Fortress Ensures Courts Behave Justly".
