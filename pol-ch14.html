In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • India has one single court system shaped like a pyramid: Supreme Court at the top, High Courts in the states, district and subordinate courts below. Higher courts supervise the lower ones.
  • The Supreme Court began on 28 January 1950 (Article 124). It has the Chief Justice of India plus 33 judges, total 34, fixed by the 2019 Act.
  • Judges are appointed by the President through the collegium (CJI and four senior-most judges). The NJAC (99th Amendment, 2014) tried to replace it but was struck down in 2015.
  • A judge keeps office till 65 and can be removed only for proved misbehaviour or incapacity, by a special majority in both Houses. No judge has ever been removed.
  • Its powers: original (Art 131), writ (Art 32), appellate (Arts 132 to 134A, 136), advisory (Art 143), court of record (Art 129), and review and curative powers. Seven safeguards protect its independence.

Key terms

Collegium
Body of the CJI and four senior-most Supreme Court judges that recommends judges for appointment and transfer
Consultation vs concurrence
First Judges Case: consultation is not concurrence (executive primacy). Second Judges Case: consultation means concurrence (judiciary primacy)
NJAC
National Judicial Appointments Commission created by the 99th Amendment (2014), with judiciary and executive members; struck down in 2015
Original jurisdiction (Art 131)
Exclusive power to hear Centre-State and State-State disputes directly, not on appeal
Writ
Order to enforce Fundamental Rights under Art 32: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto
Special Leave to Appeal (Art 136)
Discretionary appeal from any court or tribunal except military courts; not a right
Advisory jurisdiction (Art 143)
President may seek the Court's opinion; fifteen references so far
Curative petition
Last remedy after a review is dismissed; evolved in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002)

1 · The judicial system and the Supreme Court#

  • India has a single, unified judicial system that brings all levels of courts under one framework, unlike many federal nations with separate state and federal courts.

The pyramid

  • Supreme Court:
    • Apex
    • Guardian of the Constitution and final interpreter of law
  • High Courts: state level
  • District and subordinate courts: below the High Courts
  • Control: Lower courts work under the supervision and control of the higher courts, so laws are interpreted and applied uniformly across the country.
  • The Supreme Court was established on 28 January 1950.
  • It protects fundamental rights, settles disputes between governments and maintains the rule of law.

Composition and strength (Article 124)

  • Art. 124 provides for the establishment and composition of the Court.
  • The Court has a Chief Justice of India (CJI) and as many other judges as Parliament decides by law, so Parliament can revise strength with workload and population.
  • Present strength: 1 CJI + 33 other judges = 34.
  • The number was raised by the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019, to meet rising cases and reduce pendency.
  • Shortcut:
    • Strength. 1 + 33 = 34
    • Set by Parliament
    • Last raised in 2019
Current affairs · Aug 2026SC strength raised to 37SC (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2026 raises strength from 33 to 37 excluding CJI. Article 124(1) lets Parliament fix number. Art 124(3) distinguished jurist route never used.

2 · Appointment of judges and the Collegium#

  • Under Art. 124(2) the President appoints every Supreme Court judge after consultation with such Supreme Court and High Court judges as he deems necessary. What "consultation" meant became a long debate.
Current affairs · July 2026Appeals to two-judge HC benchUnder the 2026 exams amendment, appeals lie before a two-judge High Court bench within 30 days, decided within 3 months, P. Ramachandra Rao (2002) doubts rigid trial limits.

Early practice (1950 to early 1980s)

  • Appointments were made by the President on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers, after consulting the CJI.
  • The CJI's opinion was respected but not binding, so the Executive had a major role.

Judges Cases and the collegium

StageCaseWhat it held
First Judges Case (1982)S.P. Gupta v. Union of India"Consultation" is not "concurrence"; the President is not bound by the CJI's advice; primacy to the executive. Criticised as a threat to independence
Second Judges Case (1993)Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (constitutional bench)Reversed it: "consultation" means "concurrence"; primacy to the judiciary; CJI recommends in consultation with the two senior-most judges: basis of the collegium
Third Judges Case (1998)President's opinion sought under Art. 143Collegium expanded to five: CJI + four senior-most judges; if even two judges disagree, the CJI should not send the recommendation forward
  • Memory aid (Judges Cases): Gupta 1982 = executive, Advocates-on-Record 1993 = judiciary, Collegium of five 1998 (Art. 143 opinion): "Government Asks, Court decides."
  • Shortcut: Case years. 82 - 93 - 98: Executive wins, Judiciary wins, Collegium grows from 3 to 5.
  • The 1998 collegium is the present system for appointing judges to the Supreme Court and High Courts.

The attempted reform: NJAC

  • Parliament passed the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014, creating the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) with members from both judiciary and executive, for transparency and accountability.
  • In 2015 the Supreme Court struck it down: it violated the independence of the judiciary, part of the basic structure.
  • So the collegium continues.

  • Union Law Ministry: receives the recommendations
  • Prime Minister: advised by the Ministry, advises the President

3 · Appointment of the Chief Justice of India#

  • The CJI is appointed by the President under Art. 124(2)
  • The process evolved through convention and judicial practice

CJI appointment through time

PeriodPractice
1950-1973The senior-most judge was conventionally appointed CJI: an unwritten rule of seniority protecting the judiciary from political influence
1973Convention ignored: Justice A. N. Ray appointed CJI, superseding three senior judges (Shelet, Hegde and Grover)
1977Convention ignored again: Justice M. H. Beg superseded Justice H. R. Khanna
Since 1977Senior-most judge strictly appointed; now a well-established constitutional custom
  • Both supersessions drew wide criticism as executive interference.
  • The Constitution does not explicitly require seniority.
  • Formally the President appoints, on the recommendation of the outgoing Chief Justice, after consultation within the judiciary.
  • Memory aid (Supersession): Ray 1973 (over three), Beg 1977 (over Khanna): "Rules Broken twice".

4 · Qualifications, oath and salaries#

Qualifications (Art. 124(3))

  • A person must be a citizen of India and satisfy any one of:

Any one condition

RouteRequirement
JudgeJudge of a High Court (or two or more in succession) for at least 5 years
AdvocateAdvocate of a High Court (or two or more in succession) for at least 10 years
JuristIn the President's opinion, a distinguished jurist
  • Memory aid (Qualifications): Judge 5 years, Advocate 10 years, Jurist by President's opinion: "5, 10, Jurist".

Oath or affirmation (Art. 124(6))

  • Made and subscribed before entering office, administered by the President or a person he authorises.
  • The judge swears:
    • True faith and allegiance to the Constitution
    • Uphold sovereignty and integrity of India
    • Perform duties faithfully, without fear or favour, affection or ill-will
    • Uphold the Constitution and the laws

Salaries (Art. 125)

  • Salaries are fixed by Parliament by law
  • Until then as in the Second Schedule
  • Allowances, privileges, leave and pension also by Parliamentary law, till then as in the Second Schedule.
  • Protection clause: after appointment, salary, allowances or pension cannot be reduced to the judge's disadvantage.

5 · Tenure and removal#

  • Tenure and removal are in Arts. 124(2)-(4) and aim at security of office and independence.

Tenure

  • A judge holds office until age 65 (Art. 124(2)).
  • A judge may resign by writing to the President, or vacate office on appointment to another constitutional post (for example President or Governor).
  • Any age question is decided by an authority and manner provided by Parliament.

Removal (Art. 124(4))

  • Only by the President, on a motion passed by both Houses.
  • Only two grounds: proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
  • Special majority in each House: majority of total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting.

Procedure in practice: Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968

Removal of a judge

  • Motion: signed by 100 Lok Sabha or 50 Rajya Sabha members
  • Admission: Speaker or Chairman may admit or refuse
  • Inquiry committee: 3 members: a Supreme Court judge, a High Court Chief Justice, a distinguished jurist
  • Report: to Speaker or Chairman, who places it before the House
  • Voting: if charges proved, both Houses vote by special majority
  • President's order: removes the judge!bad
Removal of a judge
  • Memory aid (Impeachment numbers): Hundred in LS, Fifty in RS, Three-member committee: "How Few They need!" (100, 50, 3).
  • So far no Supreme Court judge has been removed, though motions were initiated (for example against Justice V. Ramaswami in 1993).

Also in the handout

  • The age limit and secure tenure prevent arbitrary removal and allow judges to function independently.
  • The difficulty of the impeachment procedure makes it almost impossible to remove a judge arbitrarily.

8 · Original jurisdiction (Art. 131)#

  • The Court's jurisdiction is set out mainly in Arts. 131 to 139A. Original jurisdiction means cases are heard directly, without filing first in a High Court or lower court.

Exclusive original jurisdiction: disputes

  • 1. Between the Government of India and one or more States
  • Or 2. Between the Government of India and one or more States on one side and one or more other States on the other
  • Or 3. Between two or more States, if and only if the dispute involves a question (of law or fact) on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends
  • Examples in the handout: The Cauvery water conflict (Tamil Nadu vs Karnataka) and State boundary issues.

Not covered by original jurisdiction

Exclusions

Excluded matterDetail
Pre-Constitution agreementsTreaty, agreement, covenant, engagement, sanad or similar instrument made before the commencement of the Constitution
Excluded treatiesTreaty or agreement that expressly says the Supreme Court has no jurisdiction
Inter-State water disputesHandled under the Inter-State Water Disputes Act, 1956
Finance Commission mattersAdjustments and recommendations under Art. 280
Adjustment of certain expenses and pensionse.g. sharing defence or administrative expenses between Centre and States
Ordinary commercial disputesPurely commercial disputes between Centre and a State
Recovery of damagesClaims by a State against the Centre for damages or compensation

9 · Writ jurisdiction (Art. 32)#

  • Under Art. 32 every citizen can approach the Supreme Court directly to enforce Fundamental Rights. Dr B. R. Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul of the Constitution". The Court may issue directions, orders or writs, including five:

Five writs

WritMeaningUse
Habeas Corpus"to have the body"Produce an illegally detained person so the court can examine detention and order release; most powerful weapon for personal liberty
Mandamus"we command"Directs a public authority (officer, agency, lower court) to do a public or statutory duty; cannot be issued against the President, Governors or private individuals
Prohibition(none given)Higher court tells a lower court or tribunal to stop proceedings beyond its jurisdiction or against natural justice; preventive, before judgment
Certiorari"to be certified"Higher court quashes an order of a lower court or tribunal that acted without jurisdiction or against due process; preventive and corrective
Quo Warranto"by what authority"Inquires into legality of a claim to public office; removes an unlawful holder
  • Memory aid (Writs): Habeas, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo warranto: "Hot Masala Puffs Cost Quite a lot".
  • Shortcut:
    • Prohibition vs Certiorari. Prohibition = before judgment (stop)
    • Certiorari = quash (preventive and corrective)

Supreme Court (Art. 32) versus High Courts (Art. 226)

Writ jurisdiction compared

BasisSupreme Court (Art. 32)High Courts (Art. 226)
Constitutional basisArt. 32; itself a Fundamental RightArt. 226; a constitutional power, not a Fundamental Right
Purpose and scopeEnforces Fundamental Rights onlyFundamental Rights and other legal rights (statutory or common law)
Territorial extentWrits run throughout IndiaNormally within the High Court's territorial limits, may reach outside if the cause of action partly arises within the state
DiscretionCannot refuse a genuine petition for violation of FRsMay refuse if an effective alternative remedy exists
NatureOriginal but not exclusive; citizens may also move High CourtsOriginal and concurrent with the Supreme Court for FRs
Types of writsAll fiveThe same five
Significance"Heart and soul of the Constitution" (Ambedkar)"First line of defence" for citizens' rights

Art. 32 versus Art. 226 in one look

  • Supreme Court:
    • FRs only
    • All India
    • Cannot refuse
    • Itself an FR
  • High Court:
    • FRs plus other legal rights
    • Own territory (may extend)
    • May refuse if alternative remedy
    • Constitutional power only

  • If the person is found to be holding the office without lawful authority, the court can remove him or her.It prevents unlawful occupation of public posts.

10 · Appellate jurisdiction (Arts. 132-136)#

  • The Court is the final court of appeal in constitutional, civil and criminal matters, under Arts. 132 to 136.

Appeals by article

ArticleMatterRule
132ConstitutionalLies from any judgment, decree or final order of a High Court involving a substantial question of law on interpreting the Constitution; only when the High Court certifies
133CivilHigh Court certifies a substantial question of law of general importance needing Supreme Court decision. The old Rs 20,000 money limit was removed by the 30th Amendment Act, 1972
134CriminalSee below
134ACertificateAfter judgment, decree or order the High Court may certify fitness for appeal on a substantial question of law: the gateway for civil and constitutional appeals
136Special leaveDiscretionary; any court or tribunal except military courts
  • Memory aid (132-133-134): Constitution 132, Civil 133, Criminal 134: "Constitution, Civil, Criminal: one, two, three after 131."

Criminal appeals (Art. 134)

  • Appeal lies when the High Court:
    • 1. Reverses an acquittal and sentences the accused to death
    • 2. Withdraws a case from a subordinate court, tries it and convicts
    • 3. Certifies the case fit for appeal
  • In the first two categories appeal is of right: no High Court certificate needed.
  • If the High Court reverses a conviction and acquits, there is no automatic right of appeal.
  • 1970 expansion: Parliament widened criminal appellate jurisdiction. Appeals also lie where the High Court (1) reverses an acquittal and sentences to life imprisonment or at least ten years, or (2) takes over a case, convicts, and sentences to life or ten years or more.

Special Leave to Appeal (Art. 136)

  • Grants special leave from any judgment, decree or order of any court or tribunal in India (except military courts).
  • Used where there is gross injustice or violation of due process.

Four features of special leave

FeatureMeaning
DiscretionaryNot an appeal as of right; the Court decides whether to entertain it
Any stageAgainst a final decision or an interlocutory (interim) order
Any subjectConstitutional, civil, criminal, tax, labour, revenue, professional conduct or other questions of law
Any court or tribunalNot only High Courts (other than military courts)
  • The Court calls it an exceptional, residual power to be used sparingly: grave miscarriage of justice, glaring error of law or question of general public importance.

11 · Advisory jurisdiction (Art. 143)#

  • The President may seek the Court's opinion on legal or constitutional questions of great public importance, making the Court a constitutional adviser to the executive.

Two clauses

ClauseSubjectCourt's duty
143(1)Any question of law or fact of public importance, or a substantial question on interpreting the ConstitutionCourt may tender or may refuse its opinion; opinion not binding but carries high authority and is generally accepted
143(2)Disputes from pre-Constitution treaties, covenants or agreements outside Art. 131Court must tender its opinion (investigation and report)
  • Shortcut: 143. (1) = may, (2) = must. Pre-Constitution disputes are barred from Art. 131, so they come through 143(2).

12 · Court of Record and contempt (Art. 129)#

  • A Court of Record preserves its proceedings and judgments as permanent evidence. Two powers follow. 1. Preservation of records: judgments, orders and acts are recorded for perpetual memory and serve as precedents
  • Accepted as authentic evidence and cannot be questioned in any court
  • They form part of the doctrine of precedent. 2. Power to punish for contempt: any act lowering the Court's dignity or obstructing justice. In 1991 the Court ruled this covers contempt of itself, High Courts, subordinate courts and tribunals across the country

Contempt of Courts Act, 1971

  • Enacted on the recommendations of the H. N. Sanyal Committee (1961).

Two kinds of contempt

  • Civil contempt: Wilful disobedience of a court's order, judgment or undertaking
  • Criminal contempt:
    • Publication or act that scandalises or lowers a court's authority
    • Prejudices or interferes with judicial proceedings
    • Obstructs the administration of justice

Rules under the Act

PointRule
Not contemptFair and accurate reporting, innocent publication, reasonable criticism of judicial acts
PunishmentSimple imprisonment up to six months, or fine up to Rs 2,000, or both
LimitationNo proceedings after one year from the alleged act
ExemptionDoes not apply to Nyaya Panchayats or village courts
  • Memory aid (Contempt numbers): Six months, One year, Two thousand: "Silence Often Tranquil".

13 · Judicial review, review and curative petitions#

  • Power to examine the constitutionality of laws passed by the legislature and executive orders, at the Centre and in the States.
  • An inconsistent law or order is declared ultra vires (beyond power), null and void, and cannot be enforced.
  • It makes the Court guardian of the Constitution, ensures no organ exceeds its authority, and is the cornerstone of constitutional democracy.

Review (Art. 137)

  • The Court may review any judgment or order passed by it, to correct its own errors.
  • Filed within 30 days
  • Heard by the same bench or judge
  • Grounds:
    • new and important evidence
    • error apparent on the face of the record
    • any other sufficient reason
  • The Court may dismiss, or issue notice to the opposite party.

Curative petition

  • Even after a review is dismissed, the Court may reconsider through a curative petition, evolved in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002).

Correcting a judgment

  • Final judgment: delivered by the Court
  • Review petition: Art. 137, within 30 days, same bench!good
  • Dismissed: review fails
  • Curative petition: Hurra 2002, limited grounds!warn

Three members: a Supreme Court judge, a High Court Chief Justice, a distinguished jurist.

Who decides the roster of benches?
The Chief Justice of India.
What is a Constitution Bench?
Five or more judges, for a substantial question on interpreting the Constitution (Art. 145(3)).
Whose disputes fall under Art. 131?
Centre and States, or States among themselves (the latter only if a legal right depends on the question).
Why did Ambedkar call Art. 32 the heart and soul?

Traps#

Trap 1 · The Judges Cases and 'consultation' S.P. Gupta (First Judges Case, 1982) held that consultation is not concurrence, so the executive had primacy. The Second Judges Case (1993) held consultation means concurrence, giving the CJI plus two senior-most judges the lead. The Third Judges Case (1998), an Art 143 reference, made it a five-member collegium and said that if two judges disagree the CJI should not send the name. NJAC came later (2014) and fell in 2015. Rule: First = executive wins; Second = judiciary wins (3 judges); Third = five-member collegium; NJAC = struck down.
Trap 2 · Article 32 versus Article 226 Art 32 is itself a Fundamental Right, enforces Fundamental Rights only, works across India, and the Court cannot refuse a genuine petition. Art 226 is only a constitutional power, covers Fundamental Rights and other legal rights, works mainly within the High Court's territory, and the court may refuse if another remedy exists. Both can issue the same five writs. Rule: Art 32 = right, FR only, nationwide, cannot refuse; Art 226 = power, wider, local, may refuse.
Trap 3 · Article 143(1) versus 143(2) Under 143(1) the President may refer a question of law or fact of public importance and the Court may tender or refuse its opinion. Under 143(2) the reference is about excluded pre-Constitution treaty disputes (outside Art 131) and the Court must tender its opinion. The opinion is not binding. Rule: 143(1) = may refuse; 143(2) = must answer; never binding.
Trap 4 · Removal and review numbers Removal motion needs 100 Lok Sabha or 50 Rajya Sabha members; the inquiry committee has three members (a Supreme Court judge, a High Court Chief Justice, a jurist); passage needs a special majority in both Houses. Qualification is 5 years as High Court judge or 10 years as advocate or distinguished jurist. Review must be filed within 30 days; contempt punishment is up to 6 months or Rs 2,000 fine; no contempt action after one year. Rule: keep the number pairs together: 100/50, 3 members, 5/10 years, 30 days, 6 months/Rs 2,000, 1 year.

Book edition: simple words and memory words#

In simple words

  • India has one single court system shaped like a pyramid: Supreme Court at the top, High Courts in the states, district and subordinate courts below. Higher courts supervise the lower ones.
  • The Supreme Court began on 28 January 1950 (Article 124). It has the Chief Justice of India plus 33 judges, total 34, fixed by the 2019 Act.
  • Judges are appointed by the President through the collegium (CJI and four senior-most judges). The NJAC (99th Amendment, 2014) tried to replace it but was struck down in 2015.
  • A judge keeps office till 65 and can be removed only for proved misbehaviour or incapacity, by a special majority in both Houses. No judge has ever been removed.
  • Its powers: original (Art 131), writ (Art 32), appellate (Arts 132 to 134A, 136), advisory (Art 143), court of record (Art 129), and review and curative powers. Seven safeguards protect its independence.

National Judicial Appointments Commission created by the 99th Amendment (2014), with judiciary and executive members; struck down in 2015
