1 · Minorities · Constitutional Safeguards · Sachar Committee · Ranganath Mishra Commission · Schemes#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2024 (affirmative action benefits not fully reaching underprivileged) · Social Justice handout pp. 29-31. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The Constitution uses "minority" without defining it; the NCM Act 1992 notifies six religious minorities (19.3% in 2011). Safeguards: Articles 14, 15, 25-30, 350A. The Sachar Committee (2006) found Muslims lag behind even SCs/STs on many indicators; the Ranganath Mishra Commission (2007) proposed 15% reservation (10% Muslims, 5% others). The Ministry of Minority Affairs (2006) runs scholarships and skill schemes.

Status AND Challenges OF Minorities

Key point

The Constitution uses the term minority in several articles without defining it. The National Commission for Minorities Act, 1992 notifies six religious minorities: Muslims, Sikhs, Christians, Buddhists, Zoroastrians (Parsis) and Jains.

Key point

Religious minorities form approximately 19.3% of the population.

Constitutional safeguards

  • Article 14: equality before law.
  • Article 15: prohibition of religious discrimination.
  • Article 25: freedom of religion.
  • Article 26: freedom to manage religious affairs.
  • Article 27: freedom from religious taxes.
  • Article 28: freedom from religious instruction in State-funded education.
  • Article 29: protection of cultural and educational rights of minorities.
  • Article 30: right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions.
  • Article 350A: instruction in mother tongue at primary stage.

Number card

  • Articles 25-28 religion
  • 29-30 cultural and educational
  • 350A mother tongue
  • 6 notified minorities
  • 19.3%.

Key challenges

  • Socio-economic backwardness, especially among Muslims, as documented by the Sachar Committee.
  • Communal tensions and riots.
  • Identity preservation under cultural assimilation pressure.
  • Under-representation in police, judiciary, civil services.
  • Educational gaps with high dropout rates.
  • Targeted violence including lynchings and hate crimes.
  • Ghettoisation in urban areas.

Sachar Committee Report (2006)

Key point

Commissioned by PM Manmohan Singh, the Justice Rajinder Sachar Committee evaluated the socio-economic and educational status of Muslims.

Key findings

  • Muslims lag behind even SCs and STs in many development indicators.
  • Female literacy among Muslims at 51.9%, below the national female average of 65.46%.
  • 25% of Muslim children aged 6 to 14 either never attended or dropped out of school.
  • Only 4% undergraduate and 8% postgraduate representation in premier colleges.
  • Female workforce participation among Muslim women at just 25% against 80% Hindu women.
  • Only 27% urban Muslims in regular jobs against 49% upper-caste Hindus.
  • Muslim representation in defence forces at just 4%.
Value addition

Verify: the 25% against 80% female workforce figure and the other Sachar numbers are reproduced exactly as the handout prints them; confirm against the report before quoting in an answer.

Recommendations

  • Equal Opportunity Commission for redressal of discrimination in jobs, education, housing.
  • Better data collection disaggregated by religion.
  • Inclusive policies in Muslim-concentrated districts.
  • Increased representation in public bodies.
  • Special development programmes in minority-dominated areas.

Ranganath Mishra Commission Report (2007)

Key point

The National Commission for Religious and Linguistic Minorities was established in 2004 and headed by Justice Ranganath Mishra.

Key recommendations

  • 15% reservation for minorities in education and government jobs, of which 10% for Muslims and 5% for other minorities.
  • Apply minority reservation in flagship schemes like MGNREGA.
  • Strengthen the Commission for Minority Educational Institutions with wider powers.
  • Set up a National Coordination Committee under RBI for credit access to minorities.
  • Increase budgetary allocation for minority welfare.
  • Periodic assessment of minority schemes.
Way forward / key point

Implementation of these reports remains partial. The 15 Point Programme for Welfare of Minorities captures some of these recommendations.

2 · Lgbtqia+ Community · Nalsa · Navtej Johar · Supriyo · Transgender Persons ACT 2019 · State BEST Practices#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Social Justice handout pp. 31-32. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • LGBTQIA+ identities fall under SOGIESC. The legal arc: NALSA (2014) third gender and self-determination; Naz (2009) reversed by Koushal (2013); Puttaswamy (2017) privacy; Navtej Johar (2018) read down Section 377; Supriyo (2023) no marriage equality. The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act 2019 and schemes like SMILE and Garima Grehs exist, but jobs, schooling, health and family rejection remain serious gaps.

Status, Rights AND Recent Developments

Key point

The LGBTQIA+ community includes Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Transgender, Queer, Intersex, Asexual and other identities under SOGIESC (Sexual Orientation, Gender Identity, Gender Expression and Sex Characteristics).

Key facts

  • Census 2011 recorded around 4.9 lakh transgender persons, though community estimates suggest much higher.
  • Includes hijras, kinnars, aravanis and jogtas as culturally distinct groups.
  • India recognises transgender as the Third Gender.

Legal Turning Points

  1. 2009
    Naz Foundation v. NCT Delhi: decriminalised consensual homosexuality.
  2. 2013
    Reversed in Suresh Kumar Koushal.
  3. 2014
    NALSA v. Union of India: recognised transgender as third gender and affirmed their right to self-determination of gender identity.
  4. 2017
    Puttaswamy judgment: recognised right to privacy as a fundamental right.
  5. 2018
    Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India: read down Section 377 and decriminalised consensual same-sex relations.
  6. 2023
    Supriyo v. Union of India: declined to grant marriage equality but recognised the need for legal protection of queer relationships.

Continuing challenges

  • No legal recognition of same-sex marriage.
  • Lack of inheritance, adoption and insurance rights for queer couples.
  • Workplace discrimination and harassment.
  • Limited healthcare access, especially mental health.
  • Family rejection leading to homelessness.

Transgender Persons Protection Framework

Transgender status and challenges

  • 92% of transgender persons denied jobs due to identity (NHRC 2018).
  • Only 6% formally employed.
  • Nearly 60% never attended school due to harassment.
  • 89% face employment challenges despite qualifications.
  • HIV prevalence at 3.1% among transgenders.
  • Mental health issues including depression and suicidal tendencies are prevalent.
  • High violence including rape and abuse.
  • Lack of gender-neutral toilets and accessible facilities.

State best practices

  • Odisha became the first State to provide food grains, pension, health, education and housing benefits to transgender persons under the BPL category.
  • Karnataka became the first State to provide 1% reservation in government services.
  • Kerala Transgender Policy 2015 is the first comprehensive State policy covering male, female and intersex.
  • Sakaalyam Scheme (Kerala) for vocational training and employment.
  • Tamil Nadu Welfare Board for transgender persons.
Way forward / key point

The path forward requires moving from protection to inclusion, addressing marriage and family rights, ensuring workplace inclusion through quotas and sensitisation, and treating LGBTQIA+ rights as a constitutional and human rights issue, not a social favour.

3 · WAY Ahead FOR Vulnerable Sections IN India · FOUR Parallel Reforms#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2019, 2013, 2023, 2024 (conclusion for any welfare or vulnerable-section answer) · Social Justice handout p. 33. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Welfare schemes carry India's commitment to substantive equality, but the gap between intent and outcome is the biggest challenge. Four parallel reforms: sensitisation, convergence, beneficiary voice and participation, and data-driven targeting. True social justice is when the most marginalised within marginalised groups access opportunity, dignity and voice on equal terms.

WAY Ahead FOR Vulnerable Sections IN India

Key point

Welfare schemes for vulnerable sections represent India's constitutional commitment to substantive equality. Yet the gap between intent and outcome remains the biggest challenge. Performance suffers because of poor awareness, weak implementation, exclusion errors, social attitudes that resist change and inadequate beneficiary participation in scheme design.

Four parallel reforms

  • 1. Sensitisation: to change mindsets of functionaries and citizens beyond just legal documents.
  • 2. Convergence: between line departments, Centre and States and the social capital of communities.
  • 3. Voice and participation: of beneficiaries at every stage of the policy process, from design to social audit.
  • 4. Data-driven targeting: through caste enumeration, MPI and outcome-based monitoring.
Memory aid · S-C-V-D

Sentence (order is free): Smart Citizens Value Dignity = Sensitisation, Convergence, Voice and participation, Data-driven targeting. (own mnemonic)

Way forward / key point

Closing line for answers: true social justice is achieved not when the State simply runs schemes but when the most marginalised within marginalised groups can access opportunity, dignity and voice on equal terms with the rest of society. As Dr Ambedkar said, political democracy must rest on the bedrock of social and economic democracy.
