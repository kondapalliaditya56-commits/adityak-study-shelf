1 · The middle path between king and rebel#

Teacher's board

When rebels establish their rule in an area, the emperor has two options: (1) continue fighting and end the rebellion; (2) lose the territory (T) and let the rebel establish an independent kingdom. The middle path: the rebel accepts to be a Mansabdar of the emperor, accepts the Mughal as emperor, sends tribute to Delhi, agrees to maintain an army / help the emperor militarily. The emperor accepts: dynastic governorship, the territory as the rebel's Watan Jagir, and non-interference in the rebel's rule.

Memory hook

The middle-path bargain: MAT and DoWN.

The rebel lays the MAT; the emperor gives the DoWN pillow; both sleep in peace.

Decode: Rebel gives = Mansabdar status (and accepts the Mughal as emperor), Army (maintains it, helps militarily), Tribute to Delhi. Emperor gives = Dynastic governorship, Watan Jagir, Non-interference.

Why it sticks: MAT is what the rebel offers, DWN is what he gets back; the empire does not shrink.

RebelOpportunity as a Mansabdar at the centre (recall successor states); dynastic governor status; the king accepts the territory as the rebel's Watan Jagir

Except for the Sikhs, who never accepted Mughal symbolic sovereignty (except once, in 1707), this pattern appears among the Jats, Marathas, Rohilkhand, Farrukhabad, Mysore and Rajputs. The teacher's ink ties this to Article 370 status (a state within a state on special terms) and sorts the regional powers: SS (Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad); RS (Marathas, Rohilkhand, Farrukhabad; plus the Sikhs); AAS (Rajputs, Mysore; plus Travancore). The Sikhs [RS] and Travancore [AAS] were not legally in the Mughal empire.

Memory hook

Sorting the regional powers: BAH! / Ma-Ro-Fa / Raj-My.

Successors shout "BAH!"; Rebels chant "Ma-Ro-Fa"; the Autonomous never joined the club.

Decode: SS = Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad (BAH). RS = Marathas, Rohilkhand, Farrukhabad (plus the Sikhs). AAS = Rajputs and Mysore (plus Travancore).

Why it sticks: the Sikhs (RS) and Travancore (AAS) are the two "not legally in the Mughal empire" odd ones; the Sikhs never accepted Mughal symbolic sovereignty, except once in 1707.

2 · Framework: three more Rebel States#

  • Apart from the Marathas, there were three more Rebel States in the 18th century: the Jat Kingdom of Bharatpur in East Rajasthan, and the two Afghan kingdoms, Farrukhabad (est. 1714) and Rohilkhand (crystallised 1737-40).
  • All three used the opportunity created by the weakening Mughal Empire to convert local military power into territorial rule, but none could establish a large, sustainable empire.
Memory hook

Three rebel states, three dates: 14, 21, 37-40.

F comes before R, in the alphabet and in time: Farrukhabad first, Rohilkhand next.

Decode: Farrukhabad = est. 1714 (Bangash Pathans). Rohilkhand = process began 1721, completed 1737-40 (Rohilla Afghans, around Bareilly). The third, the Jat Kingdom of Bharatpur, sits in East Rajasthan south of Delhi.

Why it sticks: the numbers climb 14, 21, then 37-40, just like the alphabet F, R.

3 · The Jat Kingdom (1695-1763)#

3.1 Who were the Jats and why did they resist?

  • The Jats were primarily an agrarian community of North India, with cultivation as their traditional occupation. Their hostility to the Mughal State stretched back to the reign of Jahangir 05-27, when harsh land revenue policies and the Mughal drive to subordinate local autonomy pushed the Zamindars, the traditional leaders of rural society, toward organised resistance.

3.2 How the resistance developed

  • The first organised Jat revolt came in 1669 (teacher's slide: 1669-70), led by the Zamindar Gokla. The torch passed through Rajaram 89-00 to Churaman 95-21, then Badan Singh (1722-55, consolidation), then Surajmal 56-63, each pushing resistance further. Their preferred method was guerrilla warfare: fast, decentralised and hard for a large Mughal army to counter.
  • The Jat Kingdom of Bharatpur, in East Rajasthan, south of Delhi, bordering the Mathura and Agra districts, was formally established by Churaman 95-21 Jat (1695-1721). Like many rebel-state founders (Marathas, Sikhs), Churaman 95-21 turned peasant discontent over high land revenue into political and military power.
Teacher's board

Note the two names marked imp: Churaman 95-21 = establishment of the Jat kingdom; Surajmal 56-63 = the most powerful. The hostility is about LR (high land revenue) and ending local autonomy.

Memory hook

The Jat chain: Great Rebels Created Bharatpur's Stronghold.

G-R-C-B-S = Gokla, Rajaram 89-00, Churaman 95-21, Badan Singh, Surajmal 56-63.

Decode: Gokla 1669-70 (first organised revolt) → Rajaram 89-00 (guerrilla warfare) → Churaman 95-21 (Created the kingdom, Bharatpur) → Badan Singh 1722-55 (Bridge, consolidates) → Surajmal 56-63 (Summit, most powerful). Hostility began with Jahangir 05-27 over LR and local autonomy.

Why it sticks: only the two teacher-marked "imp" names carry a job word: C = Created, S = Summit.

Memory hook

Lohagarh = Loha + garh.

Loha (iron) + garh (fort) = the Iron Fort, Surajmal 56-63's pride.

Decode: Lohagarh Fort, Bharatpur, built by Maharaja Surajmal 56-63; never conquered, even by the British.

Why it sticks: the name itself is the fact.

3.3 Surajmal 56-63: the peak

  • Under Maharaja Surajmal 56-63 the Jat State reached its peak. He built a strong, centralised State and forced the Mughal emperor to formally recognise him as an autonomous ruler (within the Mughal Empire). His reforms:
  • He recruited European soldiers to modernise his military.
  • He introduced a Mughal-style revenue system for administrative efficiency.
  • He removed relatives from court positions to reduce factionalism.
  • At its peak the Jat State stretched from Delhi to Agra and from the Chambal River to the Ganga, across present-day Delhi, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh and Rajasthan.
  • After Surajmal 56-63's death in 1763 the Jat State collapsed.
  • Panipat 1761: Surajmal 56-63 offered help to the Marathas before the Third Battle of Panipat, but the alliance could not be established because of differences on war strategy and tactics. Surajmal 56-63 wanted guerrilla warfare, but the Maratha commander Sadasiv Rao Bhau disagreed. The Jats still sent essential supplies to the Marathas during the war.
Memory hook

Jats at Panipat 1761: "No to the plan, yes to the ration."

Surajmal 56-63 said no to the plan, but his carts still rolled.

Decode: alliance failed over war strategy; Surajmal 56-63 wanted guerrilla warfare, Sadasiv Rao Bhau disagreed; the Jats still sent essential supplies. Contrast: Rohillas and Bangash joined Abdali, the Jats did not.

Why it sticks: guerrilla (his plan) versus supplies (his help), two separate facts in one line.

Memory hook

Surajmal 56-63's three reforms: E-R-K.

Surajmal 56-63 ran his State like an ERK: Europeans in, Revenue copied, Kin out.

Decode: E = recruited European soldiers; R = Mughal-style revenue system; K = removed relatives (kin) from court posts to cut factionalism. Territory: Delhi to Agra, Chambal to Ganga (D-A, C-G).

Why it sticks: Reform 1 matches Mahadji Scindia 68-94's Campoo (modern troops); Reform 3 is the contrast with R vs R infighting.

Teacher's board

On the status row: "the same thing was accepted during Churaman 95-21 Jat" (recognition of an autonomous ruler within the Mughal Empire). On the Panipat row, the Maratha link: Alamgir II 54-59 was a puppet of the Marathas. On Reform 1: Mahadji Scindia 68-94's Campoo = Fauj-i-Hind is the same kind of modernisation (see Chapter 4). On Reform 3: the Mughal family's infighting (R vs R) is the contrast.

3.4 Was the Jat Kingdom a better deal for the peasant?

  • Though the kingdom was the result of peasant revolts, it was no peasant utopia. Land revenue remained high, and real power rested with Zamindars, not with the ryots. The Jat State replicated the same extractive structure it had risen against.
Memory hook

Jat State: peasant revolt, Zamindar rule.

Born in the field, ruled from the haveli: "Kisan ki kranti, zamindar ka raj."

Decode: result of peasant revolts, but no peasant utopia: land revenue stayed high, real power with Zamindars not ryots, same extractive structure it rose against; collapse after Surajmal 56-63 died in 1763.

Why it sticks: the revolt and the rule have different owners.

4 · The Afghans: migration and the rise of Sher Shah 40-45 Suri 40-45#

4.1 Who were the Afghans and what brought them to North India?

  • Afghans had been migrating into North India since the 15th century, driven by poor economic conditions and political instability back home. In the military labour markets of North India they functioned largely as wandering warlords, skilled fighters available to whoever could pay.

4.2 Was there ever a moment when Afghans actually ruled Delhi?

  • The story of Sher Shah 40-45 Suri 40-45 is the story of all these Afghan migrants compressed into one life. His family, the Suris, a clan of the Pashtun tribe, had migrated from the Sur region of Afghanistan to India during the Lodi Sultanate in the late 15th century. His grandfather had received a small jagir in Sasaram (present-day Bihar) in service of the Lodi Sultans.
  • Sher Shah 40-45, born Farid Khan around 1486 in Sasaram, grew up not in Afghanistan but in Bihar, as a second-generation Indian Afghan, a product of the North Indian military labour market. He served under various masters, rising through ability rather than birth.
  • He served as an administrator under the Afghan governor of Bihar, then briefly under the Mughals after Babur 26-30 defeated the Lodis in 1526. During this Mughal service he closely studied Mughal administrative methods, knowledge he later turned against them. He built his power base in Bihar and Bengal and by 1540 had defeated Humayun 30-40, first reign) at the Battle of Kanauj, driving him into exile in Persia and seizing the throne of Delhi.
  • Handout comparison: compare Suri with the Bangash Pathans of Farrukhabad (same Afghan migrant background, same military-labour-market entry), but a very different ceiling on what they could achieve, partly because the political window available to them was much narrower.
Memory hook

Sher Shah 40-45's life in one climb: Sur to Sasaram to Sirhind.

Farid from Sasaram ruled Delhi for five years (40 to 45); Humayun 30-40 returned at the double-five (55).

Decode: Sur clan (Pashtun) migrates in the Lodi period → grandfather's jagir at Sasaram (Bihar) → Farid Khan born c.1486 → serves under Mughals after Babur 26-30 beats the Lodis in 1526 and studies their administration → 1540 Kanauj: beats Humayun 30-40, seizes Delhi → dies 1545 → Suri Interregnum 1540-55 → 1555 Sirhind, Humayun 30-40 retakes Delhi (dies 1556).

Why it sticks: S-S-S (Sur, Sasaram, Sirhind) bookends the story; 40, 45, 55 climb by 5 and 10.

Memory hook

Decode: Suris 1540-55 → Farrukhabad 1714 → Rohilkhand 1737-40 → Abdali 1748-61.

Why it sticks: the order is also chronological; each letter is one Afghan marker on the teacher's board timeline.

5 · The 18th century: a new surge of Afghan migration#

  • The 18th century brought a new surge of Afghan migration as political instability in their homeland intensified. The progressive weakening of the Mughal State created exactly the opportunity these groups had been waiting for. Two Afghan kingdoms emerged: Farrukhabad (today a district in Uttar Pradesh; Bangash Pathans) and Rohilkhand (also called Rampur, with capital Bareilly; Rohilla Afghans).
Teacher's board

Afghan kingdoms within the Mughal Empire (Article 370-type status): Farrukhabad (Bangash Pathans), 1714-43, Md Khan Bangash. Rohilkhand (Rohillas), 1721-48, Ali Md Khan, with 1737-40 boxed as the period when the Marathas and Nadir Shah weakened the Mughals. Mughal interests: territory does not shrink; military and money. Rebels' interest: dynastic rule over some territory. Factionalism (R vs R): Jahandar Shah 12-13 came to power by killing his brother; Farrukhsiyar 13-19 killed Jahandar Shah 12-13. Both kingdoms supported Abdali at Panipat (1761); Awadh annexed both.

6 · Farrukhabad (est. 1714)#

6.1 How did a Bangash Pathan warlord found a kingdom near Agra?

  • The founding of Farrukhabad shows how factional Mughal court politics created vacuums that outsiders could exploit. It was structurally weak from the start, sitting between powerful neighbours, principally Awadh, which preyed on it whenever it could. Its history is best understood as a sequence across three rulers.

6.2 Ahmad Khan Bangash 50-71, 1750 onwards

  • Ahmad Khan fought back and retook Farrukhabad in 1750. In 1751-52 the Marathas, specifically Malhar Rao Holkar and Jayappaji Rao Scindia sent by Peshwa Balaji Baji Rao 40-61, along with Jats under Surajmal 56-63, drove him out. (Note: Awadh had requested the Marathas and Jats in 1751.) As Abdali's invasion progressed, the Marathas signed a treaty in 1752 that allowed him to return and resume rule, at the price of handing half of Farrukhabad to Holkar and Scindia.
  • In 1761 he fought alongside Ahmad Shah Abdali 47-72 at the Third Battle of Panipat. With the Marathas defeated, he recovered his full dominions, including the half ceded in 1752. Once Abdali left India in 1761, Farrukhabad weakened.
  • In 1802 the EIC annexed Farrukhabad. (The "Ceded Provinces" refers to territories Awadh was forced to hand over under the Subsidiary Alliance of 1801; Farrukhabad was merged into the Ceded Provinces after annexation.) The Bangash Pathans joined the 1857 Revolt but were defeated.
Memory hook

Ahmad Khan's see-saw: Win, Lose, Deal, Win, Lose.

W-L-D-W-L: five beats, five dates.

Decode: 1750 Win (retakes Farrukhabad) → 1751-52 Lose (Marathas under Holkar and Scindia plus Jats under Surajmal 56-63, at Awadh's request) → 1752 Deal (treaty: returns, half of Farrukhabad to Holkar and Scindia) → 1761 Win (fights beside Abdali 47-72 at Panipat, recovers full dominions) → 1802 Lose (EIC annexes). Chapter's ruling: the short code for him is inconsistent (50-71 or 50-82), so anchor on 1750 and 1802.

Why it sticks: the story alternates, so you cannot lose track of the turn.

7 · Rohilkhand (est. 1737-40)#

7.1 How Afghan mercenaries became rulers

  • The region that became Rohilkhand was originally called Katehr: western Uttar Pradesh, around Bareilly and Moradabad. In the 17th and early 18th century, as Mughal authority weakened, local Zamindars and regional governors in Katehr hired Afghan mercenaries to settle disputes and enforce authority. These Afghan fighters, called Rohillas, became a dominant armed presence.
  • Note: Rohilkhand was formed by settled Afghan mercenaries consolidating power from within the region, already entrenched in Katehr long before Abdali entered India. The word "Rohilla" comes from "Roh," the Afghan homeland, so Rohilkhand means the land of the Rohillas.
Memory hook

Katehr to Rohilkhand: Roh, Rohilla, Rohil-khand.

Roh is home; Rohilla is the man from Roh; Rohilkhand is his khand (land).

Decode: Roh = the Afghan homeland; Rohillas = the hired Afghan mercenaries of Katehr (around Bareilly and Moradabad, western UP); Katehr was renamed Rohilkhand in 1740.

Why it sticks: the word builds itself: Roh + illa + khand.

7.2 1721: the starting point

  • When Daud Khan, the Afghan commander who had consolidated the mercenary groups, died in 1721, his adopted son and protege Ali Md Khan (1721-48) took over leadership. This is the starting point, not yet an independent State, but the moment one man gained control of a significant armed force.
  • After 1721 he moved methodically: for two decades he consolidated, slowly absorbing lands from the very Zamindars his men had been hired to protect and uniting the scattered Afghan groups under his sole command.

7.3 What finally pushed Ali Md Khan to declare independence?

  • Nadir Shah's devastating invasion of Delhi in 1739. With the Mughal State paralysed, Ali Md Khan stopped paying taxes to Delhi and began ruling Katehr as an independent power, capturing key districts including Bareilly and Moradabad.
  • Handout comparison with Farrukhabad: both Afghan kingdoms used the same window (Mughal paralysis) to convert military presence into territorial control. Md Khan Bangash did it earlier and more formally through Mughal court politics; Ali Md Khan did it through slow ground-level consolidation followed by outright defiance during the 1739 crisis.

7.4 How the Mughals responded

  • Too weak to crush him, the Mughal State fell back on its standard strategy: co-opt what you cannot defeat. In 1740 Emperor Md Shah (1719-48) formally granted Ali Md Khan the title of Subadar. From 1740 Katehr was officially renamed Rohilkhand and functioned as an autonomous State under nominal Mughal sovereignty. (Ali Md Khan took control in 1721 but was officially recognised as Nawab of Rohilkhand by the Mughals in 1740.) The title gave the rebel legitimacy; it gave the Mughals the illusion of control. Both sides understood the fiction.
Memory hook

Ali Md Khan's three A's: Absorb, Absent, Appointed.

21, 39, 40: Absorb the land, go Absent from Delhi's treasury, get Appointed by the emperor.

Decode: Absorb = 1721, after Daud Khan's death his adopted son takes over and spends two decades absorbing lands from the Zamindars; Absent = 1739, Nadir Shah paralyses Delhi, so he stops paying taxes; Appointed = 1740, Emperor Md Shah (19-48) grants the Subadar title, Katehr renamed Rohilkhand.

Why it sticks: the Mughals' rule was "co-opt what you cannot defeat": the title came only after the defiance.

7.5 How Rohilkhand ended

  • The arc is the same as Farrukhabad: rise on Afghan military power, peak during the Abdali alliance, collapse once that external prop was removed.
  • 1761: under Najib-ud-Daula 57-70, Rohilkhand actively supported Abdali at the Third Battle of Panipat.
  • 1761-71: Delhi was effectively under Rohilla influence (the Mughal emperor was in Awadh).
  • 1770: Najib dies; with no Abdali to support since 1761, Rohilkhand weakens.
  • 1771: Mahadji Scindia 68-94 recaptured Delhi and allowed Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II 60-06 to return home (the Red Fort).
  • 1774: Most of Rohilkhand was annexed by Awadh with EIC help, because of the Treaty of Allahabad (1765), which led to a military alliance. The rest, a small part, became the autonomous State of Rampur with capital Bareilly, legally under Awadh, and from 1801 under the EIC when Awadh gave half of Awadh in the Subsidiary Alliance.
Memory hook

Najib-ud-Daula 57-70's slide: 61, 70, 71, 74.

Najib-ud-Daula 57-70 backed Abdali 47-72 (61), died (70), Delhi slipped (71), Rohilkhand fell (74), but Rampur survived.

Decode: 1761 Najib-ud-Daula 57-70 backs Abdali at Panipat; 1761-71 Delhi under Rohilla influence → 1770 Najib dies → 1771 Mahadji Scindia 68-94 retakes Delhi, Shah Alam II 60-06 returns → 1774 Awadh plus EIC annex most of Rohilkhand (Treaty of Allahabad 1765 made them allies); the remainder is Rampur (capital Bareilly), under the EIC from 1801.

Why it sticks: 70 and 71 are neighbours, then 74 closes the book, with the 1765 treaty as the old pact behind it.

8 · Why Abdali came: the immediate trigger#

Handout alert

Prelims 2010. Abdali's immediate reason for the Third Battle of Panipat (1761) was to avenge the expulsion by the Marathas of his son Timur Shah from Lahore. In March 1758 Raghunath Rao 73-74 crossed into Punjab and drove away Timur Shah (Abdali's son and Governor of Punjab and Kashmir); the Marathas appointed Adina Beg Khan as Governor of Punjab for an annual tribute of Rs 75 lakh, and on his death Sabaji Scindia took charge. Sabaji Scindia commanded the Maratha garrisons at Attock and Peshawar (on the Afghan border) and fiercely defended them against Abdali. The Maratha conquest of Punjab from the Afghans was a direct challenge Abdali could not ignore: that was the immediate trigger.

Memory hook

Prelims 2010: Abdali 47-72 came for his son.

Not tribute, not safe passage, not Maratha size: it was the Timur Tears of March 1758.

Decode: Raghunath Rao 73-74 drove Timur Shah (Abdali's son, Governor of Punjab and Kashmir) out of Lahore in March 1758; options (b) Maratha expansion, (c) tribute refusal and (d) safe passage are all wrong; answer (a), the expulsion.

Why it sticks: the trap options are about politics, the real reason is about a family member.

Teacher's board

Link to the previous lecture: the practice of auctioning an office (e.g. a governorship) is what the Marathas did with Punjab (Adina Beg Khan paid Rs 75 lakh a year as tribute). The famous phrase marking Maratha reach: "Attock se Cuttack" (Orissa).

Memory hook

Punjab under the Marathas: Adina pays, Sabaji guards.

A before S: Adina Beg buys the chair, Sabaji stands at the gate.

Decode: Adina Beg Khan = Governor of Punjab for Rs 75 lakh a year (the auctioned office); on his death Sabaji Scindia = in charge, with garrisons at Attock and Peshawar on the Afghan border. "Attock se Cuttack" = Maratha reach (Cuttack = Orissa).

Why it sticks: 75 lakh to buy the post; Attock is the western end of the phrase.

Three states, three directions, one prop.

Memory hooks: chapter in one breath#

Memory hook

The Rebel States, one story from soil to Panipat.

A rebel lays the MAT, the emperor gives the DoWN: that is the middle path.

Soil first: Gokla, Rajaram 89-00, Churaman 95-21 founds Bharatpur, Surajmal 56-63 reaches the summit and dies in 1763.
