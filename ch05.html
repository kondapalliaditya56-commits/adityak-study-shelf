Exam lens#

Prelims hooks

  • Prelims 2010: the immediate reason for Abdali's invasion and the Third Battle of Panipat (1761) was to avenge the Marathas' expulsion of his son Timur Shah from Lahore (March 1758, Raghunath Rao 73-74)
  • The line-up in Punjab: Adina Beg Khan (Governor of Punjab for Rs 75 lakh a year) and Sabaji Scindia (garrisons at Attock and Peshawar)
  • Sher Shah 40-45: Kanauj 1540, Suba-Sarkar-Pargana, Grand Trunk Road, Dak Chowki, Rupiya, Patta and Qubuliat; Suri Interregnum 1540-55; Sirhind 1555

The weak Mughal centre offered rebels a middle path; Jats, Bangash Pathans and Rohillas turned local force into rule, but each stood on an outside prop and fell when it went.

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Delhi's weakness offers rebels a deal (the middle path), so Jats, Bangash Pathans and Rohillas turn local force into rule; but each stands on an outside prop (Abdali, Maratha backing) and falls when the prop is removed, and Abdali's own reason for coming was a family quarrel over Lahore.

  1. Two bad options and a middle path. When rebels establish their rule in an area, the emperor can fight on and end the rebellion, or lose the territory and let the rebel set up an independent kingdom. The middle path: the rebel accepts Mansabdar status, the Mughal as emperor, tribute to Delhi and a duty to maintain an army and help the emperor militarily. The emperor accepts dynastic governorship, the territory as the rebel's Watan Jagir, and non-interference. Both core interests are met; only the Sikhs, who never accepted Mughal symbolic sovereignty (except once, in 1707), stand outside.
    → So who actually took this path?
  2. Three more Rebel States. Apart from the Marathas, the 18th century saw three more: the Jat Kingdom of Bharatpur in East Rajasthan, Farrukhabad (est. 1714, Bangash Pathans) and Rohilkhand (process began 1721, completed 1737-40, Rohilla Afghans). All three turned local military power into territorial rule as the Mughal Empire weakened, yet none built a large, sustainable empire.
    → First, the farmers who fought back.
  3. The Jats rise from the soil. The Jats were an agrarian community, hostile to the Mughals since Jahangir 05-27, when harsh land revenue and the drive to subordinate local autonomy pushed their Zamindars into organised resistance, mainly by guerrilla warfare. The chain runs Gokla (first revolt, 1669), Rajaram 89-00, Churaman 95-21, who formally founds the Jat Kingdom of Bharatpur in 1695, and Badan Singh (1722-55), who consolidates it.
    → Then one ruler takes it to the peak.
  4. Surajmal's peak, and the fall. Surajmal 56-63 builds a strong, centralised State and forces the Mughal emperor to recognise him as autonomous. He recruits European soldiers, introduces a Mughal-style revenue system and removes relatives from court posts; the State stretches from Delhi to Agra and from the Chambal to the Ganga, with the Iron Fort Lohagarh as his pride. At Panipat 1761 he wanted guerrilla warfare, Sadasiv Rao Bhau did not, yet the Jats still sent supplies. After he dies in 1763 the State collapses: it was no peasant utopia, since land revenue stayed high and power stayed with Zamindars.
    → But the Afghans have a story that began much earlier.
  5. The Afghan who ruled Delhi. Afghans had been migrating into North India since the 15th century, driven by poor economy and instability at home, and worked as wandering warlords in its military labour markets. Farid Khan, born around 1486 at Sasaram in Bihar to the Sur clan, rises through ability, serves the Mughals after 1526 and studies their administration. By 1540, as Sher Shah 40-45, he defeats Humayun 30-40 at Kanauj and seizes Delhi.
    → And what does he do with five years?
  6. Five years that mattered (1540-45). Sher Shah 40-45 builds the Suba-Sarkar-Pargana hierarchy (Akbar 56-05 later adopts it), upgrades the Uttarapath into the Grand Trunk Road, sets up the Dak Chowki relay post, issues the standard silver Rupiya and fixes a fair land revenue directly with the peasant, recorded in a Patta and a Qubuliat. After he dies in 1545 the Afghans fragment; the Suri Interregnum (1540-55) ends when Humayun 30-40 retakes Delhi after Sirhind in 1555.
    → In the 18th century a new Afghan surge arrives.
  7. Farrukhabad: a warlord and a flattering name. Md Khan Bangash, a Pathan warlord, backs Farrukhsiyar 13-19 with 12,000 troops and is rewarded with the rank of Nawab and revenue near the Ganga; he founds Farrukhabad in 1714, after first naming a town Mohammadabad after himself, which annoyed the emperor. His son Qaim Khan is killed in 1749 through the scheme of Safdar Jung 39-54, and Farrukhabad is annexed to Awadh and plundered.
    → Then Ahmad Khan comes back.
  8. Ahmad Khan's see-saw. Ahmad Khan Bangash 50-71 retakes Farrukhabad in 1750, is driven out in 1751-52 by Marathas (Holkar and Scindia) and Jats at Awadh's request, and returns by a treaty that hands half of Farrukhabad to Holkar and Scindia. In 1761 he fights beside Abdali 47-72 at Third Panipat and recovers his full dominions. The EIC annexes Farrukhabad in 1802.
    → Rohilkhand rises another way, from the ground.
  9. Rohilkhand: hired guns become rulers. Zamindars of Katehr (around Bareilly and Moradabad) hire Afghan mercenaries, the Rohillas, who grow dominant under Daud Khan. When he dies in 1721 his adopted son Ali Md Khan takes over and spends two decades absorbing lands. In 1739 Nadir Shah paralyses Delhi, so Ali stops paying taxes; in 1740 Md Shah 19-48 co-opts him with the Subadar title and Katehr is renamed Rohilkhand.
    → Like Farrukhabad, it ends with the Abdali prop gone.
  10. The end of Rohilkhand. Najib-ud-Daula 57-70 backs Abdali at Panipat in 1761 and Delhi is under Rohilla influence until 1771, when Mahadji Scindia 68-94 retakes it. Najib dies in 1770; in 1774 Awadh, with EIC help (allies since the Treaty of Allahabad 1765), annexes most of Rohilkhand. A small remainder becomes the State of Rampur, under the EIC from 1801.
    → Why did Abdali come in the first place?
  11. Why Abdali came (Prelims 2010). In March 1758 Raghunath Rao 73-74 crossed into Punjab and drove away Timur Shah, Abdali's son and Governor of Punjab and Kashmir. The Marathas made Adina Beg Khan Governor of Punjab for Rs 75 lakh a year, and later Sabaji Scindia held Attock and Peshawar on the Afghan border. The immediate reason for Third Panipat was to avenge Timur Shah.
    → Now step back: one pattern fits all three.
  12. One pattern, one prop. The Jats rose from an agrarian base upward, Farrukhabad from court politics downward, Rohilkhand from the ground up over two decades. The common rule: each needed external support to peak (Abdali for the Afghan kingdoms, Maratha tactical backing for Surajmal 56-63) and each collapsed once that support was removed: Abdali left in 1761 and Surajmal died in 1763.

In simple words#

  • When rebels set up rule, the emperor chose the middle path:
    • The rebel becomes a Mansabdar and sends tribute and an army
    • The emperor grants dynastic governorship and a Watan Jagir. The Sikhs are the exception (except once, 1707)
  • Three more Rebel States besides the Marathas: the Jats (Bharatpur, from Churaman 95-21 Jat to Surajmal 56-63), Farrukhabad (1714) and Rohilkhand (1721 to 1737-40). The Jat State was no peasant utopia.
  • The Afghans came as wandering warlords
  • Sher Shah 40-45 ruled Delhi for five years (1540-45) and left a model of administrative reform: Suba-Sarkar-Pargana, Grand Trunk Road, Dak Chowki, Rupiya, Patta and Qubuliat
  • Farrukhabad grew from court politics (12,000 troops for Farrukhsiyar 13-19)
  • Rohilkhand grew from the ground up and was co-opted with a Subadar title in 1740. Both backed Abdali at Panipat and were annexed later
  • For Prelims 2010: Abdali came to avenge Timur Shah, expelled from Lahore in March 1758. The common rule: each Rebel State needed an external prop and fell when it was removed.

1 · The middle path between king and rebel#

Story link

Because the Mughals were too weak to crush every rebel, they offered a deal. Ask: what does each side give, and whose core interest is met?

Teacher's board
  • When rebels establish their rule in an area, the emperor has two options: (1) continue fighting and end the rebellion
  • (2) lose the territory (T) and let the rebel establish an independent kingdom.
  • The middle path: the rebel accepts to be a Mansabdar of the emperor, accepts the Mughal as emperor, sends tribute to Delhi, agrees to maintain an army / help the emperor militarily.
  • The emperor accepts: dynastic governorship, the territory as the rebel's Watan Jagir, and non-interference in the rebel's rule.

2 · Framework: three more Rebel States#

Story link

Because the deal was on offer, three more rebel States used it. Ask: which three, and what did none of them manage to build?

  • Apart from the Marathas, there were three more Rebel States in the 18th century: the Jat Kingdom of Bharatpur in East Rajasthan, and the two Afghan kingdoms, Farrukhabad (est. 1714) and Rohilkhand (crystallised 1737-40).
  • All three used the opportunity created by the weakening Mughal Empire to convert local military power into territorial rule, but none could establish a large, sustainable empire.

3 · The Jat Kingdom (1695-1763)#

Story link

Because harsh land revenue and lost autonomy angered the Zamindars, the first rebel State grew from the soil. Ask: which two names did the teacher mark imp, and why?

3.1 Who were the Jats and why did they resist?

  • The Jats were primarily an agrarian community of North India, with cultivation as their traditional occupation.
  • Their hostility to the Mughal State stretched back to the reign of Jahangir 05-27, when harsh land revenue policies and the Mughal drive to subordinate local autonomy pushed the Zamindars, the traditional leaders of rural society, toward organised resistance.

3.2 How the resistance developed

  • The first organised Jat revolt came in 1669 (teacher's slide: 1669-70), led by the Zamindar Gokla.
  • The torch passed through Rajaram 89-00 to Churaman 95-21, then Badan Singh (1722-55, consolidation), then Surajmal 56-63, each pushing resistance further.

3.3 Surajmal 56-63: the peak

  • Under Maharaja Surajmal 56-63 the Jat State reached its peak.
  • He built a strong, centralised State and forced the Mughal emperor to formally recognise him as an autonomous ruler (within the Mughal Empire). His reforms:
  • 1. He recruited European soldiers to modernise his military.
  • 2. He introduced a Mughal-style revenue system for administrative efficiency.
  • 3. He removed relatives from court positions to reduce factionalism.

3.4 Was the Jat Kingdom a better deal for the peasant?

  • Though the kingdom was the result of peasant revolts, it was no peasant utopia.
  • Land revenue remained high, and real power rested with Zamindars, not with the ryots.
  • The Jat State replicated the same extractive structure it had risen against.

4 · The Afghans: migration and the rise of Sher Shah 40-45 Suri 40-45#

Story link

Because the Afghans were soldiers for hire, one of them once ruled Delhi himself. Ask: what did Sher Shah 40-45 learn from the Mughals, and what did he build in five years?

4.1 Who were the Afghans and what brought them to North India?

  • Afghans had been migrating into North India since the 15th century, driven by poor economic conditions and political instability back home.
  • In the military labour markets of North India they functioned largely as wandering warlords, skilled fighters available to whoever could pay.

4.2 Was there ever a moment when Afghans actually ruled Delhi?

  • The story of Sher Shah 40-45 Suri 40-45 is the story of all these Afghan migrants compressed into one life.
  • His family, the Suris, a clan of the Pashtun tribe, had migrated from the Sur region of Afghanistan to India during the Lodi Sultanate in the late 15th century.
  • His grandfather had received a small jagir in Sasaram (present-day Bihar) in service of the Lodi Sultans.
  • Sher Shah 40-45, born Farid Khan around 1486 in Sasaram, grew up not in Afghanistan but in Bihar, as a second-generation Indian Afghan, a product of the North Indian military labour market.
  • He served under various masters, rising through ability rather than birth.
  • He served as an administrator under the Afghan governor of Bihar, then briefly under the Mughals after Babur 26-30 defeated the Lodis in 1526.
  • During this Mughal service he closely studied Mughal administrative methods, knowledge he later turned against them.
  • He built his power base in Bihar and Bengal and by 1540 had defeated Humayun 30-40, first reign) at the Battle of Kanauj, driving him into exile in Persia and seizing the throne of Delhi.
  • Handout comparison: compare Suri with the Bangash Pathans of Farrukhabad (same Afghan migrant background, same military-labour-market entry), but a very different ceiling on what they could achieve, partly because the political window available to them was much narrower.
Teacher's board

5 · The 18th century: a new surge of Afghan migration#

Story link

Because the Mughal State weakened again, a second Afghan wave came. Ask: which two Afghan kingdoms emerged, and which clan founded each?

  • The 18th century brought a new surge of Afghan migration as political instability in their homeland intensified.
  • The progressive weakening of the Mughal State created exactly the opportunity these groups had been waiting for.
  • Two Afghan kingdoms emerged: Farrukhabad (today a district in Uttar Pradesh; Bangash Pathans) and Rohilkhand (also called Rampur, with capital Bareilly; Rohilla Afghans).

6 · Farrukhabad (est. 1714)#

Story link

Because court politics left vacuums, a warlord with troops could buy a title. Ask: what did Md Khan Bangash get, and who swallowed his son's kingdom?

6.1 How did a Bangash Pathan warlord found a kingdom near Agra?

  • The founding of Farrukhabad shows how factional Mughal court politics created vacuums that outsiders could exploit.
  • It was structurally weak from the start, sitting between powerful neighbours, principally Awadh, which preyed on it whenever it could.
  • Its history is best understood as a sequence across three rulers.

    • The textbook example of the Wazir's loyalty being to his own State, not the Emperor he nominally served
    • the highest Mughal Mansab had become a tool for personal and regional ambition.

6.2 Ahmad Khan Bangash 50-71, 1750 onwards

  • Ahmad Khan fought back and retook Farrukhabad in 1750.
  • In 1751-52 the Marathas, specifically Malhar Rao Holkar and Jayappaji Rao Scindia sent by Peshwa Balaji Baji Rao 40-61, along with Jats under Surajmal 56-63, drove him out.
  • (Note: Awadh had requested the Marathas and Jats in 1751.) As Abdali's invasion progressed, the Marathas signed a treaty in 1752 that allowed him to return and resume rule, at the price of handing half of Farrukhabad to Holkar and Scindia.
  • In 1761 he fought alongside Ahmad Shah Abdali 47-72 at the Third Battle of Panipat.
  • With the Marathas defeated, he recovered his full dominions, including the half ceded in 1752.
  • Once Abdali left India in 1761, Farrukhabad weakened.
  • In 1802 the EIC annexed Farrukhabad. (The "Ceded Provinces" refers to territories Awadh was forced to hand over under the Subsidiary Alliance of 1801; Farrukhabad was merged into the Ceded Provinces after annexation.) The Bangash Pathans joined the 1857 Revolt but were defeated.

  • The class material is not consistent about the short form (50-71 or 50-82), so remember the anchor dates: retook Farrukhabad 1750
  • EIC annexed it 1802.

7 · Rohilkhand (est. 1737-40)#

Story link

Because Nadir Shah paralysed Delhi in 1739, a slow ground-level builder could finally declare independence. Ask: how did the Mughals respond to what they could not defeat?

7.1 How Afghan mercenaries became rulers

  • The region that became Rohilkhand was originally called Katehr: western Uttar Pradesh, around Bareilly and Moradabad.
  • In the 17th and early 18th century, as Mughal authority weakened, local Zamindars and regional governors in Katehr hired Afghan mercenaries to settle disputes and enforce authority.
  • These Afghan fighters, called Rohillas, became a dominant armed presence.
  • Note: Rohilkhand was formed by settled Afghan mercenaries consolidating power from within the region, already entrenched in Katehr long before Abdali entered India.
  • The word "Rohilla" comes from "Roh," the Afghan homeland, so Rohilkhand means the land of the Rohillas.

7.2 1721: the starting point

  • When Daud Khan, the Afghan commander who had consolidated the mercenary groups, died in 1721, his adopted son and protege Ali Md Khan (1721-48) took over leadership.
  • This is the starting point, not yet an independent State, but the moment one man gained control of a significant armed force.
  • After 1721 he moved methodically: for two decades he consolidated, slowly absorbing lands from the very Zamindars his men had been hired to protect and uniting the scattered Afghan groups under his sole command.

7.3 What finally pushed Ali Md Khan to declare independence?

  • Nadir Shah's devastating invasion of Delhi in 1739.
  • With the Mughal State paralysed, Ali Md Khan stopped paying taxes to Delhi and began ruling Katehr as an independent power, capturing key districts including Bareilly and Moradabad.
  • Handout comparison with Farrukhabad: both Afghan kingdoms used the same window (Mughal paralysis) to convert military presence into territorial control.
  • Md Khan Bangash did it earlier and more formally through Mughal court politics
  • Ali Md Khan did it through slow ground-level consolidation followed by outright defiance during the 1739 crisis.

7.4 How the Mughals responded

  • Too weak to crush him, the Mughal State fell back on its standard strategy: co-opt what you cannot defeat.
  • In 1740 Emperor Md Shah (1719-48) formally granted Ali Md Khan the title of Subadar.
  • From 1740 Katehr was officially renamed Rohilkhand and functioned as an autonomous State under nominal Mughal sovereignty.
  • (Ali Md Khan took control in 1721 but was officially recognised as Nawab of Rohilkhand by the Mughals in 1740.) The title gave the rebel legitimacy
  • It gave the Mughals the illusion of control. Both sides understood the fiction.

7.5 How Rohilkhand ended

  • The arc is the same as Farrukhabad:
    • Rise on Afghan military power, peak during the Abdali alliance, collapse once that external prop was removed. 1761: under Najib-ud-Daula 57-70, Rohilkhand actively supported Abdali at the Third Battle of Panipat. 1761-71: Delhi was effectively under Rohilla influence (the Mughal emperor was in Awadh). 1770: Najib dies
    • With no Abdali to support since 1761, Rohilkhand weakens. 1771: Mahadji Scindia 68-94 recaptured Delhi and allowed Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II 60-06 to return home (the Red Fort). 1774: Most of Rohilkhand was annexed by Awadh with EIC help, because of the Treaty of Allahabad (1765), which led to a military alliance. The rest, a small part, became the autonomous State of Rampur with capital Bareilly, legally under Awadh, and from 1801 under the EIC when Awadh gave half of Awadh in the Subsidiary Alliance.

8 · Why Abdali came: the immediate trigger#

Story link

Because both Afghan kingdoms backed Abdali at Panipat, we ask why he came at all. Ask: why is 'the expulsion of Timur Shah' the answer and Maratha expansion not?

Handout alert
  • Prelims 2010. Abdali's immediate reason for the Third Battle of Panipat (1761) was to avenge the expulsion by the Marathas of his son Timur Shah from Lahore.
  • In March 1758 Raghunath Rao 73-74 crossed into Punjab and drove away Timur Shah (Abdali's son and Governor of Punjab and Kashmir)
  • The Marathas appointed Adina Beg Khan as Governor of Punjab for an annual tribute of Rs 75 lakh, and on his death Sabaji Scindia took charge
  • Sabaji Scindia commanded the Maratha garrisons at Attock and Peshawar (on the Afghan border) and fiercely defended them against Abdali.
  • The Maratha conquest of Punjab from the Afghans was a direct challenge Abdali could not ignore: that was the immediate trigger.
Teacher's board
  • Link to the previous lecture: the practice of auctioning an office (e.g. a governorship) is what the Marathas did with Punjab (Adina Beg Khan paid Rs 75 lakh a year as tribute).
  • The famous phrase marking Maratha reach: "Attock se Cuttack" (Orissa).

9 · Synthesis: three Rebel States, one pattern#

Story link

Because each story had the same shape, the teacher joins them. Ask: what was the prop in each case, and what happened when it was removed?

The JatsFrom an agrarian base upward: Zamindar-led revolt, then territory, then a reforming ruler (Surajmal 56-63)
FarrukhabadFrom Mughal court politics downward: 12,000 troops for Farrukhsiyar 13-19, then a Nawab title, then a city, then a dynasty
RohilkhandFrom the ground up over two decades: hired mercenaries, then consolidation, then independence in the 1739 Delhi paralysis
The common ruleEach needed external support to peak (Abdali for the Afghan kingdoms, Maratha tactical backing for Surajmal 56-63), and each collapsed once that support was removed

How a Rebel State was built (the pattern)

  • Opportunity: the Mughal Empire weakens, factional court politics or paralysis (1739) creates a vacuum
  • Local military power: peasant Zamindars (Jats), Afghan mercenaries (Rohillas), a warlord with troops (Bangash)
  • The middle path:
    • Accept Mansabdar status, Mughal sovereignty in name, tribute and troops
    • Get dynastic governorship and a Watan Jagir
  • Dependence: each needed external support to peak (Abdali; Maratha tactical backing)
  • Collapse: support removed (1761 Abdali leaves; 1763 Surajmal 56-63 dies), neighbours annex (Awadh, then the EIC)
