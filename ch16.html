Mains hooks

  • Why so many famines in British India? Cash revenue demand, cash crops, less food reserve, plus the 1770 famine as the early case (the teacher's starred prompt)
  • British empire built and sustained by DnR: PS 1793 as the Sustained half (loyal Zamindars below, rightless ryots)
  • Compare PS and Ryotwari on the cultivating class (tenant at will against the over-assessed owner); neither matched the Mughal patta

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: Every system answers five questions (landowner, assessment, settlement, collection, tenants' rights), and every British answer left the ryot worse off than the Mughal patta: auction, then a fixed-forever Zamindar, then an over-assessed ryot, then an unsurveyed Mahal, with cash revenue pushing him to cash crops and famine. Each failure forced the next experiment.

  1. The Mughal patta. The Khudkasht (cultivator) holds customary occupancy rights and pays by a patta, a written record of what he owes, plus abwabs to his Primary Zamindar. The chain runs Khudkasht, Primary Zamindar, Intermediary Zamindar, then the Jagirdar or State, with Amils and Amalguzars checking. The Zamindar is a commission collector (Vatan right, 10%) and not the owner: only the King is legal owner. When the State needs money in a hurry it auctions the collection (Ijaradari).
    → But in 1765 the EIC takes over the collecting.
  2. Diwani, but the old chain (1765-70). The Treaty of Allahabad gives the EIC the Diwani of Bengal, Orissa and Bihar. It keeps the Mughal collection (Orientalism) under Deputy Subadar Muhammad Reza Khan as Diwan. But the patta is ignored and rents run far beyond the record, then back-to-back droughts strike: the Great Bengal Famine of 1770 kills 50 lakh to 1 crore, the demand is doubled and brutality is used. Reza Khan is jailed without trial 1772-74 as scapegoat.
    → By 1772 the EIC is in trouble itself.
  3. Bankrupt (1772). Since 1757 the EIC bought Indian goods with India's own land revenue, so the famine hit its income directly. Nabobs' corruption, the famine and the army and Dual System expenses bring it to the verge of bankruptcy. Parliament lends 1.4 million pounds at 4% on condition of parliamentary oversight, which leads to the Regulating Act 1773.
    → Meanwhile Warren Hastings 73-85 tries to squeeze more.
  4. The auction year (1772). Warren Hastings 73-85, an Orientalist, revives Ijaradari as the Farming System: each district's collection is auctioned to the Revenue Farmer, who works under a European District Collector. The farmers squeeze the ryots to recover their bids, the demand is so high that peasants pay nothing, and revenue does not improve.
    → Now the EIC wants certainty and a loyal landlord class.
  5. Cornwallis 86-93, the Believer (1793). Cornwallis 86-93 comes from a landlord family and believes in English landlordism ('my way or highway'). His Permanent Settlement Act 1793 covers Bengal, Orissa, Bihar, North Madras and Benaras. Four pillars: the Zamindar becomes legal owner; settlement is with him; LR is fixed at 10/11 of the 1789-90 revenue forever (Rs 66 becomes Rs 60); and the Sunset Clause auctions his land if he misses the evening deadline.
    → But the results diverge from the intentions.
  6. What the Permanent Settlement did. Frequent auctions at first (Jotedars and the Zamindar's family buy), later loyal Zamindars (largely on the British side in 1857) and certain revenue. But Zamindars do not invest, become absentee, and the ryot is a tenant at will with the patta ignored. Wellesley 98-05 gives back seizure, imprisonment and rent rises (Regulation VII of 1799), and the Cornwallis 86-93 Code clogs the courts.
    → Ryots revolt and the British have to act.
  7. Patching the ryot's rights (1859 and 1885). The Rent Act 1859 gives occupancy rights after 12 years on the same plot, revives the patta and bans imprisonment, but Zamindars evict at year 11 and keep lathiyals; the Indigo Revolt 1859-63 and the Pabna Agrarian Leagues 1873 follow. After Ripon 80-84's Rent Commission of 1880, the Bengal Tenancy Act 1885 under Dufferin 84-88 counts 12 years across plots in a village and is implemented sincerely.
    → Meanwhile, in the south, Munro rejects the Bengal model.
  8. Munro's Ryotwari (1792-1820). Cornwallis 86-93 and Wellesley 98-05 tried to create big Zamindars in Madras through the Poligars; it failed. Thomas Munro convinced the Court of Directors in 1801: Read had begun in Baramahal in 1792, Munro applied it 1801-07, and as Governor of Madras 1820-27 re-established it. His reasons: Scottish yeoman ideology, no big Zamindars, stressed finances, eliminating Poligars, political stability. The ryot owns, is settled with directly, after a field survey (SCAM-B).
    → But the survey is the hard part.
  9. Over-assessment and the Deccan riots. Phase 1 took 33% to 50% of gross produce and ignored costs: ruin, abandoned land, moneylender debt. Phase 2 used net produce (Ricardian Economic Rent): Pringle 1830 reached 55%, the Wingate Settlement 1835 fixed it for 30 years; in Madras the Torture Commission 1855 led to a 50% cap. Mirasidars wore three hats, Patils were demoted, and when the cotton boom of the US Civil War years (1861-65) collapsed, debts to Marwari and Gujarati moneylenders plus crop failures led to the Bombay Deccan Peasant Riots of 1875.
    → In the north, a third answer is built for Awadh.
  10. Mahalwari (1822-1856). In Awadh half came under British rule in 1801 and the Taluqdars were crushed 1801-22. Holt Mackenzie's recommendations became Regulation VII of 1822: settlement with the Mahal through its Lambardar, a hybrid where the Taluqdar owns in some areas. R.M. Bird's Revised Mahalwari of 1833 assesses at Mahal level, 66% of rental value for 30 years; Dalhousie 48-56 carries it to Punjab and Central India (CUP). Improper surveys and the summary settlement of Awadh in 1856 feed the 1857 revolt.
    → One more pressure squeezed all three systems: cash.
  11. Cash instead of grain. The Mughals took much in kind; the British demanded cash. Ryots, compelled or induced by moneylenders and traders, shifted from food crops to indigo, cotton, opium, jute and sugarcane. Land that grew rice, wheat and millets served British factories (after the Industrial Revolution India became an export market and a source of raw materials). Less grain in reserve fed the famines of the late 19th century.
    → So whichever system, the ryot lost.
  12. Why three systems, and why the ryot lost. Cornwallis 86-93 the landlordist made PS, Munro the yeoman man made Ryotwari, and Mahalwari was a practical compromise: ideology, geography and fiscal pressure. In all three, high demand, faulty surveys, legal vulnerability and predatory moneylending left the cultivating class worse off than under the Mughal patta.

In simple words#

  • Under the Mughals the Khudkasht (the cultivator) held customary rights and a patta (a written record of what he owed)
  • The Zamindar only collected on commission and the King alone was the legal owner
  • From 1765 the EIC held the Diwani:
    • The patta was ignored, droughts struck and the demand was doubled, giving the 1770 famine
    • By 1772 the EIC was bankrupt and tried the Farming System (an auction)
  • Cornwallis 86-93 made the Permanent Settlement 1793:
    • The Zamindar owned the land, paid 10/11 fixed forever and lost it at the Sunset Clause
    • The ryot became a tenant at will
  • Munro answered with Ryotwari (the ryot owns and is settled with directly, after a field survey)
  • Holt Mackenzie and R.M. Bird built Mahalwari (settlement with the Mahal). Both suffered from faulty surveys and over-assessment
  • Cash revenue pushed ryots into cash crops and moneylenders' hands, so food reserves fell and famines followed
  • The Rent Act 1859 and the Bengal Tenancy Act 1885 only partly repaired the ryot's position

0 · The chapter on one board#

Story link

Because the State's duty to its people is economic security in an agrarian economy, every land system is judged on five questions. Ask: who owns, how is it assessed, who settles, who collects, and what do tenants get?

Teacher's board
  • Why this chapter matters for UPSC: the State's duty to its people is economic security, and in a pre-industrial country that means the agrarian economy: agriculture is the big share of GDP, and ryots are the big share of the work profile.
  • Then he gives the five-question frame he repeats for the Mughal system, Ijaradari, PS 1793, Ryotwari and Mahalwari: who is the landowner, how is the revenue assessed, with whom is the settlement made, who collects, and what rights do tenants have.

1 · The Mughal blueprint for land revenue#

Story link

Because the British inherited the Mughal chain, you must know it first: it is the yardstick the later systems fall short of. Ask: where did the Khudkasht's protection come from, and who really owned the land?

  • The Khudkasht was the actual cultivator. He held customary occupancy rights over his land (he could not simply be thrown off).
  • He paid land revenue by the Patta system: a document (patta) recorded the amount he owed, a legal safeguard against arbitrary over-extraction.
  • He also paid abwabs (informal tributes) to his Primary Zamindar.

The Mughal revenue chain: who collected, who paid up

  • Khudkasht (cultivator):
    • Customary occupancy rights
    • Pays LR per his patta
    • pays abwabs to the Primary Zamindar
  • Primary Zamindar: keeps his commission, passes the rest up
  • Intermediary Zamindar: collects, keeps his cut, passes the rest up
  • Jagirdar if the land was a jagir
  • the State if it was khalisa land

  • The handout's text names the supervising State officers as Amils and Amalguzars.
  • The teacher and the Mahalwari section stress that Amlas are different: private employees of Zamindars, not State officers (Section 4.1).
  • Treat "Amlas" in the figure as a label slip and use Amils and Amalguzars for the State's supervisors.
  • Amils and Amalguzars (government officers) supervised the chain so that Zamindars did not under-report collections.
  • Critical point: the Zamindar was not the owner of land.
  • He was a revenue collector on commission (the teacher's board: Vatan right to collect LR and keep a 10% commission).
  • He also had police duties alongside revenue work.
  • Legal private ownership of property was not a Mughal idea
  • It was British, imported from English law
  • Ijaradari: when the State needed money in a hurry it auctioned the right to collect land revenue to the highest bidder.
  • The British would replicate this (Section 2.2).

  • The Khudkasht's right was customary (from custom, not from a law made by the State), recorded in his patta.
  • Land was not a commodity: it changed hands only by conquest or gift, so there was no land market.
  • The market in land develops with the British, from 1793.

2 · Bengal: three experiments#

Bengal: three experiments and the repairs
Story link

Because the EIC held the Diwani from 1765, it had to collect land revenue, and Bengal became the laboratory. Ask: what did each of the three experiments do to the ryot, and why did the next one follow?

2.1 Experiment 1: Dual Government (1765-72)

  • In 1765 the EIC received the Diwani (right to collect land revenue) of Bengal, Orissa and Bihar through the Treaty of Allahabad (ToA 1765) (the treaty itself is in Chapter 10).
  • It did not administer directly. It appointed Deputy Subadar Muhammad Reza Khan as its Diwan.
  • The Mughal collection system continued: the EIC followed Orientalism (Indian traditions are best administered on their own terms).
  • Ideology did not stop greed. In five years:
    • Frantic experiments to squeeze more revenue
    • The Patta system was ignored
    • rents were extracted far beyond what pattas recorded. Then back-to-back droughts.
  • The Great Bengal Famine of 1770 killed between 50 lakh and 1 crore.
  • The EIC doubled the revenue demand in 1770 and used brutality to collect.
  • For the first time some British men acted as whistleblowers, writing to London newspapers.
  • Reza Khan was made a scapegoat: imprisoned without trial on corruption charges, 1772-74.
Teacher's board
  • In the Pre-British era the words "rent" and "revenue" were used interchangeably (his note beside the slide).
  • Underlines: Deputy Subadar, Orientalism, Patta ignored, back-to-back droughts, doubled in 1770, brutality, imprisoned without trial, 1772-74.
  • The famine fits his nature factor and human factor idea from Chapter 10: droughts were nature
  • The doubled demand, ignored pattas and brutality were human

  • Chapter 10's handout says about 1 crore, one-third of Bengal.

  • Since 1757 the EIC had bought Indian goods with India's own land revenue rather than gold from England, so a famine hit its income directly (the Drain logic of Chapter 10).
  • Famine destroyed the agrarian economy and land revenue collapsed.
  • By 1772 the EIC was on the verge of bankruptcy: rampant corruption by its officials (the "Nabobs"), the 1770 famine, and heavy expenses of the army and the Dual System.

Teacher's board
  • Till 1813 the ideology was Orientalism: "Rule India the India way." That is why the EIC kept Mughal collection after 1765 and revived Ijaradari in 1772 (and why Hastings 73-85 is called an Orientalist).
  • The turn away from it follows the Charter Act of 1813 (Chapter 17).
  • Warren Hastings 73-85, himself an Orientalist, revived the Mughal Ijaradari system.

2.3 Primer: tax, property, settlement (taught first in the lecture)

Handout note

  • The handout puts them here as Section 2.3
  • Read them before the pillars of PS 1793.

Rights in land have five layers:

LayerRight
a)The ryot's right to cultivate and occupy his plot
b)The right to fair rents (tenant ryot) or fair land revenue (owner)
c)The landowner's right to sell, lease, rent and mortgage land and receive those proceeds
d)The right to collect taxes (State, or Vatan right holders such as Zamindars)
e)The right to receive tax proceeds and revise tax rates (State; Jagirdars receive but by the State's grant)
Teacher's board
  • Two kinds of rights on his board (Lecture 17, slide 3): rights given by law (made by the State, an organisation, through rules) and customary or traditional rights (from the conventions of a society), e.g. the Vatan rights of Zamindars.
  • (The star note "MIH" and the small "7 ... PPT" beside the law branch are.)
Teacher's board
  • Meaning of words changes, the word stays the same.
  • Zamindar before 1793 = collector of LR, not owner
  • after 1793 = legal landowner (he stars "conditional ownership": the Sunset Clause can take the land)
  • After 1947 "Zamindari abolition" meant abolishing big landownership.
  • Assessment = deciding how much tax is owed, by detailed field survey (soil quality, irrigation, crop type, plot size, rainfall; the board adds frequency of drought or flood and past LR trends) or summarily (a rough estimate).
  • The demand is a fixed absolute amount or a percentage of produce
  • it is permanent or revisable.
  • Settlement = with whom the State negotiates the tax demand, and who is responsible for depositing it.
Teacher's board
  • Settlement = agreement on LR plus the duty to deposit it.
  • His analogy: in GST the shopkeeper deposits a tax that the buyer bears, so the depositor need not be the one who bears the tax.
  • In the land systems the owner always bears the tax, but the settlement may or may not be with the owner:PS 1793: with the landowner (Zamindar)
  • Ryotwari: with the landowner (ryot)
  • Mahalwari 1822: not the landowner (the Mahal, via its Lambardar)
  • Rent is always paid by a tenant to a property owner.
  • Taxes are always borne by the property owner.
  • The State is always the ultimate recipient but may collect through its own officers or non-State intermediaries.
  • In the Farming System of 1772, assessment was by auction and settlement was with the Revenue Farmer.

2.4 Experiment 3: the Permanent Settlement Act, 1793

  • Applied in:Bengal, Orissa, Bihar, North Madras, Benaras.

  • To get cash, ryots were compelled, or induced by moneylenders and traders, to shift from food crops (feed the family, low market price) to cash crops: indigo, cotton, opium, jute, sugarcane.
From cash revenue to famine
  • The higher the demand, the greater the pressure to grow cash crops: a structural transformation.

What are the teacher's five questions for each system?Landowner? Assessment of LR? Settlement with whom? Who collects LR? Rights of tenants?
Who was the Khudkasht?The actual Mughal cultivator with customary occupancy rights; paid LR by his patta and abwabs to the Primary Zamindar.
Was the Mughal Zamindar the owner of land?No: a revenue collector with a commission (the board: Vatan right, 10%); also had police duties. Only the King was legal owner.
What was Ijaradari?Auctioning the right to collect LR to the highest bidder when the State needed money in a hurry; revived in 1772 as the Farming System.
What happened in the Dual Government (1765-72)?The EIC held the Diwani, Reza Khan (Deputy Subadar) was its Diwan, Mughal collection continued (Orientalism), the patta was ignored, drought struck, and the demand was doubled in 1770.
How many died in the Great Bengal Famine 1770?50 lakh to 1 crore in this chapter's handout (about 1 crore, one-third of Bengal, in Chapter 10).
Why was the EIC bankrupt by 1772?Nabobs' corruption, the 1770 famine and army plus Dual System expenses; Parliament lent 1.4 million pounds at 4% on condition of oversight, leading to the Regulating Act 1773.
What was Warren Hastings 73-85's Farming System?Revival of Ijaradari in 1772: district revenue auctioned to the Revenue Farmer under a European DC; peasants ruined and revenue did not improve.
Where was PS 1793 applied?Bengal, Orissa, Bihar, Benaras and North Madras (BOB).
What are the four pillars of PS 1793?Zamindar as owner; settlement with the Zamindar; permanent assessment (10/11 of the 1789-90 revenue, absolute); Sunset Clause.
Show the PS arithmetic.Ryot's produce Rs 100; LR 66%; Rs 66 collected by the Zamindar; PS fixes 10/11 of 66 = Rs 60 forever, whatever the produce; the Zamindar keeps the rest.
Why was the LR fixed so high and as an amount?Inflation would erode a fixed sum (so start high); a fixed amount incentivises the Zamindar to invest and gives the State certainty and less administration.
What was the Sunset Clause?Miss the evening deadline and the Zamindar's land is auctioned to anyone who agrees to pay the required LR (not the land's value).
Why did Cornwallis 86-93 choose Zamindars?English landlordism and agrarian capitalism, hoped investment, food security and revenue, and Zamindar loyalty for political stability; Revenue Farmers were outsiders.
Who bought land at auction after 1793?Jotedars and the Zamindar's own family.
What were the long-term results of PS 1793?Certainty of revenue; but no investment, absentee landlordism and ryots as tenants at will with patta ignored and abwabs continuing; Zamindars loyal in 1857.
What did Regulation VII of 1799 do?Wellesley 98-05 gave Zamindars back the powers to seize tenants' crops and cattle without a court order, to imprison tenants and to raise rents at will.
Why did litigation rise after 1793?The Cornwallis 86-93 Code: Collector stripped of judicial powers, court fees abolished (reimposed 1795), and Vakils with a rigid appeal hierarchy.
What did the Rent Act 1859 provide and fail at?Occupancy rights after more than 12 years on the same plot, patta revived, no imprisonment; but Zamindars evicted tenants just before 12 years and kept armed men.
What did the Bengal Tenancy Act 1885 do?After Ripon 80-84's Rent Commission 1880: defined rights, cut Zamindar powers, gave occupancy over the last plot to those with 12 years across plots in the village, and was implemented sincerely.
Where was Ryotwari applied?Sind, Coorg, Assam, Madras and Bombay (SCAM-B).
Who made Ryotwari and when?Alexander Read (1792) and Thomas Munro (1801-07, Governor of Madras 1820-27); Munro persuaded the Court of Directors in 1801; Elphinstone brought it to Bombay in 1819.
Why did Munro prefer Ryotwari?Scottish yeoman ideal; no big Zamindars in Madras; stressed finances; eliminate Poligars; political stability.
Ryotwari assessment phase 1 and phase 2?Phase 1: 33 to 50% of gross produce (Read and Munro 1792-1820), over-assessment. Phase 2: net produce under Ricardian Economic Rent.
What did Pringle and Wingate do in Bombay?Pringle (1830) applied Economic Rent but ended at 55%; the Wingate Settlement of 1835 assessed fairly for 30 years (Bombay Deccan by 1847).
What fixed Madras and who were the Mirasidars?Torture Commission 1855 led to Madras Rules 1855/1864 (50% of net, 30 years); Mirasidars were rich village revenue officers who oppressed ryots and became big landowners.
What happened to Patils and why the 1875 riots?Patils became salaried officers; moneylender debt plus the post-1865 cotton price collapse (after the US Civil War) caused the Bombay Deccan Peasant Riots of 1875.
Where was Mahalwari applied and who made it?Central Provinces, United Provinces and Punjab (CUP); Holt Mackenzie (Regulation VII of 1822) and R.M. Bird (1833).
What is a Mahal and why is Mahalwari a hybrid?A community at village, estate or district level, represented by a Lambardar; hybrid because the ryot or the Taluqdar is owner depending on the area.
What did Bird's Revised Mahalwari decide?Mahal-level assessment, community splits the share, State may auction ryot lands on shortfall, 66% of rental value for 30 years, subject to revision.
Why did Mahalwari fail?Improper surveys; Taluqdars crushed 1801-22; summary settlement of Awadh 1856; landlessness: fed the 1857 revolt in NWP and Awadh.
How did cash revenue cause famines?British demanded cash; ryots shifted to cash crops (indigo, cotton, opium, jute, sugarcane); less food grain in reserve; famines in the late 19th century.
Why three systems and why did ryots suffer?Ideology (Cornwallis 86-93 landlordism, Munro yeoman), geography and finance; Mahalwari a compromise. Ryots: high demand, faulty surveys, legal vulnerability, predatory moneylending.

Mains: British empire built and sustained by DnR?Skeleton: built by created division (Triple Alliance and Amritsar 1809, Subsidiary Alliance) and used division (Bassein 1802); sustained by PS 1793 and loyal Zamindars; close on loyalty bought cheaply.
