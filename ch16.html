Land Revenue Systems (1765–1885)

Class slides: Lecture 17 (slides 1 to 39) and Lecture 18 (slides 1 to 43)Handout pages 154-165

Bird, North-West) for places where the Zamindari model did not fit. Tracks the five questions the teacher uses for every system (landowner, assessment, settlement, collection, tenants' rights), why ryots suffered in all three, the Rent Act 1859 and Bengal Tenancy Act 1885, and how cash revenue drove the commercialisation of agriculture.

1 · The Mughal blueprint for land revenue#

The Khudkasht was the actual cultivator. He held customary occupancy rights over his land (he could not simply be thrown off). He paid land revenue by the Patta system: a document (patta) recorded the amount he owed, a legal safeguard against arbitrary over-extraction. He also paid abwabs (informal tributes) to his Primary Zamindar.

The Mughal revenue chain: who collected, who paid up

pays LR per his patta; pays abwabs to the Primary Zamindar
Primary Zamindar: keeps his commission, passes the rest up
Intermediary Zamindar: collects, keeps his cut, passes the rest up
Jagirdar if the land was a **jagir
the State if it was khalisa** land
Handout alert

The figure's bottom box says "Amlas" (state-appointed, audit chain). The handout's text names the supervising State officers as Amils and Amalguzars. The teacher and the Mahalwari section stress that Amlas are different: private employees of Zamindars, not State officers (Section 4.1). Treat "Amlas" in the figure as a label slip and use Amils and Amalguzars for the State's supervisors.

  • Amils and Amalguzars (government officers) supervised the chain so that Zamindars did not under-report collections.
  • Critical point: the Zamindar was not the owner of land. He was a revenue collector on commission (the teacher's board: Vatan right to collect LR and keep a 10% commission). He also had police duties alongside revenue work.
  • Legal private ownership of property was not a Mughal idea; it was British, imported from English law.
  • Ijaradari: when the State needed money in a hurry it auctioned the right to collect land revenue to the highest bidder. The British would replicate this (Section 2.2).
Teacher's board

Legal against customary. In the Mughal era only the King was the legal owner and no individual legal property rights existed. The Khudkasht's right was customary (from custom, not from a law made by the State), recorded in his patta. Land was not a commodity: it changed hands only by conquest or gift, so there was no land market. The market in land develops with the British, from 1793. 2 · Bengal: three experiments#

2.1 Experiment 1: Dual Government (1765-72)

  • In 1765 the EIC received the Diwani (right to collect land revenue) of Bengal, Orissa and Bihar through the Treaty of Allahabad (ToA 1765) (the treaty itself is in Chapter 10).
  • It did not administer directly. It appointed Deputy Subadar Muhammad Reza Khan as its Diwan. The Mughal collection system continued: the EIC followed Orientalism (Indian traditions are best administered on their own terms).
  • Ideology did not stop greed. In five years: frantic experiments to squeeze more revenue; the Patta system was ignored; rents were extracted far beyond what pattas recorded. Then back-to-back droughts.
  • The Great Bengal Famine of 1770 killed between 50 lakh and 1 crore. The EIC doubled the revenue demand in 1770 and used brutality to collect. For the first time some British men acted as whistleblowers, writing to London newspapers.
  • Reza Khan was made a scapegoat: imprisoned without trial on corruption charges, 1772-74.
Teacher's board

In the Pre-British era the words "rent" and "revenue" were used interchangeably (his note beside the slide). Underlines: Deputy Subadar, Orientalism, Patta ignored, back-to-back droughts, doubled in 1770, brutality, imprisoned without trial, 1772-74. The famine fits his nature factor and human factor idea from Chapter 10: droughts were nature; the doubled demand, ignored pattas and brutality were human.

Note

The handout here gives the famine toll as 50 lakh to 1 crore. Chapter 10's handout says about 1 crore, one-third of Bengal. Quote the range in this chapter's answers; "about one crore" is the upper end.

Memory hook

Greed, Drought, Double: why 1770 happened.

Decode: Greed (patta ignored, rents beyond the record) + Drought (back-to-back) + Double (revenue demand doubled in 1770, with brutality) = Great Bengal Famine 1770, 50 lakh to 1 crore. Reza Khan jailed 1772-74 as the scapegoat.

Why it sticks: three Ds, one death toll.

  • Since 1757 the EIC had bought Indian goods with India's own land revenue rather than gold from England, so a famine hit its income directly (the Drain logic of Chapter 10).
  • Famine destroyed the agrarian economy and land revenue collapsed. By 1772 the EIC was on the verge of bankruptcy: rampant corruption by its officials (the "Nabobs"), the 1770 famine, and heavy expenses of the army and the Dual System.
  • The EIC asked the British Government for an emergency bailout. Parliament granted 1.4 million pounds at 4% interest, on the strict condition of parliamentary oversight, which led directly to the Regulating Act of 1773 (Chapter 17).
Memory hook

N-F-E, then 1.4 at 4: the bankruptcy of 1772.

Decode: Nabobs' corruption + Famine of 1770 + Expenses (army and Dual System) = bankrupt by 1772. Bailout: 1.4 million pounds at 4%, condition: parliamentary oversight, so the Regulating Act 1773.

Why it sticks: "one-four at four" repeats the 4; the condition is the lock on the loan.

2.2 Experiment 2: the Farming System of Warren Hastings 73-85 (1772)

Teacher's board

Farming System = the English name for Ijaradari; Revenue Farmer = Ijaradar. Warren Hastings 73-85 was Governor of the Bengal Presidency. The ryot's sad face carries three blows in a row: 1757+ high LR, 1770 Great Bengal Famine, 1772 Ijaradari. On the right he lists events of Hastings's years (as written: "To Purandhar 1776 @ Ma", "To Salbai 1782", "HA 61-82: military alliance 1769", "2nd A-My 1780-84"); "To" reads as his shorthand for Treaty, "Ma" for Marathas, "HA" for Haidar Ali 61-82 ([unclear: check your notebook]). They belong to Chapter 12 and Chapter 13.

Teacher's board

Till 1813 the ideology was Orientalism: "Rule India the India way." That is why the EIC kept Mughal collection after 1765 and revived Ijaradari in 1772 (and why Hastings 73-85 is called an Orientalist). The turn away from it follows the Charter Act of 1813 (Chapter 17).

  • Warren Hastings 73-85, himself an Orientalist, revived the Mughal Ijaradari system.
  • The right to collect LR in each district was auctioned to the highest bidder: the Revenue Farmer (so named because, like a farmer, he aimed to extract profit through a contract; not a farmer who tills soil).
  • The Revenue Farmer worked under a European District Collector (DC) (European = English, Scottish or Irish, not English alone).
  • Outcome: farmers squeezed the maximum from ryots to recover their bids and make profit. The demand was often so high that peasants paid nothing: self-defeating. Revenue did not improve; the rural population was ruined.
Memory hook

Bid, Bleed, Bust: the Farming System.

Decode: Revenue Farmers bid at auction (assessment = auction, settlement = Revenue Farmer); they bled the ryots to recover the bid and profit; demand so high peasants paid nothing, so the system went bust. Supervisor: a European DC.

Why it sticks: three verbs, same order as the outcome.

2.3 Primer: tax, property, settlement (taught first in the lecture)

Note

The lecture teaches these concepts before the Mughal system (Lecture 17, slides 5 to 12, 21, 22); the handout puts them here as Section 2.3. Read them before the pillars of PS 1793.

Rights in land have five layers:

LayerRight
a)The ryot's right to cultivate and occupy his plot
b)The right to fair rents (tenant ryot) or fair land revenue (owner)
c)The landowner's right to sell, lease, rent and mortgage land and receive those proceeds
d)The right to collect taxes (State, or Vatan right holders such as Zamindars)
e)The right to receive tax proceeds and revise tax rates (State; Jagirdars receive but by the State's grant)
Teacher's board

Two kinds of rights on his board (Lecture 17, slide 3): rights given by law (made by the State, an organisation, through rules) and customary or traditional rights (from the conventions of a society), e.g. the Vatan rights of Zamindars. (The star note "MIH" and the small "7 ... PPT" beside the law branch are [unclear: check your notebook].)

Memory hook

Rent climbs one step, Tax climbs the next.

Decode: Tenant pays rent to the property owner; the owner pays tax to the State. Rent is borne by the tenant; tax is always borne by the owner. A salary is only for employees.

Why it sticks: a two-rung ladder: Tenant, Owner, State.

Teacher's board

Meaning of words changes, the word stays the same. Zamindar before 1793 = collector of LR, not owner; after 1793 = legal landowner (he stars "conditional ownership": the Sunset Clause can take the land); after 1947 "Zamindari abolition" meant abolishing big landownership.

Memory hook

Zamindar's three lives: Collector, Owner, Abolished.

Decode: before 1793 collector (10% commission), 1793+ legal landowner (conditional), 1947+ the big-landownership class abolished.

Why it sticks: C, O, A in order, with 1793 as the hinge.

Assessment = deciding how much tax is owed, by detailed field survey (soil quality, irrigation, crop type, plot size, rainfall; the board adds frequency of drought or flood and past LR trends) or summarily (a rough estimate). The demand is a fixed absolute amount or a percentage of produce; it is permanent or revisable.

Settlement = with whom the State negotiates the tax demand, and who is responsible for depositing it.

Teacher's board

Settlement = agreement on LR plus the duty to deposit it. His analogy: in GST the shopkeeper deposits a tax that the buyer bears, so the depositor need not be the one who bears the tax. In the land systems the owner always bears the tax, but the settlement may or may not be with the owner: PS 1793: with the landowner (Zamindar); Ryotwari: with the landowner (ryot); Mahalwari 1822: not the landowner (the Mahal, via its Lambardar).

Rent is always paid by a tenant to a property owner. Taxes are always borne by the property owner. The State is always the ultimate recipient but may collect through its own officers or non-State intermediaries. In the Farming System of 1772, assessment was by auction and settlement was with the Revenue Farmer.

Memory hook

SAP: three assessment forks.

Decode: Survey or summary; Absolute amount or percentage; Permanent or revisable. Every system in this chapter picks one side of each fork.

Why it sticks: SAP is what the ryot lost.

2.4 Experiment 3: the Permanent Settlement Act, 1793

Applied in: Bengal, Orissa, Bihar, North Madras, Benaras.

Memory hook

BOB migrated from North Madras to Benaras and Settled Permanently.

Decode: Bengal, Orissa, Bihar, North Madras, Benaras (the handout's own line; the first letters BOB lead the list).

Why it sticks: BOB is a person walking from the south to Benaras and settling.

  • Cornwallis 86-93 (GG of Bengal 1786-93) was sent with one primary assignment: fix the land revenue problem. After several experiments he passed the Permanent Settlement Act of 1793 through the GG(Bengal)'s Council and extended it to Madras on his instructions.
  • By 1801 Thomas Munro convinced the Court of Directors in London to replace it with Ryotwari in Madras, though it survived in North Madras.
Teacher's board

AUTHORITARIANISM under Cornwallis 86-93: "my way or highway." He came from a landlord family and was inspired by how agriculture was done in England (agrarian capitalism); his own words: CW 86-93 = Believer (a believer in English landlordism). The belief explains the model: see Pillar 1 below.

Pillar 1: landownership went to the Zamindar.

  • Zamindars got full proprietary rights for the first time, making them legal owners. Land became a commodity: sold, purchased, leased, rented, mortgaged. Hereditary, transferable within the family.
  • Why? (a) Ideology: belief in English-style landlordism; convert Zamindars into agrarian capitalists like England's landed gentry; security of property would induce investment. (b) Agrarian capitalism would improve food security, boost agricultural trade and grow the EIC's LR income. (c) Loyalty of Zamindars, the traditional leaders of rural society, would buy political stability.
  • Revenue Farmers, though wealthy, were not made landowners: they were not part of the traditional agrarian economy and lacked agricultural expertise.
Teacher's board

Why a big landowner? A rich man invests (irrigation, better equipment and machines as in England's Industrial Revolution of 1750-1850, hired labour), produces for profit, so production rises: food security and government LR income rise. Whom to make big landowner? An already rich person: the Zamindar (ticked; by 1727, 15 Intermediary Zamindars collected 50% of Bengal Suba's LR, the group Murshid Quli Khan (MQK 17-27) had built), a moneylender, or a merchant of urban areas. Why the Zamindar over the rest? He is part of the rural economy and the traditional leader of rural society, and thinks about agriculture 24x7 because his commission depends on production; merchants and moneylenders (Ijaradars) think about margin and interest and are outsiders to the rural economy.

Teacher's board

"PS 1793 = DnR" (Divide and Rule). His general picture: in any setting (company, country, society, politics, family, geopolitics) there are leaders and followers; DnR can pit leader against leader, follower against follower, or favour one tier over the other (L happy, F sad or L sad, F happy, the last with "Bangladesh, Nepal" as examples). The slide does not write which pattern PS 1793 is. Read from Section 2.5, it is leaders (Zamindars) rewarded, masses (ryots) stripped of rights; that is a reading, not his ink.

Memory hook

I-A-L: three reasons for the Zamindar owner.

Decode: Ideology (English landlordism, security induces investment), Agrarian capitalism (food security, trade, EIC revenue), Loyalty (traditional leaders buy political stability). Revenue Farmers were rich but outsiders.

Why it sticks: "I AL" is the name of someone who gave the land and kept the loyalty.

Pillar 2: settlement with the Zamindar. Fewer, bigger counterparties (the teacher writes "LR payers" above "counterparties") made collection simpler. The State negotiated with Zamindars; the Zamindar was responsible for depositing LR.

Pillar 3: assessment was permanent.

  • LR was fixed at 10/11 of the Zamindar's pre-existing revenue (what he had collected from ryots before deducting his commission), with 1789-90 as the base year. Fixed forever, as an absolute annual amount, not a percentage.

2.5 Impact of PS 1793

The results diverged sharply from Cornwallis 86-93's intentions.

  1. Short term: frequent land auctions; the demand was so high that many Zamindars defaulted. Bird introduced the Revised Mahalwari System in the NWP: (a) assessment at the Mahal level, not plot level, respecting the community's collective character; (b) individual contributions decided by the community; (c) if the Mahal's collective LR fell short, the State could auction individual ryots' lands; (d) LR fixed at 66% of the Rental Value (Net Assets, the teacher: net produce) for 30 years, subject to State revision.
  • Dalhousie 48-56 extended Mahalwari to Punjab and Central India.

4.4 Why Mahalwari failed

  • Improper field surveys → unjust land revenue → ryots lose land in State auctions or fall into debt and lose mortgaged land.
  • When Awadh was finally annexed in 1856 a summary settlement was made (revenue without field surveys, because surveys take time): unjust demands and landlessness.
  • Crushing of Taluqdars (1801-22), unjust demands, the summary settlement of 1856 and landlessness of ryots fed directly into the 1857 revolt in the NWP and Awadh (see Chapter 21).

5 · Commercialisation of agriculture#

  • Under the Mughals much of the demand could be met in kind (grain for grain). The British demanded revenue in cash. To get cash, ryots were compelled, or induced by moneylenders and traders, to shift from food crops (feed the family, low market price) to cash crops: indigo, cotton, opium, jute, sugarcane.
  • The higher the demand, the greater the pressure to grow cash crops: a structural transformation. Land that grew rice, wheat and millets now served British factories or global commodity markets.
  • Food security declined: less food grain in reserve when drought or flood struck. This is one of the most important long-term legacies of British land policy and fed directly into the famines of the late 19th century.
Teacher's board

Two Indias. Before the Industrial Revolution (1750-1850) India was a source of finished goods (e.g. finished cotton); after it, India became an export market for British machine-made goods and a source of raw materials (raw cotton to British factories). Britain wanted Indians to grow more cash crops (B) and fewer food crops (A) = commercialisation of agriculture, so a higher chance of famine. His starred Mains prompt: "Why so many famines in British India?" And on slide 20 he adds that the moneylender's role rises along with cash demand ("More role of Rs Lenders", and a circled AND joining cash demand and the shift).

6 · Reading the three systems together#

Why three systems? Ideology, geography and fiscal pressure pulled in different directions. Cornwallis 86-93, an Englishman who believed in landlordism, made the Permanent Settlement. Munro, a Scotsman who believed in the yeoman farmer, made Ryotwari. Mahalwari was a practical compromise where neither clean model fit.

6.2 Common causes of ryot suffering

What united all three was a common outcome: ryots suffered. Whoever the landowner was (Zamindar, ryot, Taluqdar), the mix of high land revenue demands, faulty surveys, legal vulnerability and predatory moneylending left the cultivating class worse off than under the imperfect but customary protections of the Mughal system.
