1 · Anti-defection LAW · Tenth Schedule · Provisions · 91ST Amendment · Issues#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Anti-Defection, Cooperatives, Citizenship module pp. 2-3. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The Tenth Schedule (52nd Amendment, 1985; changed by the 91st Amendment, 2003) disqualifies a legislator who voluntarily gives up party membership, defies the whip, or (for independents and nominated members) joins a party. Only a two-thirds merger is protected. The Speaker decides, subject to judicial review. The law has restricted dissent without really stopping defection.

Anti-defection LAW (tenth Schedule)

Key point

The Anti-Defection Law (ADL) was introduced through the 52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985 and modified by the 91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003. It aims to prevent political defections, corruption and instability arising from legislators changing party loyalties.

Background

  • The Aaya Ram Gaya Ram phenomenon emerged when Haryana MLA Gaya Lal repeatedly changed parties in 1967.
  • The Y. B. Chavan Committee (1969) subsequently examined political defections.

Major Provisions

ProvisionExplanation
Voluntary ResignationA member voluntarily giving up party membership attracts disqualification. Formal resignation is not necessary.
Violation of WhipVoting or abstaining against party directions without prior permission attracts disqualification unless condoned within 15 days.
Independent MemberDisqualified for joining a political party after the election.
Nominated MemberDisqualified for joining a political party after six months of taking their seat.
Merger ExceptionA qualifying merger is protected when at least two-thirds of legislature party members agree to it under Paragraph 4.
Deciding AuthoritySpeaker or Chairman decides disqualification petitions, subject to judicial review.

91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003

  • Removed the one-third split exception, while retaining the two-thirds merger provision.
  • Capped the Union Council of Ministers at 15% of Lok Sabha strength and State Councils at 15% of Assembly strength, subject to a minimum of 12 Ministers in States.
  • Barred legislators disqualified under the Tenth Schedule from becoming Ministers or holding remunerative political posts for the prescribed period.

Number card

  • 52nd Amendment 1985
  • 91st Amendment 2003
  • whip condoned within 15 days
  • nominated member 6 months
  • merger 2/3
  • ministers 15% (min 12 in States).

Issues Regarding Anti-defection LAW

1. Anti-party activities outside the House

  • A legislator may engage in anti-party activities outside the House without formally resigning or defying the whip.
  • The expression voluntarily giving up membership is not precisely defined.
  • Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India (1994): voluntary resignation can be inferred from a legislator's conduct, even without a formal resignation.
  • Halim Committee: recommended clarifying the expression to address anti-party activities outside the House.
  • Issue: distinguishing genuine political dissent from conduct amounting to voluntary resignation remains difficult.

2. Status of expelled members

  • Parties may expel legislators for genuine misconduct or as a form of vendetta politics.
  • G. Viswanathan Case (1996): an expelled member continues to be treated as belonging to the parent political party for the purposes of the Tenth Schedule.
  • Expulsion does not automatically attract disqualification. However, joining another party may do so.

3. Role of the Speaker (quasi-judicial function)

  • Political Bias: the Speaker generally belongs to a political party, creating a potential conflict of interest.
  • Natural Justice: the Speaker must examine evidence and give the legislator an opportunity to explain.
  • Inordinate Delay: the Tenth Schedule prescribes no fixed timeframe for deciding petitions.
  • Floor Management: delays may allow defectors to influence confidence motions or government formation.
  • Rajendra Singh Rana Case (2007): failure by the Speaker to exercise jurisdiction over disqualification petitions is subject to judicial scrutiny.
  • Keisham Meghachandra Singh Case (2020): the Supreme Court held that petitions should ordinarily be decided within three months, except in exceptional circumstances. It also recommended considering an independent tribunal.

4. Merger exception and bulk defections

  • The two-thirds merger provision may allow large groups of legislators to change loyalties without disqualification.
  • It may protect bulk defections while penalising individual dissent.
  • Maharashtra Political Crisis (2022): the Shiv Sena split raised questions about the distinction between a political party and a legislature party.
  • Subhash Desai Case (2023): the Supreme Court held that the political party, not merely its legislature party, controls the appointment of the whip.
  • Issue: a numerical majority within a legislature party does not automatically establish a valid merger or control over the original political party.

5. Resignation from the House

  • Legislators may resign their seats, join another party and contest bye-elections.
  • Such resignations can contribute to government instability.
  • Resignation from the House is legally distinct from voluntarily giving up party membership.
  • Politically motivated resignations may weaken the purpose of the ADL.

6. Weakening legislative dissent

  • Individual Freedom: legislators are compelled to follow party directions even when they disagree.
  • Parliamentary Debate: the Legislature may become a vote-counting exercise, with decisions taken by party leadership.
  • Executive Accountability: governments need to manage party leaders rather than persuade individual MPs.
  • Constituency Interests: representatives may be unable to oppose decisions that harm their constituencies.
  • Voter Accountability: legislators can justify unpopular decisions by referring to party directions.
Value addition

Core paradox: the law has restricted legislative dissent without effectively preventing defections.

2 · Anti-defection LAW · Supreme Court Judgments · Committees · Impact · WAY Forward#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

In simple words
  • The table of judgments runs Kihoto Hollohan (1992, law upheld, Speaker reviewable) to Subhash Desai (2023). Committees (Goswami, Halim, Law Commission 170th, NCRWC) mostly want the President or Governor deciding on ECI advice, a limited whip and a bar on defectors holding office. Defection is at root an ethical deficit, so reform must pair legal fixes with intra-party democracy.

Recommendations OF Committees

Committee / InstitutionRecommendations

Law Commission, 170th Report (1999)Restrict whips to confidence / no-confidence motions, Money Bills and vote of thanks.
NCRWC (2002)Bar defectors from public offices and remunerative political posts. Recommended safeguards against votes cast by defectors to topple governments.
Supreme Court (2020)Consider independent adjudication and ordinarily complete decisions within three months.

Impact OF Anti-defection LAW ON Democratic Functioning

Five effects

  • 1. Legislative Independence: limits the freedom of MPs and MLAs to represent constituency interests independently.
  • 2. Parliamentary Deliberation: weakens genuine debate and strengthens party leadership.
  • 3. Executive Accountability: reduces the Legislature's capacity to hold the Government accountable.
  • 4. Political Instability: defections and strategic resignations continue despite the law.
  • 5. Political Ethics: defection is principally a result of an ethical deficit in political culture. Legal measures alone cannot solve this problem.

WAY Forward

Six steps

  • 1. Independent Adjudication: transfer disqualification decisions to an impartial tribunal or authority.
  • 2. Time-Bound Decisions: codify a reasonable deadline for deciding petitions.
  • 3. Restrict Whip: limit its application mainly to votes affecting government stability.
  • 4. Reform Merger Exception: prevent misuse of the two-thirds provision for opportunistic bulk defections.
  • 5. Intra-Party Democracy: encourage internal consultation and greater freedom of expression within parties.
  • 6. Political Ethics: develop a political culture that discourages inducements and opportunistic defections.
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: defection is principally a result of an ethical deficit in political culture. The ultimate solution lies in stronger intra-party democracy and a political culture that discourages such actions.

3 · Cooperatives IN India · ICA Principles · 97TH Amendment · PART IXB · Rajendra N. SHAH (2021)#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Anti-Defection, Cooperatives, Citizenship module pp. 4-6. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • A cooperative is a jointly owned, democratically controlled enterprise. The 97th Amendment (2011) made forming cooperatives a Fundamental Right (Article 19(1)(c)), added Article 43B and Part IXB (Articles 243ZH-243ZT). In Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021) the Supreme Court struck down Part IXB for State cooperatives (no State ratification, a State subject, Entry 32) but kept it for Multi-State societies.

Cooperatives: Meaning AND ICA Principles

Key point

A cooperative is a voluntary association of persons who come together to meet common economic, social and cultural needs through a jointly owned and democratically controlled enterprise.

Seven ICA principles (International Cooperative Alliance)

  • 1. Voluntary and Open Membership: open to all eligible persons without discrimination.
  • 2. Democratic Member Control: members participate in decision-making, generally following one member, one vote.
  • 3. Member Economic Participation: members contribute to and democratically control the cooperative's capital.
  • 4. Autonomy and Independence: self-governing organisations controlled by their members.
  • 5. Education, Training and Information: for members, elected representatives and employees.
  • 6. Cooperation among Cooperatives: work together to strengthen the cooperative movement.
  • 7. Concern for Community: promote sustainable development of their communities through member-approved policies.
Memory aid · V-D-M-A-E-C-C

Sentence for the ordered list: Very Dedicated Members Always Educate, Cooperate, Care. (own mnemonic)

In the news (Saarthi IAS CA; verify dates)

India (National Cooperative Database, July 2026): 8.53 lakh cooperative societies, of which 6.63 lakh are functional. Major sectors: housing, dairy, credit, agriculture and multipurpose cooperatives. (verify)

Union OF India v. Rajendra N. SHAH (2021)

The Supreme Court examined the validity of the 97th Amendment

  • 1. State Subject: cooperative societies fall under Entry 32 of the State List.
  • 2. State Ratification: an amendment affecting State legislative powers requires ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures under Article 368(2).
  • 3. Constitutional Defect: the 97th Amendment introduced Part IXB without the required State ratification.
  • 4. Judgment: the Court invalidated Part IXB for State cooperatives, while retaining its application to Multi-State Cooperative Societies.
Value addition

Important: the amendment to Article 19(1)(c) and insertion of Article 43B remain valid.

Way forward / key point

Significance: the judgment reaffirmed federalism and State autonomy in cooperative regulation.

4 · Multi-state Cooperative Societies (amendment) ACT 2023 · Ministry OF Cooperation · National Cooperation Policy 2025#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Anti-Defection, Cooperatives, Citizenship module pp. 6-7. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The 2023 Act amended the MSCS Act 2002 to create an Election Authority, an Ombudsman and a Rehabilitation Fund. The Ministry of Cooperation (6 July 2021) runs PACS computerisation, a grain storage plan, the National Cooperative Database, NUCFDC, Tribhuvan Sahkari University and White Revolution 2.0. The National Cooperation Policy (24 July 2025) is a ten-year roadmap with six pillars and targets to 2034.

Multi-state Cooperative Societies (amendment) ACT, 2023

Key point

The Act amended the Multi-State Cooperative Societies Act, 2002 to strengthen elections, governance, audit, accountability and revival.

Major provisions

  • 1. Cooperative Election Authority: conducts and supervises elections to the Boards of Multi-State Cooperative Societies.
  • 2. Cooperative Ombudsman: grievance redressal concerning deposits, accounts, equitable benefits and members' rights.
  • 3. Rehabilitation Fund: the Cooperative Rehabilitation, Reconstruction and Development Fund supports the revival of sick Multi-State Cooperatives. Profitable societies meeting the statutory conditions contribute 1% of net profits or ₹1 crore annually, whichever is lower.
  • 4. Audit and Accountability: stronger audit standards, accounting requirements and concurrent audits for specified societies.
  • 5. Board Representation: two seats for women and one seat for SC/ST members.
  • 6. Conflict of Interest: restricts directors from participating in decisions involving their own or their relatives' interests.
  • 7. Digital Compliance: online registration, filing and compliance through the portal of the Central Registrar of Cooperative Societies (CRCS).
  • 8. Merger: allows specified mergers of State cooperatives into Multi-State Cooperatives, subject to applicable legal requirements.

Significance

  • Strengthens democratic governance through an independent Election Authority.
  • Improves financial accountability and grievance redressal.
  • Encourages professional management and digital compliance.

Issues

  • 1. Centralisation: greater regulatory control may affect autonomy of cooperative institutions.
  • 2. Federal Concerns: expansion of Multi-State Cooperatives may raise concerns over State jurisdiction.
  • 3. Financial Burden: contributions to the Rehabilitation Fund may affect profitable societies.
  • 4. Implementation: new institutions need administrative capacity and effective enforcement.

Ministry OF Cooperation

Key point

Established on 6 July 2021 under the vision of Sahkar se Samriddhi (Prosperity through Cooperation).

Need for a separate Ministry

  • 1. Fragmented Responsibility: cooperative development was handled by different Ministries and departments.
  • 2. Uneven State Laws: rules and institutional capacities vary considerably across States.
  • 3. Sector-Specific Focus: a dedicated Ministry can strengthen rural credit, dairy, agriculture and cooperative-led development.
  • 4. Multi-State Cooperatives: improves central regulation and support.
  • 5. Ease of Doing Business: simplified procedures, digitalisation and professional management.
Major initiativeObjective
PACS ComputerisationDigitises Primary Agricultural Credit Societies and improves service delivery.
World's Largest Grain Storage Plan in Cooperative SectorExpands storage and agricultural infrastructure through cooperatives.
National Cooperative Database (NCD)Creates a unified database of cooperative institutions.
National Urban Cooperative Finance and Development Corporation (NUCFDC)Strengthens urban cooperative banking through an umbrella organisation.

National Cooperation Policy, 2025

Key point

Launched on 24 July 2025, it replaced the 2002 policy and provides a ten-year roadmap for the cooperative sector. (dated, verify)

Six strategic pillars

  • 1. Strengthening the Foundation: improve governance, regulation, autonomy and access to finance.
  • 2. Promoting Vibrancy: a cooperative business ecosystem and higher members' incomes.
  • 3. Making Cooperatives Future Ready: technology adoption and professional management.
  • 4. Promoting Inclusivity: more participation of women, youth, SCs and STs.
  • 5. Entering New Sectors: tourism, insurance, taxi services, green energy and waste management.
  • 6. Expanding Membership: increase geographical reach and membership.

Major targets by 2034

  • Triple the cooperative sector's contribution to GDP.
  • Increase the number of cooperative societies by 30%.
  • Bring 50 crore citizens into the cooperative fold.
  • Develop five Model Cooperative Villages in every tehsil.
  • Expand the cooperative movement to uncovered villages.

Implementation mechanism

  • 1. Implementation Cell: established under the Ministry of Cooperation.
  • 2. National Steering Committee: chaired by the Union Cooperation Minister.
  • 3. Monitoring Framework: supports implementation and Centre-State coordination.

5 · Cooperatives · Significance · Challenges · Federal Concerns · WAY Forward#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Anti-Defection, Cooperatives, Citizenship module pp. 7-8. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Cooperatives give financial inclusion, women's empowerment (Amul, SEWA), farm resilience and democratic citizenship. Their problems are governance failures (political interference, elite capture, weak audits, regulatory overlap), financial weakness and operational gaps. The new Union role raises federal concerns because cooperatives are a State subject.

Significance OF Cooperatives

Six benefits

  • 1. Financial Inclusion: PACS provide affordable rural credit and reduce dependence on moneylenders.
  • 2. Women's Empowerment: Amul and SEWA demonstrate how cooperatives support dairy livelihoods and self-employment.
  • 3. Agricultural Resilience: inputs, credit, storage, processing and marketing services for farmers.
  • 4. Reduction of Inequality: better economic opportunities for small farmers and marginalised communities.
  • 5. Democratic Citizenship: members elect Boards and participate in institutional decision-making.
  • 6. Federal Cooperation: Multi-State Cooperatives promote economic coordination across State boundaries.

Challenges Facing Cooperatives

Governance failures

  • 1. Political Interference: political control over Boards weakens member participation.
  • 2. Elite Capture: powerful members may dominate decisions and resources.
  • 3. Weak Audits: delayed or manipulated audits reduce financial accountability.
  • 4. Regulatory Overlap: divided responsibilities may weaken supervision.
  • Example: the PMC Bank crisis (2019) exposed serious governance and financial oversight failures. The Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020 subsequently strengthened RBI oversight of cooperative banks.

Financial weaknesses

  • 1. Limited Access to Credit: poor collateral and documentation restrict institutional finance.
  • 2. Government Dependence: excessive reliance on grants may weaken autonomy.
  • 3. Mismanagement: weak financial controls increase risks of fraud and losses.
  • 4. Limited Capital: many societies lack resources for expansion and modernisation.

Operational challenges

  • 1. Outdated Management: low professional capacity and limited technical expertise.
  • 2. Manual Records: weak digital adoption increases errors and reduces transparency.
  • 3. Infrastructure Gaps: inadequate storage, processing and marketing facilities.
  • 4. Regional Imbalance: cooperatives are more developed in Western and Southern India than in several Eastern and North-Eastern States.

Federal Concerns WITH THE NEW Cooperative Structure

Six concerns

  • 1. Reduced State Autonomy: uniform audit norms, model bye-laws and digital compliance may limit States' regulatory flexibility.
  • 2. Encroachment on a State Subject: greater Union involvement raises concerns because cooperatives primarily fall under Entry 32, State List.
  • 3. Constitutional Workaround: after Rajendra N. Shah (2021), stronger regulation of Multi-State Cooperatives under Union jurisdiction has raised concerns about indirect central influence.
  • 4. One-Size-Fits-All Approach: uniform reforms may not suit different ecosystems and capacities.
  • 5. Institutional Overlap: growing role of central regulatory institutions may create coordination problems with State Registrars.
  • 6. Political and Federal Tensions: cooperatives are closely linked to rural credit, dairy, sugar and local politics, making them important in Centre-State relations.
Way forward / key point

Central issue: cooperative reforms must balance uniform accountability standards with State autonomy and cooperative federalism.

WAY Forward

Seven steps

  • 1. Democratic Governance: regular Board elections and no political interference.
  • 2. Professional Management: train managers through Tribhuvan Sahkari University and other institutions.
  • 3. Transparent Audits: strengthen accounting systems, audits and member access to financial information.
  • 4. Digitalisation: expand PACS computerisation and digital records.
  • 5. Financial Strength: improve access to institutional finance and strengthen viable business models.
  • 6. Regional Development: support cooperatives in Eastern and North-Eastern States through targeted capacity-building.
  • 7. Federal Consultation: meaningful State participation in cooperative policy and implementation.
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: cooperatives combine economic development with democratic participation. Their success depends on professional management, financial accountability, member control and cooperative federalism.
