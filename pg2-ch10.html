1 · Death Penalty · Rarest OF RARE · Clemency · Remission#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 15-17. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Capital punishment is constitutional but limited to the rarest of rare cases (Bachan Singh 1980, Machhi Singh 1983). Mercy lies with the President (Art 72) and Governor (Art 161). Life imprisonment normally means natural life; remission is regulated by BNSS and Sriharan (2015).

Position AND Arguments

Value addition

Art 21 allows deprivation of life only by a fair, just and reasonable procedure. SC upheld the death penalty but limited it by the Rarest of Rare doctrine.

For

  • deterrence
  • retributive justice
  • collective conscience on heinous crimes

Against

  • irreversible, error kills the innocent
  • dignity: cruel, inhuman
  • reform denied
  • class bias: poor lack lawyers
  • deterrence unproven vs life term
  • Extradition from abolitionist states
  • ICC does not use it

Law Commission

  • 35th Report (1967): retain
  • 262nd Report (2015):
    • Abolish except terrorism and waging war
    • Plus police reform, witness protection, victim compensation

Rarest OF RARE

Note

Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980): upheld death penalty. Life imprisonment is the rule, death the exception; weigh aggravating (exceptional brutality) and mitigating factors (age, background, reform chance); individualised sentencing: crime and criminal.

Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab (1983): five factors

  • Manner (brutality)
  • Motive (moral depravity)
  • Anti-social nature (caste hatred, bride burning)
  • Magnitude (victims, scale)
  • Victim's vulnerability (children, elderly)
  • proposed a Balance Sheet approach
Memory aid · MMAMV

Machhi Singh: Manner · Motive · Anti-social · Magnitude · Vulnerable victim. (own mnemonic)

  1. 1996
    Ravji: over-weighed the crime, ignored the criminal
  2. 2009
    Bariyar: Ravji inconsistent with Bachan Singh; mitigating factors restored
  3. 2012
    Sangeet: criticised the mechanical balance sheet; weigh crime and criminal

Limits of the doctrine

  • subjective terms ("collective conscience")
  • judge-centric: similar crimes, different sentences
  • death sentences reversed on appeal: fairness doubts

Clemency

Provisions

  • Art 72: President can pardon death sentences and use other clemency powers
  • Art 161: Governor can suspend, remit, commute death sentences but cannot grant a full pardon in death cases
  • both act on aid and advice of the Council of Ministers
CaseHeld
Kehar Singh (1989)President may re-examine evidence on mercy petitions; limited judicial review
Epuru Sudhakar (2006)no mala fide, caste, religion, political favouritism or irrelevant considerations
Triveniben (1989)undue delay may justify commutation
Shatrughan Chauhan (2014)unexplained delay and serious mental illness may justify commutation; safeguards on notifying mercy rejection and execution

Clemency Board proposal

  • need: expertise, transparency, timely decisions
  • challenge:
    • Constitutional fit
    • Binding or not
  • indicative six-month period, reasons for delay

Remission AND LIFE Imprisonment

BNSS, 2023

  • S.473 suspension and remission
  • S.474 commutation
  • S.475 minimum 14 years before statutory release in specified life cases
  • S.477 Central concurrence in specified cases
  • Life imprisonment ordinarily means remainder of natural life, not automatic release at 14 years
Note

Union of India v. V. Sriharan (2015) (Rajiv Gandhi case): constitutional clemency is distinct from statutory remission; Central concurrence needed in specified cases; courts may impose life without ordinary remission for a fixed term over 14 years or natural life. Later: SC ordered release of Perarivalan (May 2022) and six others (Nov 2022).

2 · Article 21 · POSH · Jallikattu · Assessment · Article 21A#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 17-19. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Vishakha led to the POSH Act 2013. Reputation is part of Art 21 (Swamy). Jallikattu balanced animal welfare and Art 29(1) culture (2023 upheld State laws). Art 21 is strong but invites overreach and weak implementation. Art 21A (86th Amendment) gives free education for 6 to 14.

Sexual Harassment: Vishakha TO POSH

Note

Vishakha v. State of Rajasthan (1997): PIL on workplace harassment. Violates Arts 14, 15, 19(1)(g), 21; with no law, SC framed the Vishakha Guidelines, which became the base of the POSH Act, 2013.

POSH Act, 2013

  • Internal Committee mandatory for workplaces with 10 or more employees
  • Local Committee where an IC is unavailable (incl. some unorganised sector)
  • Time-bound inquiry
  • Confidentiality
  • concerns: non-compliance, compromised committees, low awareness in informal and rural work

Reputation AND Animal Welfare

Reputation

  • Subramanian Swamy (2016):
    • Reputation is part of dignity under Art 21
    • Criminal defamation upheld
    • Balance with 19(1)(a)

Animals and Jallikattu

  • Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960
  • ** 2011:** bulls added to prohibited performing animals
  • Animal Welfare Board v. A. Nagaraja (2014):
    • Upheld bans on Jallikattu and bullock-cart races
    • Animal dignity
    • Art 51A(g) compassion
    • Shift from anthropocentric to biocentric view
  • Five Freedoms:
    • From hunger and thirst
    • Discomfort
    • Pain, injury, disease
    • Fear and distress
    • Freedom to express normal behaviour
  • 2016 Centre notification allowed traditional bull events
  • 2017 Tamil Nadu law
  • Art 29(1) culture vs animal welfare
Note

Animal Welfare Board v. Union of India (2023): five-judge bench upheld State amendments allowing Jallikattu (TN), Kambala (Karnataka), bullock-cart races (Maharashtra) with safeguards; within State competence; did not decide whether Jallikattu is an essential part of Tamil culture; no violation of Arts 14, 21, 51A(g).

Critical Assessment OF Article 21

Strengths

  • from procedure to dignity
  • positive obligations on State
  • protects the vulnerable through PIL
  • a living document

Concerns

  • judicial overreach
  • weak implementation (resources, governance)
  • balancing liberty and public interest

Article 21A: Right TO Education

Provision and features

  • "State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged six to fourteen" as the State may by law determine
  • 86th Amendment Act, 2002
  • Operational 1 April 2010 with the RTE Act, 2009
  • Needs a statute
  • NCRWC idea: extend to 6-18 for SCs, STs, girls (handout note)
  • Art 45 (DPSP, not justiciable) now directs early childhood care and education for under-6s
  • 21A covers elementary education

3 · Article 22 · Arrest Safeguards · Preventive Detention#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 19-22. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Art 22(1)-(2) protect those arrested under ordinary law (grounds, lawyer, 24-hour Magistrate). Art 22(4)-(7) govern preventive detention: Advisory Board after three months, grounds and representation. Know the laws, the 44th Amendment never in force, and the 2026 cases.

Ordinary Arrest: 22(1) AND 22(2)

Rights

  • information of grounds
  • lawyer of choice
  • produced before nearest Magistrate within 24 hours (excluding travel time)
  • custody beyond 24 hours needs a Magistrate's authority
Memory aid · ILPM

Four rights: Informed · Lawyer · Produced within 24 hours · Magistrate authority beyond. (own mnemonic)

BNSS

  • S.47 grounds and bail right
  • S.48 inform relative or friend
  • S.58 no detention beyond 24 hours
  1. 1994
    Joginder Kumar: arrest not routine, must be justified
  2. 1997
    D. K. Basu: arrest safeguards
  3. 2014
    Arnesh Kumar: police must justify arrest, esp. offences up to seven years
  4. 2023
    Pankaj Bansal: grounds in writing under PMLA
Note

Jaskaran Jeet Singh Deol v. State of Punjab (2026) (verify): grounds in writing, in a language the accused understands; violation of 22(1) makes the arrest unlawful and entitles release; re-arrest for the same offence needs prior judicial approval with reasons; superior oversight, another officer to investigate, departmental action; High Courts may award compensation.

Value addition

Art 22(3) exceptions: enemy aliens (not every foreigner) and persons under preventive detention laws.

Preventive Detention

PunitivePreventive
Purposepunish after convictionprevent anticipated harm
Basisoffence provedmaterial suggesting prejudicial activity
Procedureafter criminal trialunder preventive detention laws
Safeguardsordinary criminal procedurespecial constitutional safeguards

Legislative powers

  • Union List Entry 9: defence, foreign affairs, security of India
  • Concurrent List Entry 3: security of a State, public order, essential supplies and services

Safeguards 22(4)-(7)

  • 22(4): no detention beyond three months without Advisory Board (persons who are, were or are qualified to be High Court judges) finding sufficient cause
  • 44th Amendment (1978) would cut it to two months but was never brought into force
  • Text still says three
  • 22(5):
    • Grounds communicated as soon as may be
    • Earliest chance of representation
    • 22(6): facts may be withheld in public interest
  • 22(7): Parliament may fix cases beyond three months without a Board, maximum periods, Board procedure
Memory aid · ABRP

Safeguards: Advisory Board (3 months) · Basis (grounds) communicated · Representation · Parliament fixes limits. (own mnemonic)

Issues and NCRWC

  • no fixed time for giving grounds
  • broad public-interest secrecy
  • Board of merely "qualified" persons
  • subjective satisfaction
  • NCRWC:
    • Only sitting High Court judges on Boards
    • Cap at six months
    • Fixed time limits for grounds
LawStatus
Preventive Detention Act, 1950expired 1969
MISA, 1971repealed 1978
COFEPOSA, 1974in force
National Security Act, 1980in force
PBM and Essential Commodities Act, 1980in force
Value addition

UAPA (s.43D(5) bail limits) and PMLA raise arrest and bail concerns but are not ordinary preventive detention laws under 22(4)-(7).

Judicial safeguards

  • Rekha (2011):
    • Not a routine police shortcut
    • Ordinary law first
    • Real need shown even if already in jail
  • Ameena Begum (2023):
    • Public order differs from law and order
    • Relevant material with rational probative value
  • Mulla Afroz v. Union of India (2026) (verify):
    • Under NSA, a police confession alone cannot justify detention
    • Detention must address future threats, not punish past offences
    • Ordinary process cannot be bypassed
    • Order quashed, ₹10 lakh costs on Uttar Pradesh

Critique

  • liberty lost without conviction
  • arbitrary use against opponents and activists
  • no Art 22(1)-(2) protection
  • Art 21 still applies
  • must not bypass ordinary criminal justice

4 · Right Against Exploitation · Articles 23-24 · SEX WORK · Child Labour#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Fundamental Rights 2 handout pp. 22-26. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Arts 23 and 24 bind both the State and private persons and cover citizens and non-citizens. PUDR and Bandhua Mukti Morcha widened forced labour. Buddhadev Karmaskar (2022) gave sex workers equal protection. The 2016 child-labour law bans work under 14, with family and entertainment exceptions.

Article 23

Value addition

Arts 23-24 are available to citizens and non-citizens and are enforceable against the State and private individuals.

Provision

  • 23(1):
    • Bans traffic in human beings, begar and similar forced labour
    • Punishable by law
  • 23(2): State may impose compulsory service for public purposes without discrimination only on religion, race, caste or class
  • The Article is a prohibition
  • Offences come from statutes

Judicial interpretation

  • Begar:
    • Unpaid service by coercion
    • Forced labour: work against free will through physical, economic or other pressure
  • PUDR v. Union of India (1982):
    • Pay below minimum wage can be forced labour
    • Economic compulsion counts
    • Protects workers against public and private employers
  • Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984):
    • Bonded labour
    • State must identify, release, rehabilitate

Laws

  • Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976
  • Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act (ITPA), 1956
  • BNS, 2023 on trafficking and exploitation

Trafficking AND SEX WORK

ITPA 1956

  • prohibits brothel keeping, living on earnings of another's prostitution, procuring or inducing, detaining persons, public solicitation
  • Voluntary adult sex work is not itself an offence
  • Trafficking, exploitation, brothel activity and solicitation remain punishable
  • trafficking (recruitment, transport, harbouring, receipt by prohibited means for exploitation) must be distinguished from voluntary adult sex work

For legalisation / decriminalisation

  • better regulation
  • spot trafficking and coercion
  • lower STD and HIV risk
  • labour rights
  • access to justice against violence
  • remove middlemen
  • better living conditions

Against

  • may raise demand and trafficking
  • commodification of women
  • ethical concerns
  • prefer rehabilitation and livelihoods
  • poverty limits free choice
  • patriarchal police attitudes

Models cited

  • Germany, Denmark, New Zealand use different regulation or decriminalisation models
Note

Budhadev Karmaskar v. State of West Bengal (2022): sex workers are entitled to equal protection of law; Art 21 dignity covers them and their children. Directions: police must not interfere with consenting adult sex work; take sexual-assault complaints seriously; no physical or verbal abuse; protect identity in raids; children not separated from mothers merely for occupation. Trafficking offences remain.

Article 24: Child Labour

Provision

  • "No child below fourteen shall be employed in any factory or mine or other hazardous employment"
  • Applies to public and private employers

Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986

  • original:
    • Banned children only in listed hazardous work
    • Permitted other work under rules: clashed with Art 21A
  • 2016 amendment: bans work under 14 in all occupations, with exceptions: family enterprise (non-hazardous, after school or in vacations) and the audio-visual entertainment industry (artists in ads, films, TV, sports; not circus)
  • new category Adolescents (14-18): barred from mines, inflammable substances or explosives, specified hazardous processes

Issues

  • family exception hard to monitor
  • conflict with education where families need earnings
  • adolescents exposed to dangerous work outside the schedule
  • poverty: ban alone fails
  • enforcement: inspections, rehabilitation and schooling of rescued children
Way forward / key point

21A vs 24: 21A gives the right to education (6-14); 24 protects against hazardous work (under 14); the 2016 Act extends the statutory ban to all occupations with exceptions.
