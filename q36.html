2020PYQ 3(Prelims 2020)

One common agreement between Gandhism and Marxism is:

Answer: (A)

(A) ✓ — Per of the record, both Gandhism and Marxism converge on a "final goal of a stateless society". Marx: classless-stateless society emerges after Dictatorship of the Proletariat withers away. Gandhi: Ram Rajya / Sarvodaya where individuals govern themselves through moral self-rule (Sarva Seva Sangh / Gram Swaraj), needing no coercive State. The means and the path differ sharply, but the ultimate end-state both envision is one without organised state coercion.

Wrong options:

(B) ✗ — Per of the record, "Class struggle" is the Marxist engine of history but Gandhi explicitly rejected it. Gandhi promoted the Trusteeship theory — asking the rich and poor to cooperate peacefully like one family — directly opposing the Marxist call for the oppressed to violently overthrow capitalists. Hence "class struggle" is NOT a Gandhism-Marxism common point.

(C) ✗ — Abolition of private property is the Marxist plank Gandhi REFUSED: his Trusteeship asked owners to hold wealth for society — property preserved but moralised.

(D) ✗ — Economic determinism is Marx's engine of history; Gandhi's springs were moral — self-rule of the soul. The one convergence is the FINAL goal of a stateless society.

FYQ 1 Mains-FYQ · 15 marks · 250 words

"Gandhi stood almost alone in 20th-century Indian thought in rejecting the Industrial Revolution." Discuss.

Demand of the question: "Discuss" + "almost alone" demands both (a) the substance of Gandhi's rejection and (b) its uniqueness against the consensus position. The body must establish the contrast — name the Indian thinkers who were pro-IR, then explain Gandhi's reasons for the opposite view.

Suggested intro (~25 words): Across the 20th-century Indian intellectual spectrum — Moderates, Extremists, Nehru, Ambedkar — the Industrial Revolution was the path to modernity and national strength. Gandhi was the sole significant dissenter. Body guidance — 4 themes:

1. The consensus around IR (see 'Was Gandhi Anti-Modern?'). Naoroji, Tilak, Aurobindo, Nehru, Ambedkar — all backed industrialisation, differing only on tactics (state-led vs free-market). Even Marx held IR was the necessary precursor to socialist transformation.

2. Gandhi's substantive rejection (see 'Was Gandhi Anti-Modern?' and 'The Economic Vision — Village Self-Sufficiency'). IR centralised production, destroyed village handicrafts, created urban concentrations that uprooted moral self-sufficiency. Gandhi's village economy preserved the conditions for individual moral responsibility — which industrial wage-labour systematically erodes.

3. The deeper reasoning — moral, not just economic (see 'Stateless Society and Enlightened Anarchism' and 'The Economic Vision — Village Self-Sufficiency'). Gandhi's anti-IR position was downstream of his vision of the human person as fundamentally moral. Industrial society makes morality optional; village society makes it structural. Sarvodaya — welfare of all — required the village.

4. The unique uniqueness — even Mao broke ranks ('Was Gandhi Anti-Modern?' ◆). Mao's Great Leap Forward (1958-62) eventually turned anti-IR — but in Gandhi's lifetime, no major political thinker held this position. Gandhi was first; Mao followed two decades later.

Closing verdict: Gandhi's anti-IR position was not Luddite nostalgia. It was a coherent moral-political analysis — village = moral autonomy; industry = wage-labour dependency. The position cost him allies within the INM, but it shaped Khadi, Trusteeship, & Sarvodaya — the trio that defined the Gandhian alternative to both capitalism and socialism.

Revise: 'Was Gandhi Anti-Modern?' + 'Stateless Society + Enlightened Anarchism' + 'Economic Vision — Village Self-Sufficiency'.

FYQ 2 Mains-FYQ · 10 marks · 150 words Examine why Mahatma Gandhi deliberately kept

"Swaraj" undefined as a political goal, & the strategic effect of that choice.

Demand of the question: "Examine" + "deliberately undefined" = explain the choice as a strategic decision, not a vagueness. The body must show what the strategic effect was — & the cost.

Suggested intro (~25 words): Gandhi never precisely defined "Swaraj" — not because he didn't know what it meant, but because the strategic value of leaving it open exceeded the cost of leaving it ambiguous. Body guidance — 3 themes (see 'The Inclusive Approach — Why Swaraj Was Deliberately Never Defined'):

1. The choice was deliberate (see 'The Inclusive Approach — Why Swaraj Was Deliberately Never Defined'). Gandhi was fully aware of India's caste, religious, linguistic and class divisions. A precise political definition of Swaraj would have alienated at least one major bloc — depending on whether it was "Hindu Raj", "republican democracy", "village federation" or "stateless society".

2. The strategic effect. The ambiguity allowed every community to read its own aspirations into "Swaraj" — Hindus saw cultural revival; Muslims initially saw constitutional protection; peasants saw zamindari abolition; workers saw labour rights. Inclusive mobilisation depended on this openness.

3. The cost — Jinnah's critique (see 'The Inclusive Approach — Why Swaraj Was Deliberately Never Defined'). Jinnah's sharpest criticism was that "Swaraj" was a Hindu cultural concept dressed as a national one, & that Muslims could never be safe inside its undefined borders. Gandhi's counter was that the openness was the source of inclusivity — but Jinnah's critique drove the 1940 Pakistan demand. The ambiguity that enabled inclusive mobilisation in the 1920s-30s could not bear the weight of partition negotiation in the 1940s.

Closing verdict: Deliberate ambiguity built the mass movement; the same ambiguity could not carry the constitutional moment. Both effects came from the same choice.

Revise: 'The Inclusive Approach — Why Swaraj Was Deliberately Never Defined'.
