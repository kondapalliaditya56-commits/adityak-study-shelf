2001PYQ 1Tipu Sultan's modern embassies

Who among the following Indian rulers established embassies in foreign countries on modern lines?

Answer: (D)

Why (d) is correct: Tipu82-99 set up modern embassies in France, Turkey, & Egypt — alongside other administrative innovations such as a new coinage and the Mauludi Luniselar Calendar. You will find this in 'What made Tipu82-99 a different kind of ruler — and why did he still lose?' (inside the ⚠ Alert block). The chapter's 'What made Tipu82-99 a different kind of ruler — and why did he still lose?' section also covers Tipu82-99's iron-cased rocket artillery (later studied by William Congreve), his government-owned trading company (30 trading centres including Muscat, Oman), his sanad request to the Khalifa, and his French alliance — Tree of Liberty + Jacobin Club membership — which made him an ideological as well as military adversary in British eyes.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • (a) Haider Ali61-82 built the modern Mysore army on a French model (with Risalas) and fought the EIC in the early Anglo-Mysore Wars, but he did not set up modern embassies in foreign countries.
  • (b) Mir Qasim was a Nawab of Bengal who fought the EIC at the Battle of Buxar (1764) — covered elsewhere in your notes — and did not establish foreign embassies.
  • (c) Shah Alam II60-06 was the Mughal Emperor whose authority was reduced after Buxar; foreign embassies were not part of his programme.

2017PYQ 1 Fragmented polity in mid-18th centuryMains 2017 · 10 marks · 150 words

"Clarify how mid-eighteenth-century India was beset with the spectre of a fragmented polity."

Demand of the question: Show how India was a fragmented polity by the mid-eighteenth century. The verb " clarify " means make the case clearly with evidence — give multiple regional examples. The phrase " spectre of a fragmented polity " tells you the question accepts the premise as a recognised condition; your job is to show how, not to debate whether it was fragmented.

Suggested intro (~25 words): Mid-eighteenth-century India was indeed beset by the spectre of a fragmented polity — Mughal authority had broken apart into many separate regional powers across the subcontinent.

Guiding themes (pick 4–5 for a 150-word answer):

1. Successor States — Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad (see Chapter 3, Successor States).

2. Maratha Confederacy (see Chapter 4, Rise of Marathas).

3. Jats + Afghans (see Chapter 5, Jats and Afghans).

4. Sikh State — Banda Bahadur08-15 → Misl Confederacy → Ranjit Singh01-39 (see Chapter 6 — 'How did Banda Bahadur08-15 establish the first Sikh State — and why did it collapse?' + 'What kept the Sikh State alive after Banda Bahadur08-15's defeat?' + 'How did Ranjit Singh01-39 unify the Misls and build a powerful Sikh State?' + 'What were the terms of the 1809 Treaty of Amritsar with the EIC?').

5. Rajputs post-Aurangzeb58-07.

6. Travancore — Marthanda Varma29-58.

7. Mysore — Haider Ali61-82 + Tipu82-99.

Suggested conclusion (~20 words): The fragmented polity that haunted mid-eighteenth-century India was already a settled reality — the EIC's later expansion only used this break-up; it did not cause it.

2014PYQ 2 Why were so many empire-shaking battles fought at Panipat?Mains 2014 · 10 marks · 150 words

"The third battle of Panipat was fought in 1761. Why were so many empire-shaking battles fought at Panipat?"

Demand of the question: Explain why this one place — Panipat — kept becoming the site of empire-deciding battles. You must cover two parts: (a) the geographic reason (why this terrain), and (b) the historical pattern (multiple battles, each empire-defining). The verb " why " calls for causal explanation, not narrative description. Keep the 1761 anchor in mind — your answer must connect to the Third Battle of Panipat as the prompt's reference point.

Suggested intro (~25 words): Many empire-shaking battles were fought at Panipat because Panipat was India's natural Delhi gateway from the northwest — and whoever won there won the throne, as the third battle of Panipat in 1761 again confirmed.

Guiding themes (pick 4–5 for a 150-word answer):

1. Geography — Delhi gateway (see Chapter 4, Marathas; see Chapter 5, Afghans).

2. 1st Panipat 1526 — Babur26-30 defeats Ibrahim Lodi → birth of the Mughal Empire (see Chapter 1, Mughal Background).

3. 2nd Panipat 1556 — Akbar56-05 vs Hemu → consolidation of Mughal rule (see Chapter 1, Mughal Background).

4. 3rd Panipat 1761 — Ahmad Shah Abdali vs Marathas → peak-and-break of Maratha imperial expansion (see Chapter 4, Marathas; see Chapter 5, Afghans).

5. Sikh-side resistance to Afghan invasions — Ranjit Singh01-39 vs Zaman Shah 1799 (see Chapter 6 — 'How did Ranjit Singh01-39 unify the Misls and build a powerful Sikh State?').

Suggested conclusion (~20 words): The empire-shaking battles fought at Panipat — 1526, 1556, 1761 — happened because controlling Panipat meant controlling the Delhi throne.

FYQ 1 Why did the Already Autonomous States survive longer than the Successor States? 150 words · 10 marks "The Already Autonomous States (Rajputs, Travancore, Mysore) lasted longer in the 18th century than the Mughal Successor States (Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad). Examine the reasons."

Demand of the question: Show why the three Already Autonomous States survived longer + compare with how Successor States collapsed earlier. The verb "examine" means lay out the reasons clearly using regional evidence.

Suggested intro (~25 words): The Already Autonomous States survived longer than the Successor States because each carried a pre-Mughal identity, a defensible geography, and a strategic resource base of its own.

Guiding themes (pick 4-5 for a 150-word answer):

1. Pre-Mughal identity — Rajputs had centuries-old clan identity (see 'Akbar56-05 and Rajputs' and 'Rajputs vs Aurangzeb58-07 and Marathas'); Travancore was never under the Mughals (see 'Travancore:'; Mysore inherited Vijayanagar legacy (see 'How did Mysore go from being a minor kingdom to one of the most powerful states in 18th-century India?'.

2. Defensible geography — Rajputana desert + Travancore Western Ghats + Mysore plateau limited Mughal/Maratha reach.

3. Resource base — Travancore Malabar trade monopoly (see 'Travancore:'; Mysore Military Fiscalism (see 'How did Mysore fund this military machine?'; Rajputs in trade-route control (Marwar Delhi-Agra-Ahmedabad).

4. State capacity — Marthanda Varma29-58 + Haider Ali61-82 built modern European-style armies (see 'Travancore:' and 'How did Mysore go from being a minor kingdom to one of the most powerful states in 18th-century India?'); Rajputs preserved Mansabdari but on their own terms after 1707 (see 'Rajputs vs Aurangzeb58-07 and Marathas'.

5. Slower EIC takeover — EIC used Subordinate Alliance (Subsidiary Alliance) rather than direct annexation, buying time (see 'Rajputs vs Aurangzeb58-07 and Marathas' and 'Travancore vs Mysore & EIC'); Mysore lasted till Fourth Anglo-Mysore War 1799; Travancore till SA 1805 + Velu Thampi revolt 1809.

Suggested conclusion (~20 words): Pre-existing identity + geography + a strategic resource base gave the Already Autonomous States the slack the Successor States never had.

Revise: 'Akbar56-05 and Rajputs' and 'Rajputs vs Aurangzeb58-07 and Marathas' + 'Travancore:' and 'Travancore vs Mysore & EIC' + 'How did Mysore go from being a minor kingdom to one of the most powerful states in 18th-century India?' and 'How did Mysore fund this military machine?'.
