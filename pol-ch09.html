In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • Article 368 (Part XX) gives Parliament the constituent power to amend the Constitution. India's Constitution is neither rigid like the USA nor flexible like the UK: it is a mix.
  • An amendment starts only as a Bill in either House of Parliament, by a minister or a private member, with no prior permission of the President.
  • There are three ways:
    • simple majority
    • special majority (majority of total membership plus two-thirds of those present and voting)
    • special majority plus ratification by half the states
  • The President must give assent (24th Amendment, 1971). There is no joint sitting if the Houses disagree.
  • Parliament cannot change the basic structure (Kesavananda)
  • The 42nd Amendment's Clauses (4) and (5) were struck down in Minerva Mills (1980)

Key terms

Constituent power
Parliament's special power to amend the Constitution, different from ordinary law-making.
Special majority
Majority of the total membership of the House plus two-thirds of members present and voting.
Simple majority
Majority of members present and voting, as for an ordinary law.
Ratification by states
Approval by at least half of the State Legislatures, needed for federal provisions, before presidential assent.
Article 368(3)
Says Article 13 does not apply to amendments; still, basic structure limits them.
Article 368(4) and (5)
Added by the 42nd Amendment: bar on judicial review and no limit on power; struck down in Minerva Mills.
Rigid, flexible, mixed
USA is rigid, UK is flexible, India is mixed (semi-rigid).
GST Council
Introduced by the 101st Amendment; amending its provisions needs state ratification.
Remember it as

Special majority = majority of total membership + 2/3 present and voting; federal items also need half the states.

1 · A living and evolving document#

  • Constitutions elsewhere often undergo overhauls after political upheavals. The Soviet Union had four constitutions in seven decades, and France experimented with several since its Revolution. India still works under the Constitution adopted in 1949.
  • Its strength is change without losing continuity: firm in core values, flexible in application.
  • The framers knew no constitution could foresee every future challenge.

2 · Article 368 at a glance#

  • Article 368, in Part XX, gives the exclusive procedure and authority for amending the Constitution. It confers on Parliament the constituent power, distinct from ordinary legislative power, and fixes a special, rigid procedure.

The five clauses

ClauseSubjectGist
(1)Constituent powerParliament may add, vary or repeal any provision, following Article 368 procedure
(2)ProcedureBill in either House, special majority, President's assent. Proviso: ratification by half the States for federal matters
(3)Immunity from Article 13"Nothing in Article 13 shall apply to any amendment made under this Article"
(4)Bar on judicial reviewInserted by the 42nd Amendment (1976). Struck down in Minerva Mills (1980)
(5)No limitationAdded by the 42nd Amendment (1976). Parliament's power is wide but not unlimited

  • Shortcut: 1-2-3-4-5. 1 = power. 2 = procedure. 3 = Art. 13 shield. 4 and 5 = 42nd Amendment, both defanged.

3 · Clauses (1) and (2): power and procedure#

Clause (1): constituent power

  • A special power, distinct from ordinary legislative power under Article 245.
  • Overriding effect over other provisions: the amending power cannot be curtailed by any other article.
  • Parliament may add, vary or repeal any provision, provided Article 368 is followed.
  • So the power is not unlimited but procedurally regulated.

Clause (2): procedure

How a Constitution Amendment Bill passes

  • Introduction: Bill in either House of Parliament (not Lok Sabha only). No prior permission of the President. Minister or private member may introduce
  • Passage in each House: Special majority: majority of the total membership of the House AND two-thirds of members present and voting
  • Federal subject?: If yes, ratification by not less than one-half of the State Legislatures is needed before presentation
  • Presentation to President: President is bound to give assent (made obligatory by the 24th Amendment, 1971)
  • Result: The Constitution stands amended according to the Bill's terms
How a Constitution Amendment Bill passes
  • This ensures amendments reflect broad consensus, not a transient majority.

Special majority: the two parts

  • Part 1: Majority of the total membership of the House
  • Part 2: Two-thirds of members present and voting
  • Memory aid (Passing steps): Introduce in either House, Special majority in each House, Ratify if federal, Assent compulsory: "I See Red Apples".
  • Shortcut: Four truths. Either House. No President's prior nod. Assent obligatory. No joint sitting. (Joint sitting is missing: see Section 9.)

5 · Clauses (3), (4) and (5)#

Clause (3): immunity from Article 13

  • "Nothing in Article 13 shall apply to any amendment made under this Article."
  • A constitutional amendment is not an ordinary law, so it cannot be invalidated merely for violating Fundamental Rights.
  • But Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973): Article 13 does not apply, yet amendments cannot alter the basic structure.
  • So the clause exempts amendments from Article 13 but not from the basic structure doctrine.

Clause (4): bar on judicial review

  • Inserted by the 42nd Amendment Act (1976): no amendment, including those touching Fundamental Rights, can be questioned in any court on any ground.
  • Meant to make amendments non-justiciable.
  • Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) struck down the absolute exclusion: judicial review is part of the basic structure. Amendments that damage or destroy the basic structure can still be challenged.

Clause (5): no limitation

  • Also added by the 42nd Amendment (1976): no limitation whatsoever on Parliament's constituent power.
  • Meant to affirm Parliament's supremacy in amendment.
  • The handout says it was curtailed by Kesavananda Bharati and Minerva Mills: power is wide but not unlimited. Parliament may amend any provision but cannot alter the basic identity or foundational features.

What the 42nd Amendment tried and what happened

  • Tried:
    • Clause 4: no judicial review
    • Clause 5: no limit on amending power
  • Result:
    • Minerva Mills (1980) struck down the absolute exclusion
    • Basic structure survives (see Ch 6)
  • Clause 5 detail: The Basic Structure chapter credits Minerva Mills with striking down Clauses (4) and (5). Here the handout names Kesavananda and Minerva Mills together. For "which case struck down", answer Minerva Mills (1980).

6 · The three types of amendment#

  • Article 368 (Part XX) classifies amendments by rigidity. The Constitution can be amended in three ways.

1. Simple majority of Parliament

  • Amended like ordinary legislation, by a majority of members present and voting. No State ratification, and not by the special Article 368 procedure.

2. Special majority of Parliament

  • Applies to the majority of constitutional provisions. The Bill must be passed by a majority of the total membership of each House and two-thirds of members present and voting.
  • Prevents frequent or politically motivated changes to vital principles.
  • Covers:
    • Fundamental Rights
    • Directive Principles of State Policy
    • all other provisions not under the simple-majority category or the ratification category

3. Special majority plus ratification by half of the States

  • For provisions of federal significance. Needs special majority of Parliament and ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.

9 · Criticism of the procedure#

Criticisms listed

CriticismArgument
No separate constitutional bodyNo independent constitutional convention. Parliament that makes ordinary laws also amends the Constitution, blurring legislative and constituent power
Centralised power of amendmentOnly Parliament can initiate amendments, States cannot. Weakens federalism
Limited role of StatesOnly half of the States needed, even in federal matters. Dilutes shared sovereignty in a quasi-federal system
No time frame for ratificationNo deadline. States may delay or withhold consent indefinitely
No provision for joint sittingIf the two Houses disagree the Bill lapses. Article 368 has no joint sitting, a rigidity absent in ordinary law-making
Similarity to ordinary legislative processApart from special majority, it resembles normal law-making. Constitution more amenable than in the USA or Australia
Ambiguous and incomplete provisionsVague wording forced judicial interpretation (Kesavananda, Minerva Mills). Gaps about limits
Central dominance but judicial dependenceUnion controls initiation but judiciary defines the limits: a dual dependence blurring accountability

Balanced verdict and voices

  • Experts have appreciated the design: neither too rigid to resist change nor too flexible to invite instability. Reforms such as the Right to Education (86th Amendment, 2002) and the 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) came while keeping stability.

From Lakshmikanth · Indian Polity, 8th ed. Ch 11, Amendment of the Constitution
  • The bill for amendment can be initiated only in either House of Parliament, not in state legislatures. Each House passes it separately.
  • For federal provisions, state legislatures ratify by a simple majority of members present and voting. If half the states agree, the formality is complete even if others do nothing.
  • 'Total membership' means all seats of the House, whatever the vacancies or absentees. Lok Sabha Rules apply the special majority at all effective stages of the Bill, not only the final vote.
  • The President can neither withhold assent nor return the Bill for reconsideration. After assent the Bill becomes a constitutional amendment Act.
  • Simple-majority matters are amended under other articles, outside Article 368, and are not treated as amendments of the Constitution for Article 368.
  • State legislatures cannot start amendments, except by passing a resolution requesting Parliament to create or abolish a legislative council, and Parliament may ignore it.
  • The Constitution is silent on whether states may withdraw their approval once given. In the USA three-fourths of the states are needed, and an amendment can also be proposed by a convention called on the petition of two-thirds of state legislatures.
  • An ordinary Bill can have a joint sitting in a deadlock, but an amendment Bill cannot. The procedure is also called too sketchy, leaving wide scope for the judiciary.
  • The GST Council item was added by the 101st Amendment (2016) and relates to Article 279-A.
  • Lakshmikanth differs:
    • It says Article 368 itself provides two types (special majority; special majority with state ratification), while the simple-majority type sits in other articles. The handout shows three types under the amendment scheme
    • For the exam, state all three ways. K.C. Wheare: this variety in the amending process is 'wise but rarely found'

Traps#

Trap 1 · What the two majorities mean Special majority is NOT just two-thirds. It needs a majority of the total membership of each House and two-thirds of members present and voting. Simple majority means a majority of those present and voting. Rule: two tests together: total membership and present-and-voting.
Trap 2 · Half the states, not three-fourths Federal amendments need ratification by half of the State Legislatures (the USA needs three-fourths). There is no time limit for states, and only Parliament can start an amendment Bill. Rule: half, not three-fourths; Centre initiates, states only ratify.
Trap 3 · President and joint sitting The President cannot refuse assent after the 24th Amendment (1971). No prior permission is needed to introduce the Bill, and a private member may introduce it. There is no joint sitting for amendment Bills, so disagreement means the Bill lapses. Rule: assent is compulsory; joint sitting is not available.
Trap 4 · Article 13 immunity is not full immunity Clause (3) says Article 13 does not apply to amendments, and Clauses (4) and (5) tried to end judicial review and all limits. Kesavananda and Minerva Mills held that the basic structure still limits amendments. Rule: Article 13 does not apply, basic structure does.
