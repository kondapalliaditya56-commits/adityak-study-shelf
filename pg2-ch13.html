1 · NCSC AND NCST · Articles 338, 338A · Functions · Achievements · Critique · Reforms#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 3-8. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The National Commission for Scheduled Castes (Article 338) and the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (Article 338A) are constitutional watchdogs. They check whether the safeguards given to SCs and STs are really working, inquire into complaints and advise the government on development. They can summon people and documents like a civil court, but they can only recommend, not order. Delays, vacancies, weak independence and thin staff reduce their impact.

National Commission FOR Scheduled Castes (NCSC): THE Basics

ArticleWhat it does
338Creates the NCSC and lays down its composition, powers and functions. Because it sits in the Constitution, it is a constitutional body, not a mere statutory one.
341The President, after consulting the Governor of the State concerned, specifies which castes are to be treated as Scheduled Castes in that State or Union Territory. A caste is therefore an SC only through this notification.
366(24)Defines “Scheduled Castes” for constitutional purposes (the castes, races or tribes, or parts of them, that are notified under Article 341).

Functions: what the NCSC actually does

  • 1. Investigation and monitoring. It examines the constitutional and legal safeguards meant for SCs, evaluates how well they work and points out where implementation is failing. Example: reviewing reservation in education, jobs and promotions in central and State services.
  • 2. Inquiry into complaints. It looks into cases where SCs are denied their rights, face atrocities or are discriminated against, and recommends remedies. Example: violence against Dalits that falls under the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act.
  • 3. Advice on development planning. It takes part in, and advises on, the planning of socio-economic development for SCs so that policy is inclusive. Example: inputs on targeted schemes like the Scheduled Castes Sub-Plan.
  • 4. Annual and special reports. It reports to the President on how the safeguards are being implemented and on the SCs’ socio-economic progress.
  • 5. Recommendations to governments. It suggests policy measures so that safeguards, welfare schemes and development programmes are implemented effectively.
  • 6. Extra role. It also performs similar functions for the Anglo-Indian community. Until the 102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018 the NCSC also handled matters relating to OBCs
  • That work now belongs to the NCBC

NCSC: AN Analytical LOOK

Where the NCSC has worked (strengths)

  • Service safeguards. It has scrutinised recruitment, promotion and reservation practices across government services. It encouraged SC/ST employee associations, liaison officers and special grievance cells in ministries and PSUs to deal with discrimination. It has intervened where reservation benefits were denied in central universities and UPSC recruitment.
  • Education. It focuses on literacy and access to higher education, with special attention to female literacy. It monitors scholarships and pushes for better schools and hostels for SC students. Its review of the Post-Matric Scholarship Scheme found poor disbursement of funds and recommended Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT).
  • Atrocities and civil rights. It monitors atrocity cases, especially custodial violence and police excesses. NCRB data for 2021 shows crimes against SCs make up nearly 89% of all atrocity cases against SCs and STs combined, which shows the scale of the problem. However, it tracks numbers and procedures more than it pushes wider social reform.
  • Land and economic issues. It recognises that SCs depend heavily on agriculture and argues for land reform and protection against land alienation. Its proposals on redistribution and tenancy rights usually meet political resistance. Land disputes in Tamil Nadu and Bihar show that Dalits remain vulnerable to land grabbing.

Where it falls short (limitations)

  • Non-binding recommendations: despite civil-court-like investigative powers, its findings have no legal force, so governments can ignore them.
  • Procedural delay: inquiries drag on, so justice becomes slow and often ineffective.
  • Perceived closeness to government: appointments and alignment with government positions raise doubts about its independence.
  • Late reports: annual reports arrive late and are often not discussed in Parliament. The 12th Report (for 2018-20) was tabled only in 2022.
  • Elite bias: priorities tend to favour better-off SC groups, while the poorest Dalits are under-represented.
  • Reactive style: it rarely uses its suo motu power to start action on its own.

Reforms suggested

  • Autonomy: appoint members through a bipartisan process so the executive cannot control them.
  • Timely reporting: make annual submission and discussion in Parliament mandatory for accountability.
  • Inclusion: concentrate on the most marginalised sub-groups inside the SC category rather than letting a few groups capture benefits.
  • Data-driven work: use qualitative and quantitative studies and involve social scientists in policy advice.
  • Clear roles: sort out overlaps with the Ministry of Social Justice.
  • Proactive role: use suo motu powers in atrocities, land disputes and denial of reservation.
Way forward / key point

Concluding thought: caste identities are slowly weakening in cities because of education and mobility, but caste inequality persists, especially in rural India. Leaders such as B. R. Ambedkar, K. R. Narayanan and Ram Nath Kovind show progress, yet they also show why institutional safeguards are still needed. The NCSC has to change from a procedural watchdog into a proactive reformist body.

National Commission FOR Scheduled Tribes (NCST): Background

Who is a “Scheduled Tribe”?

  • A “tribe” generally means a community with shared culture, language and territory, often living in relative isolation. A Scheduled Tribe (ST) is a constitutional category: communities notified under Article 342 for special protection.
  • The Constitution neither defines “tribe” nor gives criteria for inclusion. Tribal communities are recognised by distinct cultural traditions, remoteness and socio-economic disadvantage.
  • Census 2011: India has 10.42 crore STs, about 8.6% of the population. They live on over 15% of the country’s area and sit on nearly 20% of natural resources.
  • They are concentrated in Central India (Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Rajasthan) and the North-East.
  • Development levels vary widely: Mizoram has near-universal literacy, while groups such as the Jarawas and Shompens of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands are highly vulnerable.
  • The NCST is created under Article 338A (inserted when the single SC/ST commission was split).

Functions of the NCST

  • 1. Investigation. Examines how constitutional and legal safeguards for STs are implemented. Example: monitoring the PESA Act in Scheduled Areas.
  • 2. Complaint redressal. Inquires into specific complaints of denial of rights or discrimination. Example: land alienation disputes in Jharkhand and Odisha.
  • 3. Planning advice. Gives inputs on socio-economic policy, evaluates schemes and suggests reforms. Example: recommending focus on tribal education and livelihood under the Tribal Sub-Plan.
  • 4. Reporting. Submits annual and special reports to the President on tribal rights and welfare.
  • 5. Policy recommendations. Advises the Union and States on protection, welfare and development of STs.

Additional functions under the 2005 Rules

  • Securing the rights of tribals over minor forest produce.
  • Protecting tribal ownership over land, water and mineral resources.
  • Making sure tribals displaced by development projects are rehabilitated.
  • Preventing land alienation and helping restore land that has already been alienated.
  • Promoting tribal participation in forest management and social afforestation.
  • Helping implement PESA, 1996 in Scheduled Areas.
  • Encouraging a shift from jhum (shifting) cultivation to sustainable livelihoods.

Critique and limitations

  • Dysfunction: parliamentary reports show the NCST has been largely inactive for about four years and has not submitted reports to Parliament, which undermines its mandate of monitoring safeguards.
  • Weak regional reach: it works with only six regional offices, many understaffed. Requests for offices in tribal-heavy States such as Andhra Pradesh and Maharashtra remain unmet.
  • Lack of independence: the Ministry of Tribal Affairs overshadows it, which blurs the line between an advisory commission and an executive department.
  • Exclusion from policy-making: it has complained of inadequate consultation on key laws such as the Forest Rights Act, which directly affect tribal welfare.
  • Underfunding: chronic budget shortages limit inquiries, outreach and monitoring, and reduce operational independence. A 2022 parliamentary committee noted it was working with under 50% of sanctioned strength.

Way forward for the NCST

  • Fill vacancies quickly so a fully functional body with expert members restores credibility and timely monitoring.
  • Expand regional presence with more, properly staffed offices, so it can talk to tribal communities directly.
  • Reinforce autonomy from the Ministry of Tribal Affairs through structural reform, so it acts as a neutral protector.
  • Formal consultation: make it mandatory to consult the Commission on policies that affect STs, so tribal voices are built into law-making.
  • Better budget and financial autonomy for investigations, outreach and monitoring.
Way forward / key point

Concluding thought: the NCST was meant to be the guardian of tribal rights under Article 338A. Staff shortages, limited independence, weak outreach and poor funding have blunted it. Its effectiveness depends on timely reporting, government responsiveness and proactive suo motu action, especially because tribals are over 8% of the population and still face marginalisation.

In simple words
  • The NCBC got constitutional status in 2018, so it can no longer be changed at the executive’s will and it has civil-court powers in inquiries. All three commissions (SC, ST, OBC) suffer from similar problems: vacancies, advisory-only powers, delays and low awareness. National Commission FOR Backward Classes (NCBC)

Why backwardness is a caste question in India

  • Backward classes in India are rooted in systemic disadvantage tied to caste, occupation and social hierarchy.
  • Social “class” is fluid (a person can move up), but caste-based backwardness carries intergenerational barriers in education, jobs and representation.
  • To address this, constitutional and statutory mechanisms were created. They ended in the NCBC.
  • Once the NCBC was given constitutional status (2018), it no longer depends on executive discretion. It works as an autonomous constitutional body, giving it autonomy while keeping it accountable to the highest constitutional office.

Justice Rohini Commission (OBC sub-categorisation)

  • Set up in 2017 under Article 340 to examine sub-classification of the 27% OBC quota.
  • It proposed dividing OBC castes into four groups so that benefits reach the less-advantaged castes, not only the dominant ones.
  • Proposed split: Group 1 gets 2%, Group 2 gets 6%, Group 3 gets 9% and Group 4 gets 10% (total 27%).
  • The final report was still awaited as per the handout.

Powers of the NCBC (civil-court powers)

  • It can summon and enforce the attendance of any person from anywhere in India and examine them on oath.
  • It can require the discovery and production of official or private documents.
  • It can receive evidence on affidavits and requisition public records from courts or government offices.
  • It can issue summons for witnesses and documents, and has any other powers the President gives it.
  • These quasi-judicial powers let it investigate independently and hold institutions accountable.

Benefits of constitutional status (with examples)

  • 1. More authority. Civil-court powers in inquiries make officials accountable. It can summon officers, demand records and take affidavits while probing OBC grievances.
  • 2. Wider mandate. Earlier it only revised the OBC list
  • Now it also monitors welfare schemes and safeguards. It can probe denial of scholarships or weak enforcement of reservation
  • 3. Parliamentary oversight. Its reports are tabled in Parliament, which forces an executive response. Its 2022 report on under-use of OBC welfare funds made ministries explain corrective steps.
  • 4. Independence. Tenure and service safeguards stop arbitrary changes to how it works.
  • 5. Stronger social-justice role. It acted on the reservation backlog in central PSUs and pushed recruitment drives for OBC candidates.
  • 6. Symbolic and political value. Constitutional status in 2018 reassured the roughly 40% of India that is OBC of the State’s commitment to equity.

Challenges Faced BY SC, ST AND OBC Commissions

Seven shared problems

  • 1. Vacancies and resource gaps. Persistent understaffing and small budgets weaken them. A 2022 Parliamentary Committee noted the NCST was running on less than 50% of sanctioned strength, which hurt its inquiries.
  • 2. Erosion of autonomy. Politics influences appointments and recommendations are not binding, which reduces credibility. NCBC’s 2020 reports on OBC reservation backlog in central universities were ignored by ministries.
  • 3. Weak statutory powers. They cannot enforce compliance or fix structural problems. The NCBC cannot define “backwardness”, as clarified in the Maratha Quota judgment (2021).
  • 4. Case backlog and delayed reporting. Thousands of complaints are pending and annual reports reach Parliament late, which blunts accountability.
  • 5. Opaque functioning. Little transparency in how members are selected and limited disclosure of outcomes lower public trust.
  • 6. Representation deficit. Retired bureaucrats dominate instead of experts, academics or community representatives, so perspectives are narrow.
  • 7. Low outreach and awareness. Target communities often do not know how to complain. NCRB 2020 showed rising SC/ST atrocity cases but few complaints reached the commissions because people did not know the procedure.

Pathways for reform

  • Independence: redo appointment processes with clear criteria, expert representation and less political interference.
  • Wider powers:
    • Consider making recommendations binding
    • Let the NCBC define backwardness parameters
    • Harmonise powers across the SC, ST and OBC commissions
  • Efficiency: fill vacancies quickly, give adequate budgets and modernise case-handling infrastructure.
  • Transparency: digital case tracking, fixed timelines for resolving complaints and regular public disclosures.
  • Data and research: build full caste/tribe databases and commission impact studies of welfare schemes (like post-NFHS style surveys).
  • Collaboration: structured coordination with ministries, State commissions and NGOs, and partnerships with academic institutions.
  • Capacity: a dedicated cadre for these commissions, with training for members and staff in rights, technology and emerging challenges.
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: strengthening the SC, ST and OBC commissions needs autonomy, resources and binding powers. With transparency, data-driven policy and collaboration, they can become credible constitutional watchdogs that deliver substantive equality.

Types OF Rights

What is a right? A justified claim that allows individuals to live with dignity and freedom. Rights are broadly of four types.

TypeMeaning and key featuresExamples
Natural rightsBelong to every person by birth. They are not granted by the government. Considered universal and inalienable.Right to life, liberty and property
Moral rightsBased on moral values, conscience and social acceptance. Generally not legally enforceable.Right to respectful and fair treatment
Legal rightsRecognised and protected by law and enforceable through courts.Civil rights (life, liberty, equality); Political rights (voting, contesting elections); Economic rights (work, fair wages, social security)
Human rightsUniversal and inalienable rights available to all individuals: civil, political, economic, social and cultural. Recognised under the UDHR, 1948.Right to life, dignity, equality, education and freedom

Fundamental Rights vs Constitutional Rights

Both are categories of legal rights. The difference is where they sit and how they are enforced.

BasisFundamental RightsOther constitutional rights
MeaningBasic rights guaranteed under Part III of the ConstitutionRights given by the Constitution but outside Part III
ProvisionsArticles 12-35Various provisions, such as Article 300A and Article 326
ExamplesRight to Equality, Freedom, Life and Personal LibertyRight to Property (Art 300A) and Right to Vote (Art 326)
EnforcementDirectly enforceable through Article 32 (Supreme Court) and Article 226 (High Court)Enforceable through appropriate legal remedies, depending on the right
Value addition

Key point: all Fundamental Rights are constitutional rights, but not all constitutional rights are Fundamental Rights.

3 · National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) · Composition · Working · Functions · ROLE · Reforms#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 11-16. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The NHRC was set up in 1993 under the Protection of Human Rights Act after worries about custodial violence and encounter deaths. It inquires into violations (suo motu or on complaint), visits jails, reviews safeguards and spreads awareness. Its recommendations are only advisory, it cannot touch the armed forces except to ask for reports, and GANHRI has criticised its independence. Reforms aim to give it binding force, transparent appointments and more staff.

WHY AND HOW THE NHRC WAS Created

Background

  • Human rights are the inherent and inalienable rights of every person, ensuring dignity, equality and freedom regardless of race, caste, gender or belief.
  • In the 1980s and early 1990s, concern about custodial violence, encounter deaths and systemic discrimination pushed India to build an institution to protect rights. This led to the NHRC under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993.
  • Parliament built a three-tier structure: the NHRC at the national level, State Human Rights Commissions (SHRCs) in States, and Human Rights Courts in districts. This aims for both accountability and access.
  • It works in line with the UN-endorsed Paris Principles (1993), which call for autonomy, independence and accountability of national human rights institutions.

HOW THE NHRC Works (procedure)

Step by step (governed by the 1993 Act and the NHRC (Procedure) Regulations, 1994)

  • 1. Complaint. Individuals, NGOs or the Commission itself (suo motu) can start a case. It should generally relate to violations within one year of occurrence. The Law Division first screens it before it is admitted.
  • 2. Inquiry. After admission the NHRC can either investigate itself through its Investigation Division (headed by an officer of Director General of Police rank) or ask the concerned authority or police for a report. It then decides whether the act is a “violation” under the law.
  • 3. Hearings and recommendations. In serious matters (custodial deaths, bonded labour, encounter killings) it holds open hearings or field visits. It then recommends compensation, disciplinary action or a policy change.
  • 4. Follow-up. The ministry or State must submit an Action Taken Report (ATR) within one month. The NHRC reviews compliance and may publish non-compliance in its annual report, which is tabled in Parliament, so there is public accountability.
  • 5. Coordination and research. It works through specialised divisions (Research, Training, Policy, Awareness) to build institutional knowledge.

Functions (section 12 OF THE 1993 ACT)

Eleven functions

  • 1. Inquiry into violations (Sections 17-18): into violations, or negligence in preventing them, by a public servant, suo motu, on a petition, or on a court’s reference. This is its core function.
  • 2. Intervening in court proceedings that involve allegations of human rights violations, with the court’s approval.
  • 3. Inspecting detention centres (jails, juvenile homes, psychiatric facilities) to study living conditions and suggest reform, in line with the UN Mandela Rules (2015).
  • 4. Reviewing legal safeguards: Fundamental Rights, criminal procedure, prison laws and police manuals, and advising on effective enforcement.
  • 5. Addressing factors that inhibit rights:
    • Terrorism, displacement, communal violence, environmental damage
    • Suggesting preventive or remedial steps
  • 6. International alignment:
    • Studying treaties like the UDHR 1948, ICCPR 1966 and CEDAW 1979 and recommending domestic alignment
    • Coordinating with the UN Human Rights Council and other national institutions under the Paris Principles
  • 7. Research and policy on themes like gender violence, bonded labour and climate justice.
  • 8. Human rights literacy: publications, training for police and officials, campaigns in schools and universities with NCERT and UGC.
  • 9. Working with civil society: supporting NGOs and rights defenders as partners in advocacy and victim assistance.
  • 10. Miscellaneous: any other function needed for promoting rights, including setting up human rights courts, monitoring bonded labour and overseeing women and child protection.
  • 11. Added by practice and amendment: monitoring Supreme Court orders on custodial deaths, manual scavenging and police reforms, and overseeing the National Action Plan on Business and Human Rights (NAP-BHR) started by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs.
Note

GANHRI (Global Alliance of National Human Rights Institutions) accredits national human rights bodies. It has repeatedly raised concerns about India’s NHRC. Recent reports (2024-25) say the selection process is non-transparent (vacancies not publicly advertised; vague application, screening and appointment procedures), follow-up on serious violations is weak, independence from the executive is limited and civil society participation is thin. These critiques contributed to the recommendation to downgrade the NHRC from “A” grade accreditation. (verify current status)

ROLE Played BY THE NHRC

Main contributions

  • Guidelines and legal oversight. It issued detailed guidelines on custodial deaths, which later shaped Supreme Court law (D. K. Basu v. State of West Bengal, 1997). It criticised misuse of special security laws such as POTA and TADA and recommended safeguards against arbitrary detention.
  • Socio-economic rights and marginalised groups. It took up the starvation deaths in Odisha (2001-02), leading to stronger monitoring of food-security schemes in Kalahandi and Bolangir. In 2022 it flagged large-scale displacement of tribals in Chhattisgarh due to mining and told the State to ensure rehabilitation.
  • Complementing the judiciary. In NHRC v. State of Arunachal Pradesh (1996) the Supreme Court upheld the NHRC’s standing to move the court to protect Chakma refugees. Its prison reports (2017-18) fed into the Justice Amitava Roy Committee recommendations on decongesting prisons.
  • Policy advocacy and awareness. Its recommendations helped shape the Juvenile Justice Act (2000) and the Mental Healthcare Act (2017). It launched open online courses on human rights in 2020.

Challenges AND Where THE NHRC HAS Failed

Six challenges

  • 1. No enforcement power. Recommendations are advisory, so compliance is poor. It cannot impose penalties or directly award compensation. In 2016, then NHRC Chairperson Justice H. L. Dattu himself called it a “toothless tiger” in an interview.
  • 2. Limited jurisdiction. In cases involving the armed forces it can only seek a report from the government. It cannot take up cases older than one year, or violations by private individuals unless State complicity is proved.
  • 3. Structure and composition. Too many retired judges make it more judicial than human-rights-centred. There is limited diversity: SCs, STs, women and civil society activists are under-represented. Critics call it a “post-retirement” home for judges and bureaucrats.
  • 4. Operations and resources. A heavy backlog from excess caseload
  • Inadequate staffing, especially in investigation and research (many on deputation)
  • Scarce funds that hamper outreach, especially at State level
  • 5. Independence and credibility. The executive dominates appointments, raising doubts about impartiality
  • Reliance on government resources reduces perceived autonomy. GANHRI downgraded its accreditation in 2017, citing political interference and lack of pluralism
  • 6. Accessibility and awareness. Many citizens, especially from marginalised groups, do not know the NHRC’s mandate. Procedural complexity and urban-centred working limit access.

WAY Forward: Reforming AND Strengthening THE NHRC

Six reform directions

  • 1. Powers and jurisdiction. Make recommendations binding in grave violations, with a statutory duty on governments to respond. Extend jurisdiction to violations by private actors and security forces, with independent investigation wings.
  • 2. Transparent, inclusive appointments. Publicly advertise vacancies with clear application, screening and shortlisting steps. Add diversity: women, SC/ST, minorities and rights activists alongside judges.
  • 3. Resources and decentralisation. A dedicated budget and permanent staff instead of deputation. Regional benches and stronger SHRCs for faster handling.
  • 4. Accountability and follow-up. An online tracker for the status of recommendations and government responses
  • Periodic independent audits and parliamentary review of annual reports
  • 5. Awareness and victim support. Targeted outreach in rural, tribal and marginalised areas
  • Multilingual online complaint portals
  • Free legal and medical aid for victims
  • 6. Regular legal and institutional review. Amend the Protection of Human Rights Act periodically to meet global standards like the Paris Principles
  • Evaluate performance through external committees and civil society consultation

4 · UDHR 1948 AND THE Constitution · State Human Rights Commissions · Human Rights Courts#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 16-19. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • The UDHR (1948) set the moral base for human rights, and many of its ideas appear in India’s Preamble, Fundamental Rights, DPSP and Fundamental Duties. State Human Rights Commissions (SHRCs) are the State-level counterparts of the NHRC, and Human Rights Courts (Section 30) try human rights offences in districts. The 2019 amendment changed tenure and eligibility. Both SHRCs and these courts are weak in practice.

Universal Declaration OF Human Rights (UDHR), 1948

What it is

  • Adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948. It was the first international document to recognise that all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights.
  • Drafted after the Second World War to prevent future atrocities by setting common standards of justice, freedom and peace.
  • It rests on four principles: Dignity, Liberty, Equality and Brotherhood.
  • It has 30 Articles, broadly grouped into civil-political rights and socio-economic rights.

How the Indian Constitution reflects the UDHR

  • Preamble: promises Justice, Liberty, Equality and Fraternity, reflecting the UDHR’s commitment to dignity and freedom.

Other constitutional provisions that echo the UDHR

  • Article 21A: free and compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14 (UDHR Art 26).
  • Article 45: early childhood care and education for children below 6.
  • Article 300A: right to property (UDHR Art 17).
  • Article 326: adult suffrage, reflecting the right to political participation (UDHR Art 21).
Way forward / key point

Conclusion: the Indian Constitution gives legal and institutional form to many UDHR principles by protecting dignity through Fundamental Rights, DPSP and Fundamental Duties.

State Human Rights Commissions (SHRCs)

Role

  • Working inside a State’s boundaries, SHRCs monitor prisons, handle custodial violence, review safeguards and create awareness.
  • They are the first line of institutional redress for citizens and complement the NHRC in building a nationwide human rights architecture based on constitutional morality and accountability.
AspectProvision (after the 2019 amendment)
CompositionA Chairperson and 2 Members. Chairperson: a retired Chief Justice or Judge of a High Court (after 2019, even a retired HC judge is eligible, not only a Chief Justice). Members: (i) a serving or retired HC judge or a District Judge with 7 years’ experience; (ii) an expert in human rights.
Appointing authorityThe Governor, on the recommendation of a committee headed by the Chief Minister, with the Speaker of the Assembly, the State Home Minister and the Leader of Opposition in the Assembly. If the Legislature has two Houses, the Chairman and Leader of Opposition of the Legislative Council are also included.
ConsultationIf a sitting HC judge or District Judge is to be appointed, prior consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court is mandatory.
Tenure3 years or until age 70 (reduced from 5 years by the 2019 Amendment). Eligible for reappointment.
RemovalOnly the President can remove them (not the Governor), on grounds like insolvency, infirmity, conviction or misbehaviour, proved by a Supreme Court inquiry (similar to the NHRC).
After tenureCannot take employment under the State or Central Government.
Conditions of serviceDecided by the State Government but cannot be changed to their disadvantage after appointment.
AdministrationThe Secretary holds administrative and financial powers, subject to the Chairperson’s control (2019 amendment).

Powers and functions (Section 29 of the 1993 Act) — preventive, corrective and advisory

  • 1. Inquiry into violations (suo motu, on petition or on a court’s direction): custodial deaths, police excesses, bonded labour, discrimination by State authorities.
  • 2. Intervening in judicial proceedings involving human rights allegations, so State agencies follow constitutional and legal safeguards.
  • 3. Inspecting and monitoring jails, detention centres and mental health institutions, and recommending humane treatment, medical care and legal aid.
  • 4. Reviewing legal safeguards and recommending legislative or policy changes.
  • 5. Examining structural causes such as terrorism, communal violence and systemic exclusion.
  • 6. Engaging with international norms by studying treaties and advising the State to align local laws.
  • 7. Research, education and awareness through drives, school curricula, workshops and tie-ups with NGOs and universities.
  • 8. Collaborating with civil society, acting as a bridge between NGOs and the State.

Working mechanism and inquiry procedure

  • 1. Complaint and admissibility. Any person, NGO or public authority can complain (online, by post or in person)
  • The Commission also takes suo motu notice of media reports. Cases older than one year are ordinarily not admitted unless there are exceptional circumstances
  • 2. Preliminary scrutiny (Section 17). It may seek information or a report from the State Government or a subordinate authority within a set time. If there is no reply it proceeds on its own. If it is satisfied that remedial action has already been taken, it may close the matter and inform the complainant.
  • 3. Inquiry and hearing. It inquires directly or through its investigation division with civil-court powers under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (summoning witnesses, requisitioning documents, recording evidence on oath). Hearings are open and follow natural justice.
  • 4. Recommendations (Section 18). On finding a violation it may recommend compensation or damages, advise disciplinary or criminal proceedings against erring officials, and suggest policy or administrative reform. The State Government must send an Action Taken Report within one month.
  • 5. Special procedure for armed forces (Section 19). For the armed or paramilitary forces it can only call for a report from the Central Government and make recommendations on that basis. The Centre must report back within three months, so oversight exists, though limited.
  • 6. Documentation and follow-up. Inquiries, decisions and compliance updates are published in the Annual Report tabled in the State Legislature. Persistent non-compliance is publicly highlighted, which adds moral and political pressure.
  • 7. Coordination and outreach. It works with the NHRC, Human Rights Courts and civil society to harmonise standards and avoid duplication, with field visits and training especially in rural and tribal areas.

Key challenges

  • Non-binding: State governments often ignore recommendations.
  • Resource constraints: staff shortages and no financial autonomy in many SHRCs.
  • Limited jurisdiction: cannot revisit cases already before the NHRC, and cannot take up violations older than one year.
  • Implementation gap: reports laid before legislatures often see delays or minimal follow-up.

Human Rights Courts (section 30 OF THE 1993 ACT)

Idea: to give speedy justice and real enforcement, the Act requires Human Rights Courts in every district. They are the grassroots judicial mechanism.

  • Establishment (Section 30): the State Government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, notifies a Human Rights Court for each district to try offences from human rights violations. In most States, the Sessions Court is designated.
  • Jurisdiction: they try human rights violations or negligence by public servants that amount to criminal offences. The aim is expeditious trial, because such cases would otherwise face delays in ordinary courts.
  • Special Public Prosecutor (Section 31):
    • The State must appoint one for every Human Rights Court
    • A practising advocate with at least 7 years’ experience
  • Link with SHRC/NHRC:
    • SHRCs and the NHRC investigate and recommend
    • Human Rights Courts actually try offences and punish. This bridges the gap between recommendation and enforcement

Limitations and recent developments

  • In practice these courts are used rarely. Many States have designated Sessions Courts only on paper, with no separate infrastructure, exclusive judges or funds. Victims are unaware, and SHRCs refer few cases.
  • Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Maharashtra have started exclusive benches or pilot human rights courts.
  • The Supreme Court (2018, Re: Inhuman Conditions in 1382 Prisons) directed States to make these courts operational to deal with custodial violence and prison abuse.
