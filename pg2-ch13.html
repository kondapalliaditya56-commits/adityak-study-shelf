1 · NCSC · NCST · Articles 338, 338A · Functions · Critique#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 3-8. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • NCSC (Art 338) and NCST (Art 338A) watch over safeguards for SCs and STs. They have civil-court powers in inquiries, but their advice is not binding. Both suffer from delay, thin staff and weak independence.

National Commission FOR Scheduled Castes (NCSC)

ArticleProvision
338sets up the NCSC, its composition, powers and functions
341President (after consulting the Governor) specifies which castes are SCs in each State/UT
366(24)defines Scheduled Castes

Functions

  • investigate and monitor safeguards (e.g. reservation in education, jobs, promotions)
  • inquire into complaints of denial of rights, atrocities, discrimination (SC/ST Atrocities Act cases)
  • advise on planning for SC development (e.g. SC Sub-Plan)
  • annual and special reports to the President
  • recommend measures to governments
  • Also functions for the Anglo-Indian community
  • Until the 102nd Amendment, 2018 it also dealt with OBCs

Work done

  • Scrutiny of recruitment and promotion
  • Liaison officers, grievance cells, SC/ST employee associations
  • education:
    • Literacy, scholarships, hostels
    • Post-Matric Scholarship review urged DBT
  • atrocities: NCRB 2021 shows crimes against SCs about 89% of atrocities against SCs/STs together
  • land: advocates reform and protection against alienation

Limitations

  • non-binding recommendations
  • delays
  • perceived alignment with government
  • late reports, e.g. 12th Report (2018-20) tabled only in 2022
  • elite-centric focus
  • rare suo motu action

Reforms

  • bipartisan appointments
  • timely reporting and discussion in Parliament
  • focus on marginalised sub-groups
  • data-driven work
  • clear role vs Ministry of Social Justice
  • proactive suo motu power

National Commission FOR Scheduled Tribes (NCST)

Background

  • ST notified under Article 342
  • The Constitution does not define "tribe"
  • Census 2011: 10.42 crore STs (8.6%), on over 15% of area, with nearly 20% of natural resources
  • Concentrated in Central India and the Northeast
  • Mizoram near-universal literacy vs vulnerable groups like Jarawas and Shompens
  • NCST is under Article 338A

Functions

  • examine safeguards (e.g. PESA in Scheduled Areas)
  • complaints (land alienation in Jharkhand, Odisha)
  • advise on planning (e.g. Tribal Sub-Plan)
  • annual and special reports to the President
  • policy advice to Union and States

Additional functions (Rules, 2005)

  • minor forest produce rights
  • ownership of land, water, minerals
  • rehabilitation of those displaced by projects
  • prevent land alienation and restore it
  • tribal role in forest management and social afforestation
  • implement PESA 1996
  • shift from jhum to sustainable livelihoods
AchievementsCritique
2023: notice on Great Nicobar (84.1 sq km tribal reserve, 130 sq km forest)inactive about four years, no reports to Parliament
opposed 2019 FRA amendmentsonly six regional offices, understaffed
2017 report: 28% of displaced tribal households in Chhattisgarh mining lacked rehabilitationovershadowed by Ministry of Tribal Affairs
2020 input: 452 new Eklavya schoolsinadequate consultation (e.g. FRA)
summoned officials in Pathalgadi cases (Jharkhand, 2016)chronic underfunding
2018-19: 4.5% shortfall of STs in central PSUs2022 committee: working with under 50% of sanctioned strength
Way forward / key point

Way forward (NCST): fill vacancies, more regional offices, autonomy from the Ministry, mandatory consultation on ST policies, better funding.

2 · NCBC · Challenges OF Sc-st-obc Commissions · Types OF Rights#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 8-11. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • NCBC was given constitutional status in 2018 and has civil-court powers. All three commissions share the same ills: vacancies, non-binding advice, delays and thin outreach. Rights come as natural, moral, legal and human rights; Fundamental Rights are legal rights inside Part III.

NCBC

Note

Table of NCBC composition and the Constitution Article are in a picture in the handout and were not readable; the notes below use only the text. Constitutional status came in 2018 (the 102nd Amendment appears in the NCSC section).

Idea

  • backwardness here is caste-linked (occupation, hierarchy), producing intergenerational barriers
  • status:
    • No longer dependent on executive discretion
    • An autonomous constitutional body accountable to the highest constitutional office

Justice Rohini Commission, 2017, under Article 340

  • sub-classification of the 27% OBC quota into 4 groups: G1 2%
  • G2 6%
  • G3 9%
  • G4 10% (total 27%)
  • Final report awaited

Powers (civil court)

  • summon people across India and examine on oath
  • discovery and production of documents
  • Evidence on affidavits
  • Requisition public records
  • summons for witnesses and documents
  • any further powers given by the President

Benefits of constitutional status

  • civil court powers in inquiries
  • mandate beyond list revision: welfare and safeguards
  • parliamentary oversight (e.g. 2022 report on under-used OBC welfare funds)
  • autonomy and tenure protection
  • role in social justice (central PSU backlog)
  • symbolic value for about 40% of the population

Challenges OF SC, ST AND OBC Commissions

Challenges

  • vacancies and resource gaps (2022 panel: NCST under 50% strength)
  • erosion of autonomy: political appointments, non-binding advice (NCBC 2020 backlog reports ignored)
  • weak powers: NCBC cannot define backwardness (Maratha Quota Judgment, 2021)
  • backlog, late reports
  • opaque selection
  • retired bureaucrats dominate
  • low awareness (NCRB 2020: rising atrocities, few complaints)

Reforms

  • independence with clear criteria and experts
  • Binding recommendations
  • NCBC to define backwardness
  • Harmonise powers
  • fill posts, budgets, modern infrastructure
  • digital case tracking, fixed timelines
  • caste and tribe databases, impact studies
  • coordination with ministries, states, NGOs, universities
  • dedicated cadre and training
Memory aid · VAD-OTRCF

Common ills of commissions: Vacancies · Autonomy · Delay · Opaque · Toothless · Representation · Coverage · Funds. (own mnemonic)

Way forward / key point

All Fundamental Rights are Constitutional Rights, but not all Constitutional Rights are Fundamental Rights.

3 · National Human Rights Commission · Composition · Working · Challenges#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 11-16. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • NHRC (Protection of Human Rights Act 1993) is a recommendatory body with civil-court powers, built to Paris Principles. Its weak spots are no binding power, limits on armed forces cases, a one-year bar, and doubts about independence (GANHRI).

Origin AND Structure

Why

  • 1980s-90s concern over custodial violence, encounter deaths, discrimination
  • Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993
  • NHRC set up 12 October 1993
  • three tiers: NHRC (national), SHRCs (state), Human Rights Courts (district)
  • follows Paris Principles (1993)
AspectDetail
CompositionChairperson + 5 full-time members + 7 ex-officio members
Appointment committeePM (chair), Home Minister, Lok Sabha Speaker, RS Deputy Chairman, LoP in both Houses; appointed by the President
Chairpersonretired Chief Justice of India or SC judge
Membersone SC judge (serving/retired); one HC Chief Justice/judge; three with human rights experience (at least one woman)
Ex-officiochairpersons of the national commissions for SC, ST, Women, Minorities, BC, Child Rights and the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities
Term3 years or age 70, whichever earlier; reappointable
Removalby the President for misbehaviour/incapacity after Supreme Court inquiry
Payfixed by Centre, not varied to disadvantage
Powerscivil court powers; call for information; recommend compensation or prosecution; use investigation agencies; Secretary-General has administrative and financial powers
Memory aid · PHLS-LL

Appointment panel: PM · Home Minister · LS Speaker · RS Deputy Chairman · two LoPs. (own mnemonic)

Working

Process

  • Complaint by individual, NGO or suo motu
  • Generally within one year of the violation
  • Screened by the Law Division
  • inquiry: own Investigation Division (DG of Police rank) or report from authority
  • Hearings or field visits in serious cases (custodial deaths, bonded labour, encounters)
  • Recommends compensation, disciplinary action or policy change
  • Action Taken Report within one month
  • Non-compliance shown in annual report tabled in Parliament
  • divisions for research, training, policy, awareness

Functions (Section 12)

  • inquiry (suo motu, petition or court reference; Sections 17-18)
  • intervene in court cases with permission
  • inspect jails and detention centres (UN Mandela Rules 2015)
  • review safeguards
  • examine factors like terrorism, displacement, communal violence
  • Study treaties (UDHR 1948, ICCPR 1966, CEDAW 1979)
  • Work with UNHRC
  • research, education, partner NGOs
  • Monitor Supreme Court orders on custodial deaths, manual scavenging
  • National Action Plan on Business and Human Rights
Note

GANHRI (accredits national human rights bodies): 2024-25 reports flag a non-transparent selection (no public advertisement, vague screening), weak follow-up on serious violations, limited independence, little civil society role; recommendation to downgrade from "A" grade. The handout also says GANHRI downgraded NHRC in 2017 (two statements; verify).

ROLE AND Notable Cases

Role

  • Custodial death guidelines linked to D. K. Basu (1997)
  • Critique of POTA/TADA misuse
  • Odisha starvation deaths (2001-02) leading to scheme monitoring
  • 2022 Chhattisgarh mining displacement
  • NHRC v. State of Arunachal Pradesh (1996):
    • Locus to move court for Chakma refugees
    • Prison reports fed the Justice Amitava Roy Committee
  • Shaped Juvenile Justice Act 2000 and Mental Healthcare Act 2017
  • Online courses (2020)
Case (year)NHRC action
Gujarat riots (2002)suo motu; pressed the State; moved SC
Punjab mass cremations (1995-2012)Rs 1.75 lakh each to 1,051 families
Silicosis, Gujarat (2011-)investigated tribal labourers' conditions
Rohingya, Jammu (2017)basic facilities, non-discrimination
Maharashtra farmer suicides (2018)notices over agrarian distress
Manipur encounters (2013-16)monitored SIT on 1,528 alleged fake encounters; SC called NHRC "toothless tiger"
Covid migrants (2020)reports from states and railways
Hathras (2020)cognisance of alleged gangrape and hurried cremation
Manipur violence (2023)notices on displacement and sexual violence

Limits AND Reform

Challenges

  • Advisory only
  • Cannot penalise or award compensation directly
  • Justice H. L. Dattu (2016) said "toothless tiger"
  • jurisdiction:
    • Armed forces only via report
    • Cases older than one year
    • Private persons unless State complicity
  • composition:
    • Retired judges dominate
    • Limited diversity
    • "post-retirement forum"
  • operations: backlog, deputation staff, funds
  • independence: executive-dominated appointments
  • access: low awareness, urban-centric

Where it failed

  • G. N. Saibaba (2024-25): complaints on prison conditions dismissed
  • "Nata Pratha" minor girl (2024):
    • Police inaction found
    • Slow justice
  • DMF Odisha (2025): reports sought, little visible action

Way forward

  • Binding recommendations in grave cases
  • Cover private actors and security forces
  • Own investigation wing
  • advertised vacancies, diverse membership
  • dedicated budget, permanent staff, regional benches
  • Online tracking of recommendations
  • Audits
  • Parliamentary review
  • Multilingual portals
  • Free legal and medical aid
  • Amend the Act to meet Paris Principles
  • External performance review

4 · UDHR · State Human Rights Commissions · Human Rights Courts#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Low. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Constitutional and Statutory Bodies handout pp. 16-19. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • UDHR (10 Dec 1948, 30 Articles) is echoed in India's Preamble, FRs, DPSPs and duties. States have SHRCs (3-year term after the 2019 amendment) and Human Rights Courts (Sec 30) to try offences, but both are under-used.

UDHR 1948 AND THE Constitution

UDHR

  • adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948 after World War II
  • principles:
    • Dignity, Liberty, Equality, Brotherhood
    • 30 Articles
Indian provisionUDHR link
Preamblejustice, liberty, equality, fraternity
Arts 14-18equality and non-discrimination (UDHR 1, 7)
Arts 19-22freedom, life, liberty (UDHR 3, 9, 19, 20)
Arts 23-24exploitation (UDHR 4)
Arts 25-28religion (UDHR 18)
Arts 29-30cultural and educational rights (UDHR 26, 27)
Art 32remedies (UDHR 8)
DPSP 38, 39, 41, 42, 47socio-economic rights (UDHR 22-25)
Duties 51A(e), (f), (h)harmony and women's dignity, composite culture, scientific temper
Art 21A / 45 / 300A / 326education (UDHR 26) / early childhood care / property (UDHR 17) / adult suffrage (UDHR 21)

State Human Rights Commissions

AspectProvision (post-2019 Amendment)
CompositionChairperson + 2 Members; Chairperson: retired HC Chief Justice or judge (2019 change); Members: serving/retired HC judge or District Judge (7 years), and a human rights expert
AppointmentGovernor on committee headed by the Chief Minister with Speaker, State Home Minister, LoP (and Council Chairman and LoP if bicameral)
ConsultationChief Justice of the HC if a sitting judge is appointed
Tenure3 years or age 70 (cut from 5 years in 2019); reappointable
Removalonly by the President, after SC inquiry
After tenurecannot hold State or Central employment
AdministrationSecretary has administrative and financial powers (2019)

Powers and functions (Section 29)

  • inquiry (suo motu, petition, court)
  • intervene in proceedings
  • inspect jails and mental health institutions
  • review safeguards, structural causes, international norms
  • research, awareness, work with NGOs

Procedure

  • Complaint online/post/in person
  • Media cases suo motu
  • Older than one year ordinarily not admitted
  • Section 17 report from the State
  • Civil court powers (CPC 1908)
  • Section 18 recommend compensation, discipline/prosecution, reforms
  • ATR within one month
  • Section 19 armed/paramilitary forces: report from the Centre, which responds within three months
  • annual report to the State Legislature

Challenges

  • non-binding
  • staff and funds
  • Cannot revisit cases before NHRC
  • One-year bar
  • delayed legislative follow-up

Human Rights Courts (section 30)

Set-up

  • State Government with the Chief Justice of the High Court's concurrence notifies one per district, usually the Sessions Court
  • tries criminal offences from human rights violations or negligence by public servants
  • Special Public Prosecutor (Section 31), advocate with at least 7 years
  • link:
    • SHRC/NHRC investigate and recommend
    • These courts try and punish

Limits and developments

  • courts exist mostly in name:
    • No exclusive judges, infrastructure, funds
    • Little awareness
  • Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Maharashtra have started exclusive benches/pilots
  • 2018 SC order (Re: Inhuman Conditions in 1382 Prisons) asked states to operationalise them
