1 · Introduction and mandate#

The CBI is India's premier national investigative agency, often called the "top crime-fighting institution". It is the nodal agency for complex crimes that cross state boundaries and involve corruption, economic offences or national security.

  • Established 1963 through a Government of India Resolution; draws statutory authority from the Delhi Special Police Establishment (DSPE) Act, 1946.
  • Not a constitutional body: combines police powers with national accountability.
  • Began with corruption in central government offices; now covers financial frauds, terrorism, cybercrimes and inter-state offences.
  • Works under the Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT), Ministry of Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions.
  • Motto: "Industry, Impartiality, Integrity".
  • Debates continue on its autonomy and accountability.
Motto
III

Industry, Impartiality, Integrity: "Investigate, Impartially, with Integrity."

Handout alert

The introduction says the CBI draws its statutory authority from the DSPE Act, 1946, while the Legal Status row says it is "not a constitutional or statutory body by origin" but functions under that Act. Use: created by executive resolution (1963), working under the DSPE Act, not constitutional.

2 · Historical evolution#

From SPE to CBI

1941
Special Police Establishment (SPE) set up by the British Government to investigate corruption in the War and Supply Department during World War II
1946
DSPE Act: transfers superintendence of the SPE to the Home Department and widens its functions to all departments of the Government of India
1962-64
Santhanam Committee on Prevention of Corruption: existing mechanisms inadequate for white-collar crime; recommends a central agency with wider jurisdiction
1 April 1963
MHA Resolution establishes the CBI; inherits DSPE powers; mandate widened to anti-corruption, economic offences and special crimes
Later
Terrorism, financial fraud, narcotics, cybercrime, human trafficking; becomes Interpol's National Central Bureau (NCB) for India
Why it grew

Demand for a professional, credible agency able to investigate crimes across state borders while keeping national integrity and public trust.

Dates
SDSC

SPE 1941, DSPE Act 1946, Santhanam 1962-64, CBI 1963: "Some Detectives Search Clues."

5 · Organisational structure and divisions#

The CBI is a multidisciplinary agency (legal, forensic and administrative expertise) with headquarters in New Delhi and regional offices in all major states. Director, Special/Additional Directors, Joint Directors, DIGs, SPs, Inspectors, Sub-Inspectors and technical experts form the ladder.

6 · Jurisdiction and powers#

Scope

The CBI's jurisdiction covers offences with:

  • Inter-State or international implications;
  • National importance (integrity, security, economic interests);
  • Central Government employees and PSUs (corruption, bribery, misuse of office).

It may investigate: cases notified under the DSPE Act, 1946; offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988; serious economic offences, bank frauds, cybercrimes and smuggling; and any case referred by the Central Government, Supreme Court or High Courts.

Taking over cases

Three routes to a CBI case

State request
A State Government formally asks the Centre to transfer a case to the CBI
Judicial direction
The Supreme Court or a High Court orders a CBI probe in the interest of justice
Central notification
The Centre directs a case of national significance under Section 5 of the DSPE Act
Takeover routes
SJC

State request, Judicial direction, Central notification (Sec. 5): "Someone Just Called."

Powers of investigation

Powers similar to a police officer under the CrPC and the DSPE Act:

  • Search and seizure: search premises, seize evidence, secure documents.
  • Arrest and custody: arrest suspects in registered cases.
  • Summoning and interrogation: summon witnesses, record statements, interrogate the accused.
  • Prosecution: after investigation, prosecutes in Special CBI Courts under the supervision of the Department of Personnel and the CVC.

Territorial limits

  • Jurisdiction in Union Territories is direct.
  • Within states it depends on state consent under Section 6 of the DSPE Act.
  • Where a state has withdrawn general consent, the CBI cannot register new cases but can continue cases already registered before withdrawal (reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in 2020, CBI vs State of Rajasthan).

7 · Prior permission: Sections 6A and 17A#

The prior sanction story

Section 6A DSPE Act (via CVC Act 2003)
No inquiry or investigation under the PC Act against Central officers of Joint Secretary level and above without prior sanction; meant to block "frivolous or motivated" probes
2014
Supreme Court, Dr. Subramanian Swamy v. Director, CBI: Section 6A unconstitutional, violates Article 14 by distinguishing officers on rank
Now
CBI no longer needs prior approval to investigate senior officials in corruption cases
Section 17A (PC Amendment Act 2018)
Prior approval of the appropriate government before inquiry into any public servant for acts in discharge of official duty

Section 6A (struck down 2014)

  • Joint Secretary and above
  • DSPE Act
  • via CVC Act 2003
  • violated Article 14 by rank-based distinction

Section 17A (2018)

  • Any public servant
  • for acts in official duty
  • approval of the appropriate government
  • balances honest decision-making and accountability

Exceptions to approval

  • No prior approval if the accused is caught red-handed while accepting a bribe.
  • In cases referred by courts or constitutional authorities (for example the CVC or Lokpal) the CBI proceeds without further sanction.
Sanction shortcut

6A = rank-based, dead in 2014 (Art. 14). 17A = act-based, alive since 2018. Red-handed and court-referred cases skip approval.

8 · CBI against State Governments#

Law and order is a State subject (List II, Seventh Schedule), but the CBI derives authority from the DSPE Act for offences in Central departments or with inter-state implications. State consent (Section 6) is the precondition for CBI jurisdiction in a state.

General consent

  • Given by a state to investigate all cases within its borders, no per-case permission needed

Specific consent

  • When general consent is withdrawn, the CBI must seek case-by-case approval from the state
Withdrawals

As of 2024, West Bengal, Kerala, Telangana, Punjab, Mizoram and Chhattisgarh have withdrawn general consent, so fresh approval is needed for each investigation.

Judicial interpretation

State of West Bengal v. Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights (2010):

  • Even without state consent, the constitutional courts (SC and HCs) can direct the CBI to investigate under Articles 32 and 226.
  • This flows from the judiciary's duty to enforce fundamental rights and the rule of law.
  • So CBI powers are statutory, but judicial directives can override state consent in exceptional cases.

Coordination

  • Joint Task Forces for inter-state crimes and corruption in PSUs.
  • Periodic MHA-CBI-State DGP meetings on procedures and mutual assistance.
  • Digital complaint and data-sharing portals for inter-jurisdictional investigations.
States that withdrew consent
WKTPMC

West Bengal, Kerala, Telangana, Punjab, Mizoram, Chhattisgarh: "We Keep Thieves Properly Monitored, Closely."

Dual superintendence

The CVC Act, 2003 clarified accountability:

  • Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 cases: CVC exercises superintendence.
  • All other cases (economic offences, bank frauds, special crimes): the Central Government retains supervisory control.
  • The framework followed Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998), which directed institutional safeguards against political interference.
Oversight
AFJ

Administrative (DoPT), Functional (CVC), Judicial (courts): "All File Justly."

10 · Functions and responsibilities#

Investigative functions

Investigative functions
FunctionWhat it covers
Anti-corruptionPrincipal agency under the PC Act, 1988: Central employees and PSUs; MPs, Ministers and senior officials; large-scale procurement, tender and defence corruption; under CVC superintendence
Economic and financial crimesComplex financial frauds, bank scams, securities manipulation, cyber-offences, money laundering, referred by RBI, Ministry of Finance, ED, SFIO, SEBI; often nodal agency for multi-agency coordination
Special crimesTerrorism, organised crime, human trafficking, high-profile murders with inter-state or national ramifications; examples Nirbhaya, 2G Spectrum, Satyam
Court-monitored probesCourts entrust cases when state agencies' impartiality is questioned, supervised by the judiciary; examples Vyapam and Saradha chit fund

Preventive and policy functions

  • Vigilance coordination: advises the CVC and ministries on systemic reforms, vigilance manuals, corruption-risk mapping; preventive vigilance reviews.
  • Capacity building: CBI Academy at Ghaziabad trains CBI officers and state police cadres in digital forensics, interrogation and financial-crime detection.
  • Legal and legislative inputs: assists MHA and the Law Ministry in drafting criminal and anti-corruption laws, notably the Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018.

Advisory and coordinating role

  • Cooperates with Interpol (NCB), State Police, Income Tax Department, Customs and DRI, Lokpal and CVC.
  • Helps frame national crime statistics, best-practice manuals and procedural reforms.

International cooperation

  • Nodal agency for Interpol India: extradition requests, Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties (MLATs), red-corner notices.
  • Represents India at UNODC, Interpol General Assembly and OECD Working Group on Bribery.
  • Investigates foreign bribery, transnational frauds and cross-jurisdiction cybercrime.

Prosecution and judicial interface

  • Prosecutes all registered cases through CBI Special Courts.
  • The Director of Prosecution works independently within the CBI and coordinates with the Directorate of Public Prosecution under the Law Ministry.
  • Assists in appeals and legal opinions in high-profile corruption cases.
