In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • The CBI is India's premier investigating agency for corruption, economic offences and serious crimes. It is not a constitutional body: it was set up on 1 April 1963 by a Ministry of Home Affairs resolution after the Santhanam Committee (1962-64), and it works under the DSPE Act, 1946.
  • Roots:
    • The Special Police Establishment (1941) against wartime corruption, then the DSPE Act 1946. It sits under the DoPT
    • Motto Industry, Impartiality, Integrity
  • The Director is chosen by a three-member committee: PM, Leader of Opposition, Chief Justice of India (or nominee). Tenure is 2 years, extendable one year at a time up to 5 (2021 amendment).
  • For corruption cases (Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988) the CVC superintends the CBI
  • For all other cases the Central Government does. Within a state the CBI needs state consent (Section 6), but the Supreme Court and High Courts can order a CBI probe anyway
  • Section 6A (prior sanction for Joint Secretary and above) was struck down in 2014 (Subramanian Swamy). Today Section 17A (2018) asks for prior approval of the appropriate government for acts done in official duty.

Key terms

Delhi Special Police Establishment Act: the source of the CBI's powers; Section 6 is state consent, Section 5 central notification

State's blanket permission for CBI to investigate in its territory

1 · Introduction and mandate#

  • The CBI is India's premier national investigative agency, often called the "top crime-fighting institution". It is the nodal agency for complex crimes that cross state boundaries and involve corruption, economic offences or national security.
  • Established 1963 through a Government of India Resolution
  • Draws statutory authority from the Delhi Special Police Establishment (DSPE) Act, 1946
  • Not a constitutional body: combines police powers with national accountability.
  • Began with corruption in central government offices
  • Now covers financial frauds, terrorism, cybercrimes and inter-state offences
  • Works under the Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT), Ministry of Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions.
  • Motto: "Industry, Impartiality, Integrity".
  • Debates continue on its autonomy and accountability.
  • Memory aid (Motto): Industry, Impartiality, Integrity: "Investigate, Impartially, with Integrity."

2 · Historical evolution#

From SPE to CBI

  • 1941: Special Police Establishment (SPE) set up by the British Government to investigate corruption in the War and Supply Department during World War II
  • 1946: DSPE Act: transfers superintendence of the SPE to the Home Department and widens its functions to all departments of the Government of India
  • 1962-64:
    • Santhanam Committee on Prevention of Corruption: existing mechanisms inadequate for white-collar crime
    • Recommends a central agency with wider jurisdiction
  • 1 April 1963:
    • MHA Resolution establishes the CBI
    • Inherits DSPE powers
    • Mandate widened to anti-corruption, economic offences and special crimes
  • Later:
    • Terrorism, financial fraud, narcotics, cybercrime, human trafficking
    • Becomes Interpol's National Central Bureau (NCB) for India
From SPE to CBI
  • Why it grew: Demand for a professional, credible agency able to investigate crimes across state borders while keeping national integrity and public trust.
  • Memory aid (Dates): SPE 1941, DSPE Act 1946, Santhanam 1962-64, CBI 1963: "Some Detectives Search Clues."

6 · Jurisdiction and powers#

Scope

  • The CBI's jurisdiction covers offences with:
  • Inter-State or international implications;
  • National importance (integrity, security, economic interests);
  • Central Government employees and PSUs (corruption, bribery, misuse of office).
  • It may investigate:
    • Cases notified under the DSPE Act, 1946
    • Offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988
    • Serious economic offences, bank frauds, cybercrimes and smuggling
    • And any case referred by the Central Government, Supreme Court or High Courts

Taking over cases

Three routes to a CBI case

  • State request: A State Government formally asks the Centre to transfer a case to the CBI
  • Judicial direction: The Supreme Court or a High Court orders a CBI probe in the interest of justice
  • Central notification: The Centre directs a case of national significance under Section 5 of the DSPE Act
  • Memory aid (Takeover routes): State request, Judicial direction, Central notification (Sec. 5): "Someone Just Called."

Powers of investigation

  • Powers similar to a police officer under the CrPC and the DSPE Act:
  • Search and seizure: search premises, seize evidence, secure documents.
  • Arrest and custody: arrest suspects in registered cases.
  • Summoning and interrogation: summon witnesses, record statements, interrogate the accused.
  • Prosecution: after investigation, prosecutes in Special CBI Courts under the supervision of the Department of Personnel and the CVC.

Territorial limits

  • Jurisdiction in Union Territories is direct.
  • Within states it depends on state consent under Section 6 of the DSPE Act.
  • Where a state has withdrawn general consent, the CBI cannot register new cases but can continue cases already registered before withdrawal (reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in 2020, CBI vs State of Rajasthan).

  • Territorial Limitations The CBI’s powers are not automatically applicable throughout India.

7 · Prior permission: Sections 6A and 17A#

The prior sanction story

  • Section 6A DSPE Act (via CVC Act 2003):
    • No inquiry or investigation under the PC Act against Central officers of Joint Secretary level and above without prior sanction
    • Meant to block "frivolous or motivated" probes
  • 2014: Supreme Court, Dr. Subramanian Swamy v. Director, CBI: Section 6A unconstitutional, violates Article 14 by distinguishing officers on rank
  • Now: CBI no longer needs prior approval to investigate senior officials in corruption cases
  • Section 17A (PC Amendment Act 2018): Prior approval of the appropriate government before inquiry into any public servant for acts in discharge of official duty
The prior sanction story

Section 6A against Section 17A

  • Section 6A (struck down 2014):
    • Joint Secretary and above
    • DSPE Act
    • Via CVC Act 2003
    • Violated Article 14 by rank-based distinction
  • Section 17A (2018):
    • Any public servant
    • For acts in official duty
    • Approval of the appropriate government
    • Balances honest decision-making and accountability

Exceptions to approval

  • No prior approval if the accused is caught red-handed while accepting a bribe.
  • In cases referred by courts or constitutional authorities (for example the CVC or Lokpal) the CBI proceeds without further sanction.
  • Shortcut: Sanction shortcut. 6A = rank-based, dead in 2014 (Art. 14). 17A = act-based, alive since 2018. Red-handed and court-referred cases skip approval.

8 · CBI against State Governments#

  • Law and order is a State subject (List II, Seventh Schedule), but the CBI derives authority from the DSPE Act for offences in Central departments or with inter-state implications. State consent (Section 6) is the precondition for CBI jurisdiction in a state.

General against specific consent

  • General consent: Given by a state to investigate all cases within its borders, no per-case permission needed
  • Specific consent: When general consent is withdrawn, the CBI must seek case-by-case approval from the state
  • Withdrawals: As of 2024, West Bengal, Kerala, Telangana, Punjab, Mizoram and Chhattisgarh have withdrawn general consent, so fresh approval is needed for each investigation.

Judicial interpretation

  • State of West Bengal v. Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights (2010):
  • Even without state consent, the constitutional courts (SC and HCs) can direct the CBI to investigate under Articles 32 and 226.
  • This flows from the judiciary's duty to enforce fundamental rights and the rule of law.
  • So CBI powers are statutory, but judicial directives can override state consent in exceptional cases.

Dual superintendence

  • The CVC Act, 2003 clarified accountability:
  • Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 cases: CVC exercises superintendence.
  • All other cases (economic offences, bank frauds, special crimes): the Central Government retains supervisory control.
  • The framework followed Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998), which directed institutional safeguards against political interference.
  • Memory aid (Oversight): Administrative (DoPT), Functional (CVC), Judicial (courts): "All File Justly."

Preventive and policy functions

  • Vigilance coordination:
    • Advises the CVC and ministries on systemic reforms, vigilance manuals, corruption-risk mapping
    • Preventive vigilance reviews
  • Capacity building: CBI Academy at Ghaziabad trains CBI officers and state police cadres in digital forensics, interrogation and financial-crime detection.
  • Legal and legislative inputs: assists MHA and the Law Ministry in drafting criminal and anti-corruption laws, notably the Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018.

Advisory and coordinating role

  • Cooperates with Interpol (NCB), State Police, Income Tax Department, Customs and DRI, Lokpal and CVC.
  • Helps frame national crime statistics, best-practice manuals and procedural reforms.

International cooperation

  • Nodal agency for Interpol India: extradition requests, Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties (MLATs), red-corner notices.
  • Represents India at UNODC, Interpol General Assembly and OECD Working Group on Bribery.
  • Investigates foreign bribery, transnational frauds and cross-jurisdiction cybercrime.

Prosecution and judicial interface

  • Prosecutes all registered cases through CBI Special Courts.
  • The Director of Prosecution works independently within the CBI and coordinates with the Directorate of Public Prosecution under the Law Ministry.
  • Assists in appeals and legal opinions in high-profile corruption cases.

Also in the handout

  • Subsequent Developments and Diversification - Over the decades, the CBI evolved beyond its initial anti-corruption focus to become a multi-faceted investigative organisation.
  • It also participates in preventive vigilance reviews and inter-departmental audits to identify procedural loopholes.
  • The system leverages machine learning to identify red flags in tendering, PSU contracts, and procurement patterns.
  • This cross-regulatory coordination strengthens financial vigilance and reduces investigation latency.

    • Fiscal and economic laws (export-import control, customs, central excise, income tax, foreign exchange), taken up in consultation with or at the request of the department
    • Coordination of anti-corruption agencies and state police
    • Taking up cases of public importance on a state's request
    • Keeping crime statistics
  • Murder, kidnapping and rape are taken up on a state's reference or when the SC or a High Court directs.
  • CBI versus state police:
    • The DSPE takes cases mainly about Central Government affairs or employees (even if state employees are involved), plus employees of Central public undertakings and statutory bodies
    • The state police take cases mainly about state affairs
  • Lokpal Act, 2013 changes:
    • A Directorate of Prosecution (headed by a Director under the CBI Director, appointed on the CVC's recommendation) for Lokpal cases
    • Officers of SP rank and above are appointed on the recommendation of a committee of the Central Vigilance Commissioner, Vigilance Commissioners, Home Secretary and DoPT Secretary
  • The DSPE (Amendment) Act, 2014: if there is no recognised Leader of Opposition, the leader of the single largest opposition party in the Lok Sabha sits on the Director's selection committee.
  • Lakshmikanth differs: it states plainly that the CBI is not a statutory body (powers from the DSPE Act, set up by 1963 resolution). Use that line for the exam.
  • Lakshmikanth differs: its mission is 'to uphold the Constitution and law through in-depth investigation and successful prosecution, give leadership to police forces and be the nodal agency for inter-state and international cooperation', with a nine-point vision. The handout lists a five-point mission (professionalism, integrity, public trust, innovation, coordination, updated 2022). Use the handout in class.

Recall sheet#

Cover the right column and answer. Same questions as the flashcards.

Is the CBI constitutional or statutory?
Not constitutional; created by MHA Resolution 1 April 1963; works under the DSPE Act 1946 (handout also says "not statutory by origin").
Under which ministry does the CBI work?
DoPT, Ministry of Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions.
CBI motto?
Industry, Impartiality, Integrity.
What was the SPE?
Special Police Establishment, 1941, British; corruption in the War and Supply Department.
What did the DSPE Act 1946 do?
Moved SPE superintendence to the Home Department and widened its functions to all departments.
Which committee led to the CBI?
Santhanam Committee on Prevention of Corruption (1962-64).
Who selects the CBI Director?
Committee of PM (chair), Leader of Opposition in Lok Sabha, and CJI or nominee, under Section 4A DSPE Act.
Tenure of the Director?
Two years, extendable year by year up to five (DSPE Amendment Act 2021).
Who can remove the Director?
Only the Central Government, on the recommendation of the same committee (Section 4B and Vineet Narain).
Director's service conditions?
Same as a State DGP; cannot be varied to his disadvantage.
Which Directors need ACC approval?
Special, Additional and Joint Directors.
Name the main divisions.
Anti-Corruption, Economic Offences, Special Crimes, Policy and Coordination (plus Prosecution and Technical and Forensic in the divisions section; Administration in the legal-status table).
Three routes for the CBI to take over a case?
State request; SC or HC direction; Central notification under Section 5 DSPE Act.
What is general consent?
A state's blanket permission for the CBI to investigate all cases within it; if withdrawn, case-by-case (specific) consent is needed.
Which states withdrew general consent (2024)?
West Bengal, Kerala, Telangana, Punjab, Mizoram, Chhattisgarh.
After withdrawal of consent, can the CBI continue earlier cases?
Yes, cases registered before withdrawal (CBI vs State of Rajasthan, 2020); it cannot register new ones.
What did West Bengal v. CPDR (2010) hold?
Courts (SC and HC) can direct a CBI probe under Articles 32 and 226 even without state consent.
What was Section 6A?
Prior sanction for probes against Joint Secretary and above, via the CVC Act 2003; struck down in 2014 under Article 14 (Subramanian Swamy).
What is Section 17A?
PC Amendment Act 2018: prior approval of the appropriate government before inquiry into acts in official duty.
Exceptions to prior approval?
Caught red-handed taking a bribe; cases referred by courts or constitutional authorities such as the CVC and Lokpal.
Who supervises the CBI?
DoPT (administrative); CVC (PC Act cases); Central Government (other cases); courts (judicial review).
Which case led to dual superintendence?
Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998), with the CVC Act 2003.
CBI role with Interpol?
National Central Bureau: extradition, MLATs, red-corner notices.
Where is the CBI Academy?
Ghaziabad.
Which 2021 reform changed the Director's tenure?
DSPE (Amendment) Act 2021: up to 5 years in three one-year extensions, with annual ACC approval.
Name three 2023 developments.
CFCC under MHA; Project CHAKRAVYUH for Red Corner Notices; AI and Data Analytics Division.
What does the Inter-Agency Coordination Group include?
CBI, ED, SFIO, NIA and DRI (formed 2022).

Traps#

Trap 1 · Statutory or not The handout says the CBI draws its authority from the DSPE Act, but also that it is not a constitutional or statutory body by origin: it was created by a 1963 resolution. Rule: not constitutional, and born by executive resolution; its powers come from the DSPE Act.
Trap 2 · Section 6A, Section 6 and Section 17A 6A was prior sanction for senior officers (struck down in 2014, Art. 14). Section 6 is state consent. Section 17A (2018) needs prior approval for acts done in official duty. Rule: 6A gone, 6 = state consent, 17A = current approval rule; no approval if caught red-handed.
Trap 3 · Who supervises the CBI The CVC superintends only corruption cases under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988. Other cases (economic offences, bank frauds, special crimes) stay under the Central Government. Rule: dual superintendence after Vineet Narain and the CVC Act, 2003.
Trap 4 · Withdrawn state consent is not the end With general consent withdrawn, the CBI cannot register new cases there but continues those already registered (CBI v. State of Rajasthan, 2020). Constitutional courts can still direct a CBI probe under Arts. 32 and 226 (West Bengal v. Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights, 2010). Rule: consent binds the CBI, not the courts.
