1 · The doctrine in a nutshell#

The Basic Structure Doctrine is one of the most significant judicial innovations in Indian constitutional law. Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution but cannot alter its fundamental identity or framework. It safeguards the ideals, spirit and philosophy on which the Constitution rests.

  • Propounded by the Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973).
  • The Court held that Article 368 confers a limited power of amendment: Parliament can change the Constitution but cannot destroy its "basic structure".
  • The doctrine balances Parliament's authority against the supremacy of the Constitution.
One line

Amend: yes. Destroy the basic structure: no.

Story of the doctrine in five steps

1951-65
Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh: Parliament may amend any part including Fundamental Rights
1967
Golaknath: Fundamental Rights cannot be amended
1971-72

2 · Before Kesavananda: three cases#

The idea of limiting Parliament's amending power did not appear suddenly. It grew through early judgments on the true scope of Article 368.

The pre-Kesavananda cases
CaseAmendment challengedIssueHeld
Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951)First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951 (curtailed the Right to Property, then a Fundamental Right, to help land reform laws)Does the amending power under Article 368 extend to Fundamental Rights?Yes. Parliament can amend any part including Fundamental Rights. "Law" in Article 13(2) means only ordinary legislation, not constitutional amendments
Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965)17th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1964 (again property rights)Extent of Parliament's amending powerEarlier view reaffirmed (any part including Fundamental Rights). Justices Hidayatullah and Mudholkar feared there might be basic features Parliament cannot alter
I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)Not named in the handout (Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh overturned)Can Fundamental Rights be amended?No. Eleven-judge bench. FRs are transcendental and immutable. A constitutional amendment is also "law" under Article 13(2), so one that curtails FRs is void

What each case did

  • Shankari Prasad: an amendment under Article 368 could not be challenged as violating Fundamental Rights. Parliament's power looked unlimited at that stage.
  • Sajjan Singh: the minority doubt was not the majority view, but it was the first judicial indication of inherent limits on the amending power.
  • Golaknath: overturned Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh. It restricted Parliament considerably and created a constitutional impasse, because the government found it hard to carry out socio-economic reforms touching property rights.

  • Parliament's power unlimited

Golaknath (1967)

Yes, Yes, No: Shankari Prasad yes, Sajjan Singh yes, Golaknath no (to amending FRs). Then Kesavananda says: yes, but not the basic structure.

3 · Parliament's reaction to Golaknath#

Golaknath created tension between legislature and judiciary. The government believed the interpretation made land redistribution and removal of inequalities impossible, so Parliament restored its authority by amendment.

Three amendments
AmendmentWhat it did
24th Amendment Act, 1971Reaffirmed Parliament's power to amend any provision including Fundamental Rights. A constitutional amendment is not a "law" under Article 13, so it cannot be struck down for violating FRs
25th Amendment Act, 1971Curtailed the right to property. Gave precedence to certain Directive Principles over Fundamental Rights. Introduced Article 31C: protects laws implementing Articles 39(b) and 39(c) (equitable distribution of resources) from judicial review
29th Amendment Act, 1972Placed two Kerala land reform laws in the Ninth Schedule, making them immune from judicial challenge
Which number does what
ACK

Amend power = 24th, C31C = 25th, Kerala laws in Ninth Schedule = 29th: "All Cows Keep calm" (24 amend, 25 property and 31C, 29 Kerala).

Number shortcut

24 = "amend anything, not a law". 25 = "31C, 39(b) and 39(c)". 29 = "Kerala, Ninth Schedule".

4 · The Kesavananda milestone#

By the early 1970s the conflict between Parliament and the judiciary was at its peak. Parliament had passed the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments. Kesavananda Bharati, head of the Edneer Mutt in Kerala, challenged them, arguing they violated Fundamental Rights.

Two questions before the Court

  • Does Parliament's power under Article 368 have any limits?
  • Can Parliament alter or destroy the essential features of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights?

The judgment

  • A 13-judge Bench, the largest in India's judicial history, gave a narrow 7:6 verdict.
  • It upheld the 24th Amendment: Parliament may amend any part including Fundamental Rights.
  • But it added the limit: the amending power does not include the power to alter or destroy the basic structure.
  • An amendment is not the same as an ordinary law under Article 13(2).

Legislative power

  • Makes laws for the country
  • ordinary laws can be struck down if they violate Fundamental Rights

Constituent power

  • Amends the Constitution
  • amendments are valid only if they respect the basic structure

Reasoning in Kesavananda

Parliament has two powers
legislative and constituent
Amendment is not "law" under Art. 13(2)
so the 24th Amendment stands
But constituent power is limited
cannot alter essential identity or framework
Result
Basic Structure Doctrine, still guiding Indian constitutionalism
Numbers to fix

13 judges, 7:6, 1973, cut-off date 24 April 1973 (used later by Waman Rao and I.R. Coelho).

Kesavananda numbers
TS

5 · After Kesavananda: consolidation#

The declaration that Parliament's amending power is not absolute became a turning point. Later cases and amendments refined the doctrine, reflecting the struggle between Parliamentary Supremacy and Judicial Review.

(a) Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)

  • After the 1971 general elections, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi was accused of unfair electoral practices. The Allahabad High Court declared her election void.
  • During the appeal, Parliament passed the 39th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975, inserting Article 329A: elections of the Prime Minister, President, Vice-President and Speaker of the Lok Sabha could not be challenged in any court.
  • The Supreme Court struck it down as violating the Basic Structure. Free and fair elections and judicial review are essential to democracy.
  • It was the first practical application of the doctrine to strike down a constitutional amendment. No individual, not even the Prime Minister, is above the Constitution or law.

(b) The 42nd Amendment Act (1976)

  • Passed during the Emergency (1975-77); called the "Mini-Constitution" for its extensive changes.
  • Inserted Clauses (4) and (5) in Article 368: no amendment can be questioned in any court on any ground, and there is no limitation whatsoever on Parliament's power to amend.
  • An attempt to give Parliament absolute amending power, nullifying the doctrine.

(c) Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

  • Challenged the 42nd Amendment, especially Clauses (4) and (5) of Article 368.
  • The Court struck both down: Parliament's amending power is not unlimited, and a limited amending power is itself part of the Basic Structure.
  • Restored balance: Fundamental Rights and DPSPs must coexist harmoniously, neither overriding the other.
  • Judicial review is an integral part of the Basic Structure.

(d) Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)

  • Concerned the Ninth Schedule, a list of laws placed beyond judicial review.
  • The doctrine applies to all constitutional amendments made after 24 April 1973 (the date of Kesavananda).
  • This is prospective application: continuity is kept while judicial review is preserved.

(e) S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

  • Arose from misuse of Article 356 (President's Rule) in several states.
  • Federalism is a basic feature and cannot be destroyed even by constitutional amendment. States have an independent constitutional existence and are not mere agents of the Centre.
  • The proclamation of President's Rule is not immune from judicial scrutiny. The President's satisfaction is reviewable if mala fide or based on irrelevant grounds.
  • Basic structure includes secularism, democracy, federalism, unity and integrity, and judicial review.

(f) I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

  • A nine-judge bench revisiting the Ninth Schedule.
  • No blanket immunity from judicial review for Ninth Schedule laws. Laws inserted after 24 April 1973 can be challenged if they violate Fundamental Rights forming part of the Basic Structure, especially Articles 14, 19 and 21.
  • Applied the "rights test" and the "essence of the right test": a law that damages or destroys the core of these rights is invalid even though protected by Article 31B.

Post-1973 cases in order
IMWBC

Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975), Minerva Mills (1980), Waman Rao (1981), Bommai (1994), Coelho (2007): "I Met Wise Boys Confidently".

Which Amendment, which Article

39th = 329A (election of PM, President, VP, Speaker beyond courts). 42nd = 368(4) and (5) (no review, no limit). Both were struck down: 39th in Raj Narain, 42nd's clauses in Minerva Mills.

Ninth Schedule pair

Waman Rao = 24 April 1973 cut-off. Coelho = no blanket immunity, rights tests, Articles 14, 19, 21.

Reading the pattern

Each time Parliament tried to place something beyond the courts (329A, 368(4) and (5), the Ninth Schedule), the Court answered with the basic structure and judicial review.

8 · Criticism of the doctrine#

Seven criticisms
No.CriticismArgument
1Judicial overreachUnelected judges strike down amendments passed by an elected Parliament. Tension between judicial review and democratic will
2No constitutional basisThe Constitution nowhere mentions "basic structure". Born of interpretation in Kesavananda (1973): an extra-constitutional innovation, absent from the intent of the Constituent Assembly
3Rewriting the ConstitutionBy finding a "law within the law", the Court acts like a constituent assembly and becomes a third legislative chamber
4Against the principle of majorityAmendments need special majorities, yet a small bench can overrule them. The Court justifies this as a counter-majoritarian safeguard
5Decision by narrow marginBorn of a 7-6 judgment on a 13-judge Bench: a sweeping revision on a narrow majority, limited democratic confidence
6Judicial subjectivityNo fixed list of basic features. Inclusion depends on judges' interpretation: uncertainty, personal bias, variation from bench to bench
7Institutional tensionFrequent invalidation fuels friction between organs, seems to undermine parliamentary sovereignty and blurs separation of powers
Seven criticisms
OBRMNSI

Overreach, Basis missing, Rewriting, Majority principle, Narrow margin, Subjectivity, Institutional tension: "Old Books Reveal Many Narrow Stories Indeed".

Group the seven

Power (1, 3) + Legitimacy (2, 4, 5) + Uncertainty and friction (6, 7).

9 · Contemporary significance#

Seven points of significance
RoleWhat it does
Guardian of constitutional spiritSafeguards democracy, secularism and rule of law. Parliament cannot alter the essential identity or moral foundations
Judicial check on absolute powerParliament can amend but not re-draft the Constitution. Rejects any absolute or unlimited constituent power
Balance between flexibility and stabilityEquilibrium between change and permanence, "privileging uncertain democracy over certain tyranny"
Protection of Fundamental RightsShield for dignity and liberty. Prevents transient political majorities abridging freedoms in Part III
Preservation of separation of powers and federalismPrevents one organ encroaching on another and protects State autonomy in India's cooperative federal framework
Global influence and legacyLogic resonates globally: cited by courts in Bangladesh, Uganda, and echoed in Germany's Basic Law under Article 79
Enduring relevanceThe Constitution's "conscience": temporary urges of majorities cannot override the framers' permanent vision. Preserves India's liberal-democratic identity
Significance in order
GJBPPGE

Guardian of spirit, Judicial check, Balance flexibility-stability, Protect FRs, Preserve separation and federalism, Global influence, Enduring relevance: "Good Judges Balance Power, Protect Generations Eternally".
