In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • The Basic Structure Doctrine says Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, but it cannot destroy its basic structure (its core identity).
  • The Supreme Court gave this in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), a 13-judge Bench, the largest ever, with a narrow 7:6 verdict.
  • Earlier, Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965) said Parliament could amend even Fundamental Rights. Golaknath (1967) reversed this.
  • Parliament hit back with the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments and later the 42nd Amendment. The Court answered in Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975) and Minerva Mills (1980).
  • There is no fixed list of basic features. The Court adds them case by case, and critics call this judicial overreach.

Key terms

Basic structure
The essential identity and framework of the Constitution that Parliament cannot alter or destroy (Kesavananda, 1973).
Constituent power
Parliament's special power to amend the Constitution under Article 368, different from its ordinary law-making power.
Article 13(2)
Bars laws that violate Fundamental Rights. Golaknath said an amendment is a 'law' here; Kesavananda said it is not an ordinary law.
Article 31C
Inserted by the 25th Amendment (1971); protected laws implementing Articles 39(b) and 39(c) from judicial challenge.
Ninth Schedule / Article 31B
List of laws placed beyond judicial review; the 29th Amendment added two Kerala land reform laws.
Prospective application
Waman Rao (1981): the doctrine applies to amendments made after 24 April 1973.
Rights test, essence test
Used in I.R. Coelho (2007): a Ninth Schedule law is invalid if it destroys the core of Articles 14, 19, 21.
Mini-Constitution
Nickname of the 42nd Amendment (1976), passed during the Emergency.

1 · The doctrine in a nutshell#

  • The Basic Structure Doctrine is one of the most significant judicial innovations in Indian constitutional law. Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution but cannot alter its fundamental identity or framework. It safeguards the ideals, spirit and philosophy on which the Constitution rests.
  • Propounded by the Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973).
  • The Court held that Article 368 confers a limited power of amendment: Parliament can change the Constitution but cannot destroy its "basic structure".
  • The doctrine balances Parliament's authority against the supremacy of the Constitution.
  • One line: Amend: yes. Destroy the basic structure: no.

Story of the doctrine in five steps

  • 1951-65: Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh: Parliament may amend any part including Fundamental Rights
  • 1967: Golaknath: Fundamental Rights cannot be amended
  • 1971-72: Parliament answers with the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments
  • 1973: Kesavananda: amend yes, destroy the basic structure no
  • 1975-2007: Raj Narain, Minerva Mills, Waman Rao, Bommai, Coelho strengthen the doctrine

Also in the handout

  • To restore its authority, Parliament responded with a series of constitutional amendments.

2 · Before Kesavananda: three cases#

  • The idea of limiting Parliament's amending power did not appear suddenly. It grew through early judgments on the true scope of Article 368.

The pre-Kesavananda cases

What each case did

  • Shankari Prasad: an amendment under Article 368 could not be challenged as violating Fundamental Rights. Parliament's power looked unlimited at that stage.
  • Sajjan Singh: the minority doubt was not the majority view, but it was the first judicial indication of inherent limits on the amending power.
  • Golaknath: overturned Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh. It restricted Parliament considerably and created a constitutional impasse, because the government found it hard to carry out socio-economic reforms touching property rights.

Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh versus Golaknath

  • Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh:
    • Amending power reaches Fundamental Rights
    • Amendment is not "law" under Art. 13(2)
    • Parliament's power unlimited
  • Golaknath (1967):
    • Fundamental Rights immutable
    • Amendment is "law" under Art. 13(2)
    • Amendment curtailing FRs is void
    • 11 judges
  • Memory aid (Case order): Shankari Prasad (1951), Sajjan Singh (1965), Golaknath (1967), Kesavananda (1973): "Some Students Got Knowledge".

3 · Parliament's reaction to Golaknath#

  • Golaknath created tension between legislature and judiciary. The government believed the interpretation made land redistribution and removal of inequalities impossible, so Parliament restored its authority by amendment.

4 · The Kesavananda milestone#

  • By the early 1970s the conflict between Parliament and the judiciary was at its peak. Parliament had passed the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments. Kesavananda Bharati, head of the Edneer Mutt in Kerala, challenged them, arguing they violated Fundamental Rights.

Two questions before the Court

  • Does Parliament's power under Article 368 have any limits?
  • Can Parliament alter or destroy the essential features of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights?

The judgment

  • A 13-judge Bench, the largest in India's judicial history, gave a narrow 7:6 verdict.
  • It upheld the 24th Amendment: Parliament may amend any part including Fundamental Rights.
  • But it added the limit: the amending power does not include the power to alter or destroy the basic structure.
  • An amendment is not the same as an ordinary law under Article 13(2).

Parliament's two functions

  • Legislative power:
    • Makes laws for the country
    • Ordinary laws can be struck down if they violate Fundamental Rights
  • Constituent power:
    • Amends the Constitution
    • Amendments are valid only if they respect the basic structure

Reasoning in Kesavananda

  • Parliament has two powers: legislative and constituent
  • Amendment is not "law" under Art. 13(2): so the 24th Amendment stands
  • But constituent power is limited: cannot alter essential identity or framework
  • Result: Basic Structure Doctrine, still guiding Indian constitutionalism
Reasoning in Kesavananda
  • Numbers to fix: 13 judges, 7:6, 1973, cut-off date 24 April 1973 (used later by Waman Rao and I.R. Coelho).
  • Memory aid (Kesavananda numbers): Thirteen judges, Seven to six: "Thirteen Sages split seven-six".
Handout alert

Handout alert: The handout numbers the two issues as "10" and "11" (a printing slip). There are only two questions. The Golaknath bench was 11 judges and Kesavananda's was 13: do not mix them.

Also in the handout

  • It explained that the Indian Parliament performs two distinct functions: (a) it makes laws for the country by using its legislative power, and (b) it amends the Constitution by using its constituent power.
  • This distinction between the two powers ensures that the process of amending the Constitution remains special and subject to constitutional limits. This historic ruling came to be known as the Basic Structure Doctrine, a principle that continues to guide Indian constitutionalism to this day 3.

5 · After Kesavananda: consolidation#

  • The declaration that Parliament's amending power is not absolute became a turning point. Later cases and amendments refined the doctrine, reflecting the struggle between Parliamentary Supremacy and Judicial Review.

(a) Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)

  • After the 1971 general elections, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi was accused of unfair electoral practices. The Allahabad High Court declared her election void.
  • During the appeal, Parliament passed the 39th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975, inserting Article 329A: elections of the Prime Minister, President, Vice-President and Speaker of the Lok Sabha could not be challenged in any court.
  • The Supreme Court struck it down as violating the Basic Structure. Free and fair elections and judicial review are essential to democracy.
  • It was the first practical application of the doctrine to strike down a constitutional amendment. No individual, not even the Prime Minister, is above the Constitution or law.

(b) The 42nd Amendment Act (1976)

  • Passed during the Emergency (1975-77)
  • Called the "Mini-Constitution" for its extensive changes
  • Inserted Clauses (4) and (5) in Article 368: no amendment can be questioned in any court on any ground, and there is no limitation whatsoever on Parliament's power to amend.
  • An attempt to give Parliament absolute amending power, nullifying the doctrine.

(c) Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

  • Challenged the 42nd Amendment, especially Clauses (4) and (5) of Article 368.
  • The Court struck both down: Parliament's amending power is not unlimited, and a limited amending power is itself part of the Basic Structure.
  • Restored balance: Fundamental Rights and DPSPs must coexist harmoniously, neither overriding the other.
  • Judicial review is an integral part of the Basic Structure.

(d) Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)

  • Concerned the Ninth Schedule, a list of laws placed beyond judicial review.
  • The doctrine applies to all constitutional amendments made after 24 April 1973 (the date of Kesavananda).
  • This is prospective application: continuity is kept while judicial review is preserved.

(e) S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

  • Arose from misuse of Article 356 (President's Rule) in several states.
  • Federalism is a basic feature and cannot be destroyed even by constitutional amendment. States have an independent constitutional existence and are not mere agents of the Centre.
  • The proclamation of President's Rule is not immune from judicial scrutiny. The President's satisfaction is reviewable if mala fide or based on irrelevant grounds.
  • Basic structure includes secularism, democracy, federalism, unity and integrity, and judicial review.

(f) I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

  • A nine-judge bench revisiting the Ninth Schedule.
  • No blanket immunity from judicial review for Ninth Schedule laws. Laws inserted after 24 April 1973 can be challenged if they violate Fundamental Rights forming part of the Basic Structure, especially Articles 14, 19 and 21.
  • Applied the "rights test" and the "essence of the right test": a law that damages or destroys the core of these rights is invalid even though protected by Article 31B.

8 · Criticism of the doctrine#

Seven criticisms

No.CriticismArgument
1Judicial overreachUnelected judges strike down amendments passed by an elected Parliament. Tension between judicial review and democratic will
2No constitutional basisThe Constitution nowhere mentions "basic structure". Born of interpretation in Kesavananda (1973): an extra-constitutional innovation, absent from the intent of the Constituent Assembly
3Rewriting the ConstitutionBy finding a "law within the law", the Court acts like a constituent assembly and becomes a third legislative chamber
4Against the principle of majorityAmendments need special majorities, yet a small bench can overrule them. The Court justifies this as a counter-majoritarian safeguard
5Decision by narrow marginBorn of a 7-6 judgment on a 13-judge Bench: a sweeping revision on a narrow majority, limited democratic confidence
6Judicial subjectivityNo fixed list of basic features. Inclusion depends on judges' interpretation: uncertainty, personal bias, variation from bench to bench
7Institutional tensionFrequent invalidation fuels friction between organs, seems to undermine parliamentary sovereignty and blurs separation of powers
  • Memory aid (Seven criticisms): Overreach, Basis missing, Rewriting, Majority principle, Narrow margin, Subjectivity, Institutional tension: "Old Books Reveal Many Narrow Stories Indeed".
  • Shortcut: Group the seven. Power (1, 3) + Legitimacy (2, 4, 5) + Uncertainty and friction (6, 7).

  • Golaknath challenged the 17th Amendment (1964), which put certain state acts in the Ninth Schedule.
  • The 24th Amendment (1971) amended both Article 13 and Article 368.
  • Kesavananda Bharati expressly overruled Golaknath. Result today: Parliament can abridge or take away a Fundamental Right only if that right is not part of the basic structure.
  • Minerva Mills reasoning: a body given a limited power cannot turn it into an unlimited power. Parliament cannot repeal or abrogate the Constitution or destroy its basic features.

What does the Basic Structure Doctrine say?
Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution but cannot alter its fundamental identity or framework.
Which case propounded the doctrine?
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973).
Who was Kesavananda Bharati?
Head of the Edneer Mutt in Kerala.
Bench and verdict of Kesavananda?
13 judges, 7:6 (the largest Bench in India's judicial history).
Cut-off date used for Ninth Schedule review?
24 April 1973, the date of Kesavananda.
Shankari Prasad (1951): what held?
Amending power under Art. 368 extends to Fundamental Rights; "law" in Art. 13(2) does not include amendments.
Which amendment was challenged in Shankari Prasad?
The First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951.
Sajjan Singh (1965): amendment and doubt?
17th Amendment (1964). Justices Hidayatullah and Mudholkar doubted that Parliament could alter basic features.
Golaknath (1967): bench and holding?
Eleven judges. Fundamental Rights immutable; an amendment is "law" under Art. 13(2).
What did the 24th Amendment do?
Reaffirmed power to amend any provision including FRs; amendment is not "law" under Article 13.
What did the 25th Amendment do?
Curtailed property right; precedence to certain DPSPs; Article 31C protecting laws under Arts. 39(b) and 39(c).
What did the 29th Amendment do?
Placed two Kerala land reform laws in the Ninth Schedule.
Parliament's two functions per Kesavananda?
Legislative power (makes laws) and constituent power (amends the Constitution).
Raj Narain (1975): what struck down?
Article 329A (39th Amendment), which barred challenges to elections of PM, President, VP and Speaker.
Why was Raj Narain significant?
First practical application of the doctrine to strike down a constitutional amendment.
Which Amendment is the "Mini-Constitution"?
The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976.
What did Article 368(4) and (5) say?
No amendment may be questioned in any court; no limitation on Parliament's power to amend.
What did Minerva Mills hold?
Clauses (4) and (5) void; limited amending power is basic structure; FR-DPSP harmony; judicial review basic.
Waman Rao (1981): holding?
Doctrine applies to amendments after 24 April 1973 (prospective application).
Bommai (1994): key holdings?
Federalism basic; States not mere agents of the Centre; President's Rule reviewable if mala fide or on irrelevant grounds.
Coelho (2007): bench and holding?
Nine judges. No blanket immunity for Ninth Schedule laws after 24 April 1973; rights test and essence of right test (Arts. 14, 19, 21).
Which cases name "judicial review" as basic?
Raj Narain, Minerva Mills, Sampath Kumar, Sambamurthy, Bommai, Coelho, Madras Bar (2014), Union of India v. Madras Bar (2021).
Which case is the NJAC case and what did it declare?
SC Advocates-on-Record Assn. (2015): independence of judiciary, judicial primacy in appointments, separation of powers, judicial accountability.
Puttaswamy (2017) declared what?
Constitutional morality; rule of law; privacy and individual dignity as core.
Name the seven criticisms.
Judicial overreach; no constitutional basis; rewriting the Constitution; against majority principle; narrow margin; judicial subjectivity; institutional tension.
Which foreign courts and law are cited in the significance?
Courts in Bangladesh and Uganda; Germany's Basic Law under Article 79.
Mains 2014: Starting from inventing the ‘basic structure’ doctrine
Intro: The doctrine, born in Kesavananda (1973), made the judiciary the guardian of the Constitution's identity. Body: Raj Narain struck down Article 329A and upheld free and fair elections; Minerva Mills struck down Article 368(4) and (5) and protected judicial review; Bommai made President's Rule reviewable and protected federalism; Coelho denied blanket Ninth Schedule immunity; Puttaswamy and NJAC cases extended the list of features; critics cite overreach and subjectivity. Close: Activism that guards democracy is justified, but it needs restraint to avoid friction between organs.
Mains 2016: What was held in the Coelho case?
Intro: A nine-judge bench revisited the Ninth Schedule and the doctrine in 2007. Body: No blanket immunity for Ninth Schedule laws inserted after 24 April 1973; laws can be challenged if they violate Fundamental Rights in the basic structure especially Articles 14 19 and 21; rights test and essence of the right test; Article 31B protection does not save a law that destroys the core of rights; judicial review also stressed in Raj Narain Minerva Mills Bommai and Madras Bar cases. Close: Judicial review is a key basic feature because it keeps every organ within its constitutional authority.
Mains 2019: “Parliament’s power to amend the constitution is limited power
Intro: Article 368 gives a limited amending power, as held in Kesavananda and Minerva Mills. Body: Parliament can amend any part but cannot alter or destroy the basic structure; constituent power is distinct from legislative power; Article 368(4) and (5) claiming unlimited power were struck down in Minerva Mills; a limited amending power is itself basic structure; amendments after 24 April 1973 can be reviewed. Close: Parliament can amend but not destroy: the amending power cannot be enlarged into absolute power.

Traps#

Trap 1 · Sajjan Singh is not Golaknath In Sajjan Singh (1965) the majority again upheld unlimited amending power. Only Justices Hidayatullah and Mudholkar doubted it, and that was not the majority view. Golaknath (1967), an 11-judge Bench, overturned both earlier cases. Rule: Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh = Parliament can amend FRs. Golaknath = it cannot. Kesavananda = it can, but not the basic structure.
Trap 2 · Kesavananda upheld the 24th Amendment Students think Kesavananda cancelled Parliament's power. It actually upheld the 24th Amendment (Parliament can amend any part, including FRs), and only added the limit of basic structure. It also separated legislative power from constituent power. Rule: power stays, but with one limit: the basic structure.
Trap 3 · Cut-off date for the Ninth Schedule Waman Rao (1981) applied the doctrine only to amendments after 24 April 1973. I.R. Coelho (2007, nine judges) said no blanket immunity: Ninth Schedule laws after that date can be tested against Articles 14, 19, 21. Rule: 24 April 1973 is the date of Kesavananda; laws before it stay protected.
Trap 4 · First strike-down and the 42nd Amendment Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975) was the first practical use of the doctrine, striking Article 329A (39th Amendment). Minerva Mills (1980) struck Clauses (4) and (5) of Article 368 added by the 42nd Amendment. Rule: 1975 = Article 329A struck; 1980 = Article 368(4),(5) struck.

Book edition: simple words and memory words#

In simple words

  • The Basic Structure Doctrine says Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, but it cannot destroy its basic structure (its core identity).
  • The Supreme Court gave this in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), a 13-judge Bench, the largest ever, with a narrow 7:6 verdict.
  • Earlier, Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965) said Parliament could amend even Fundamental Rights. Golaknath (1967) reversed this.
  • Parliament hit back with the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments and later the 42nd Amendment. The Court answered in Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975) and Minerva Mills (1980).
  • There is no fixed list of basic features. The Court adds them case by case, and critics call this judicial overreach.

Key terms

Basic structure
The essential identity and framework of the Constitution that Parliament cannot alter or destroy (Kesavananda, 1973).
Constituent power
Parliament's special power to amend the Constitution under Article 368, different from its ordinary law-making power.
Article 13(2)
Bars laws that violate Fundamental Rights. Golaknath said an amendment is a 'law' here; Kesavananda said it is not an ordinary law.
Article 31C
Inserted by the 25th Amendment (1971); protected laws implementing Articles 39(b) and 39(c) from judicial challenge.
Ninth Schedule / Article 31B
List of laws placed beyond judicial review; the 29th Amendment added two Kerala land reform laws.
Prospective application
Waman Rao (1981): the doctrine applies to amendments made after 24 April 1973.
Rights test, essence test
Used in I.R. Coelho (2007): a Ninth Schedule law is invalid if it destroys the core of Articles 14, 19, 21.
Mini-Constitution
Nickname of the 42nd Amendment (1976), passed during the Emergency.
