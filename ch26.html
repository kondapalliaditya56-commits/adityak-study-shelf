Mains hooks

  • Why did the Moderates fail to carry conviction with the nation about their proclaimed ideology and political goals by the end of the nineteenth century? (10 marks, 150 words; year not given in the bank)

A movement that neither represented the people nor threatened to unleash them could be ignored, yet it built the culture of politics (Gokhale 1913).

The story in one go#

Read it like a film first. Then go to the notes: every fact below is expanded there.

Story spine: The Moderates, shaped by Utilitarian and Liberal faith in laws and constitutions, asked the British for Limited Self-Government by petition and prayer. Because they were a narrow, elite, part-time body that distrusted the masses, their demands carried no pressure and none was met; yet they were building the culture of politics.

  1. Limit the State, by law. In the West the monarchy was limited step by step (Britain without a written Constitution, France with one). In India the opponent was a foreign power, so Step 1 for the Moderates was to limit the colonial State, with a good Constitution as the instrument. They drew on Utilitarianism (the greatest good of the greatest number, through laws) and Liberalism (rights, rule of law, a constitution).
    → So they chose methods that stay inside the State's own rules.
  2. Only the State's own doors. Constitutionalism meant Responsible Government, protected rights and Rule of Law. Constitutional methods meant using only the channels the State provides: elections, the Legislature, the Judiciary, RTI, CPGRAMS, Citizen Charters and petitions (the teacher adds FIR, Court case and NOTA). Gandhi's Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience sit outside, as extra-constitutional mass struggle.
    → But did they want the British gone?
  3. Critics, not rebels. The Moderates knew British rule was exploitative, yet they believed in its Providence: if pressurised, the British could modernise India. Their goal was Limited Self-Government within British rule, because the masses were not yet ready; the long vision was Canada or Australia with British citizenship. Banerjea wanted an end to Un-British Rule, and Bonnerjee called the INC loyal to the British and their well-wishers.
    → What, then, did they actually ask for?
  4. Eleven asks, no answers. Four were political: abolish the 15-member India Council, expand the ILC and PLCs with at least 50% indirectly elected members, give genuine powers, and Indianise the services (exam in India and Britain, age 23). Seven were economic: cut military spending (the Boxer Rebellion 1899-1901), a volunteer army, trial by jury, repeal of the Arms Act 1878, lower land revenue and no Salt Tax, PS 1793 extended, and an end to Assam tea indenture. None was met.
    → Who was asking, and for whom?
  5. Who was in the room. At the first session in 1885, 72 delegates came: Bombay 38, Madras 21, Punjab and NWP and Oudh 10, and only 3 from Bengal-Bihar-Orissa-Assam (the handout says Bengal about 5). They were upper-caste Western-educated professionals; Hindus were 90% and Muslims about 6.5% of the INC against 20% in the census. Tyabji, the first Muslim President (Madras 1887), was the exception.
    → Even that thin unity was guarded by a rule.
  6. The 1886 rule. At the Calcutta session of 1886, under Dadabhai Naoroji, the INC decided to avoid social questions (Hindu-Muslim, upper-lower caste, women, class) until 1907, so that it would not look pro-one-group or split. It would pick only fights where the opponent was a foreigner. In 1893 it stayed silent over the cow-killing riots, and Muslim participation fell.
    → The rule saved unity but left the leaders' own class interests untouched.
  7. Landlords' politics. In 1892-1905 about 20% of members were landlords, 40% lawyers and 15% traders, and the leaders were part-time politicians in a three-day annual conference. So there was no anti-Zamindar stand (they wanted PS 1793 extended), they opposed the Punjab Land Alienation Bill of 1900, and they backed Indian capital over labour (Assam tea only because the owners were European).
    → The same lens shaped their view of the factory laws.
  8. The Lancashire game. British textile makers in Lancashire lobbied for Indian factory rules to raise Indian costs; the Moderates replied that infant industries needed room or India would stay a raw-material supplier. The Acts were the Factories Act 1881 (Ripon 80-84; imp), the Factories Act 1891 (Lansdowne 88-94) and the Indian Mines Act 1901 (Curzon 99-05). The verdict is complex: the lobby's motive was real, but Indian workers, children included, suffered longer.
    → Whatever their reasons, their method was always the same.
  9. Prayer, petition, protest in print. They sent prayers and petitions, passed resolutions at annual sessions, used the press and debates, and avoided all mass action, as firm believers in the Rule of Law. The teacher's 3P is Prayer, Petitions, Protests in the press; critics, later the Extremists, called it the Strategy of Mendicancy.
    → Why did that bring no result?
  10. A sliver with no lever. Two problems reinforced each other: they did not represent the masses (about 90% Hindu, lawyers and landlords) and they distrusted the masses and would not mobilise them. A movement that neither represented the people nor threatened to unleash them could be ignored. The teacher's four power factors: claim, masses, demands, methods.
    → Yet it was not the end of the story.
  11. A beginning, not the end. Gokhale said in 1913 that without these methods the British would not have allowed the INC to function, and that the culture of direct confrontation had not yet developed. The goal itself moved on: Limited Self-Government 1885-1906, Self-Government within the empire 1906, Dominion Status 1916 and 1928, Purna Swaraj 1929.

In simple words#

  • The Moderates drew on Utilitarianism (good laws) and Liberalism (rights), so they trusted constitutional methods and wanted Limited Self-Government within British rule: an end to Un-British Rule, not to British rule.
  • They held the Providence of British Rule: if pressurised, the British could modernise India, and the masses were not yet ready for self-rule.
  • They made 11 demands (India Council out, bigger councils with 50% indirectly elected, Indianisation with age 23, army cost cut, jury, Arms Act 1878, land revenue and salt tax, PS 1793, Assam labour) and none was met.
  • The INC was elite and uneven: 72 delegates in 1885 (Bombay 38), Muslims about 6.5%, and from 1886 it kept social questions off the agenda until 1907.
  • Its leaders were part-time, mostly landlords, lawyers and traders, so it backed PS 1793, opposed the Punjab Land Alienation Bill 1900, and opposed the factory laws (the Lancashire Lobby argument).
  • Their methods were 3P (Prayer, Petitions, Protests in the press) with no mass action: critics called it Mendicancy, but Gokhale 1913 said they were building the culture of politics.

Mains PYQ skeleton: why the Moderates failed to carry conviction (from the notes only)

  • Intro: the Moderates (Utilitarian and Liberal, firm believers in Rule of Law) wanted Limited Self-Government within British rule by petitions, resolutions, the press and debate, and by 1900 their programme had produced none of its 11 demands.
  • Body (the teacher's four power factors):
    • (1) Weak claim to represent Indians: upper-caste Western-educated middle class from Madras, Bombay and Calcutta
    • Hindus 90%, Muslims about 6.5% (census: 20%)
    • Bombay alone about half the members
    • Peasants, workers, women and lower castes absent
    • So less pressure on the British.
  • (2) No trust in the masses:
    • They were not Western-educated and would not understand modern politics
    • Involvement would raise the chance of violence
    • So no mass base.
  • (3) Weak demands: limited self-government, and class-coloured demands (no anti-Zamindar stand, PS 1793, Punjab Land Alienation Bill 1900 opposed, Indian capitalists over labour).
  • (4) Weak methods:
    • 3P (Prayer, Petitions, Protests in the press), gradual, part-time politicians, a 3-day annual drama
    • called the "Strategy of Mendicancy."
  • Closing:
    • A movement that neither represented the people nor threatened to unleash them carried no real pressure
    • The British could ignore a few hundred professionals
  • Fair verdict (Gokhale 1913): they were building the institutions and culture of politics for a country with no national organisation.

1 · Moderates' ideology: the intellectual foundation#

Story link

Because the opponent was a foreign State, the Moderates wanted to limit its power with laws and a constitution. Ask: which two Western traditions and which kind of methods followed from that?

1.1 Why the Moderates wanted to limit the State: the class board

Teacher's board
  • West: the State was an indigenous monarchy, so democracy grew gradually: power of the Monarch down, power of Parliament up.
  • Nature of State = monarchy, so limiting the monarchy = limiting the State.
  • How:
    • Britain without a Constitution, France with a Constitution
    • both ended with a limited State.
  • India (and USA): the opponent was the imperialism of a foreign power.
  • Step 1 for the Moderates = limit the power of the colonial State
  • instrument = a Good Constitution, so constitutional development.
  • The Moderates' ideology centres on constitutions, laws and rights.

1.2 Two Western traditions: Utilitarianism and Liberalism

  • The Moderates were shaped by two Western philosophical traditions:

  • Isms the student already knows from Chapter 15: Orientalism, Authoritarianism, Racism, Liberalism, Utilitarianism, and evangelicalism (Christianise India).
  • Only Liberalism and Utilitarianism are the Moderates' two.
  • Utilitarianism = Good Laws, Rule of Law + efficiency.
  • Liberalism = opposed to Authoritarianism
  • "Give Best To India" in Polity, Eco(nomy) and Society.
Teacher's board

Ideology tree: Utilitarianism = "Good Laws / Rule of Law / Law = Agent of Good Change [delta sign]"; Liberalism = [anti-Colonialism]. Utilitarianism is "linked" to Constitutionalism.

1.3 Constitutionalism

  • From the isms grew the Moderates' belief in Constitutionalism: a legitimate State should
  • provide Responsible Government,
  • protect the rights of citizens, and
  • operate under the Rule of Law.
  • (Think of Constitutionalism as the values reflected in our Preamble, the Basic Structure doctrine and the Directive Principles of State Policy.)
Teacher's board
  • Constitutionalism = limiting the power of the State via a Constitution.
  • Personal reading:
    • When the "primary" purpose of the COI is said to be limiting the power of the (Nation) State, "for me: this shows distrust." India after COI 1950: distinguish Nation and Nation State
    • Constitutionalism = Ideal Democracy with Rule of Law, limiting State power is automatically covered
  • Values: Preamble + FRs + Basic Structure + DPSP.
  • Also on the Ideology tree:
    • Secularism and National Unity
    • Constitutional methods.

1.4 Two terms the teacher defines: Responsible Government and Constitutional methods

Teacher's board
  • Responsible Government = part of Constitutionalism = accountability of the Executive (E).
  • Operationalised as E responsible to the Legislature (L) / Parliament.
  • Test:
    • A Minister has to be an MP or MLA first
    • If yes, Parliament (P) is the mother of the Executive (E).
  • A child is always accountable to its mother, including elimination (no-confidence motion).
  • Open question on the board: "what should I make of it when the INC demanded immediate Responsible Government?" (no answer given; check your notebook).
  • The Moderates believed in Constitutional Methods: they would use only the institutions and mechanisms that the State itself provided to redress grievances. Today's equivalents:
  • Fighting elections, using the Legislature, approaching the Judiciary
  • Filing RTI applications, using CPGRAMS, writing to Citizen Charters
  • sending petitions to the government.
Two ways of pressing the State: Moderate against Gandhi
Teacher's board
  • Constitutional methods = use of grievance-redressal mechanisms provided by the State (State = L, E, J): FIR, Court case, RTI, CPGRAMS, NOTA, fight elections, Citizen Charter. Topic: Good Governance (Public Administration).
  • Methods map: (1) Constitutional [non-violent, legal].
  • (1.2) Extra-constitutional:

  • (2) Protests: violent ones were not by Mahatma Gandhi, peaceful ones were.
  • (3) MG methods = non-violent extra-constitutional mass struggle [Satyagraha]: Non-Cooperation (not illegal, not unconstitutional: boycott of British goods, elections) and Civil Disobedience (illegal, unconstitutional).
Teacher's board
  • Civil-servant lens:

  • Heroes flip:
    • Pre-1947 heroes = Revolutionaries, Extremists, Mahatma Gandhi (MG) (against the colonial State)
    • Post-1947 heroes = Moderates, therefore the Nation State.

1.5 Did the Moderates oppose British rule?

  • The Moderates knew British rule was exploitative: they were not naive.
  • But they believed in the Providence of British Rule: if sufficiently pressurised, the British could be made to modernise India.
  • Their goal was therefore not independence but Limited Self-Government: a greater Indian share in governance within British rule.
  • Why so limited? Because they believed the masses, without widespread Western education, were not yet ready for full self-rule.
  • Their ultimate long-term vision (rarely stated loudly): Self-Government like Canada or Australia, with full British citizenship.
  • They sought not the removal of British rule but the removal of "Un-British Rule" (Surendranath Banerjea): the gap between Britain's own stated liberal values and its actual conduct in India.
  • The first President of the INC, W.C.
  • Bonnerjee, declared that the INC was "loyal to the British and well-wishers of the British."
Teacher's board
  • Keywords:
    • "Providence of British Rule"
    • "if" "pressurised" "the British could be made to modernise India" (the whole faith rests on the if)
    • The Canada or Australia vision
    • The "loyal and well-wishers" line
    • National unity, Capitalism and Industrialisation on British lines.
  • [Dadabhai Naoroji's book = Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901)] sits beside "Un-British Rule".

1.7 Secular, pro-industry, pro-capitalism

  • The Moderates were secular in orientation and actively promoted national unity.
  • They believed in Capitalism and Industrialisation on British lines as the path to Indian prosperity.
Teacher's board
  • Why capitalism? Before 1929, all examples of IR (industrial revolution) came via capitalists (the private sector).
  • Japan from 1868 went through IR as "State aided Capitalism."

3 · Weaknesses of the Moderates and the INC#

Story link

3.1 Uneven representation and the exclusion of non-elites

  • The most serious structural weakness of the early INC was uneven representation and the total exclusion of non-elites.
  • It was dominated by Western-educated professionals: lawyers, doctors, merchants and bankers, journalists, teachers, social reformers and landowners.
  • Most members came from the three Presidencies (Bombay, Madras, Calcutta)
  • the rest of India was hugely underrepresented.
  • Even within the Presidencies, Bombay was over-represented, contributing roughly half the members, followed by Madras.
  • Bengal, despite having its own active political organisation (the Indian Association, 1876), sent only about 5 members to the first INC session, and only after being informed at the last moment.
  • Until 1905, most INC members were high-caste Hindus: about 40% Brahmins and the rest from other upper castes.
  • Impact: the INC's claim to represent the whole nation was weak, and a weak claim of representativeness hurt the success of every INC programme.

3.2 Low Muslim participation

  • Very few Muslims participated in the early INC.
  • Badruddin Tyabji of Bombay became the first Muslim President of the INC at the Madras session of 1887: a notable exception.
  • In the period 1892-1905, Hindus were 90% of members and Muslims only about 6.5%.
  • Within the Hindu membership, Brahmins alone were roughly 40%.
  • (The slide: the remainder were non-Brahmin Hindus, many upper caste, plus Muslims, Parsis and Christians.)
  • Muslim participation dropped dramatically after the 1890s, and the INC made no concerted effort to reach out to the Muslim community.
  • There was arrogance in this: a belief that the absence of a strong Muslim political party until 1906 meant no urgent effort was needed.
  • When cow-killing riots broke out in 1893, the INC stayed silent and did not condemn the violence.
Teacher's board
  • Tyabji: Madras session (1887). The 1886 rule: Calcutta session, President = Dadabhai Naoroji.
  • Census: Muslim population = 20%, against the INC's 6.5%: one-fifth of the people but about one-fifteenth of the INC.

3.3 The 1886 rule: avoiding social questions

Story link

Because the INC wanted unity above all, it chose its fights carefully. Ask: why did it pick only Indian-versus-British issues, and what did that cost?

  • To maintain unity, the INC took the pragmatic but damaging decision to avoid all social questions (caste, religion, social reform) until 1907. This was deliberate.
  • Formal political resolutions (governance, civil services, economic demands) were the arena of the Congress.
  • Communally sensitive social reform questions (caste, religion, personal law) were kept off the agenda because they risked splitting the fragile Hindu-Muslim-Parsi coalition before it had properly formed.
  • The logic:
    • National political unity had to be built first
    • raising caste or religious reform in the INC would invite the very divisions the British hoped to exploit.

3.4 Commitment and social background

  • Were the Moderates committed politicians? The commitment problem was real.
  • Moderate leaders were mostly part-time politicians, with lucrative legal practices and other professional commitments.
  • The INC was organisationally weak: it functioned more like an annual conference lasting three days than a genuine political party.
  • (Handout analogy: the INC was the trade fair, not a company with a permanent office, year-round staff and a strategic plan.)
  • Did their social background affect their politics? Yes.
  • In 1892-1905 the INC membership was about 20% landlords, 40% lawyers and 15% traders, most from landed families. This shaped their positions:
  • 1. No anti-Zamindar stand. They wanted PS 1793 extended outside Bengal, which was partly a fig leaf for protecting Zamindar interests: PS 1793 prevented the State from raising Land Revenue and so protected Zamindars from higher taxation.
  • 2. They opposed the Punjab Land Alienation Bill of 1900, designed to stop indebted peasants from losing their land to moneylenders.
  • It would have constrained land transfers, inconvenient for their class.
  • 3. They did not champion working-class interests against Indian capitalists.
  • Their stated position: only Indian Capitalism would remove poverty.
  • They actively supported the workers of Assam Tea Gardens, but only because the plantation owners there were European.
  • For labour reforms that would help Indian workers at the cost of Indian capitalists, they opposed them consistently.

3.5 The Lancashire Lobby and the Factory and Mines Acts

Story link

Because leaders were landlords, lawyers and traders, their class interest met a British trade interest. Ask: was the opposition to the factory laws wrong, or only complex?

  • The Lancashire Lobby argument. The British were not passing these labour laws out of sympathy for Indian workers.
  • British textile manufacturers in Lancashire were heavily lobbying for factory regulations in India, because stricter rules would raise Indian production costs and reduce the competitiveness of Indian textiles against British goods in global markets. The Moderates understood this game.
  • They argued that India's infant industries needed room to grow, and that heavy regulation imposed prematurely would crush indigenous industrialisation before it had begun, keeping India permanently a supplier of raw materials (and an export market for British manufactured goods) rather than a manufacturing economy.

Teacher's board
  • Tariff example. British manufactured goods entering India: British manufacturers lobbied for down or zero tariffs.
  • A British good of Rs 100 with 50% import duty sells at Rs 150, against an Indian good at Rs 130, so the Indian good wins ("if India has been independent", i.e. able to set its own duty).
  • Britain: an Indian good at 100 pounds with 50% import duty becomes 150 pounds, against a British good at 130 pounds: the British good wins at home. Tariffs protect whoever sets them.

  • The 1881 Act is the one to know ("famous, so important"): 100 workers, under 7 banned, 7-12 limited to 9 hours, machinery fenced, plus 4 holidays a month.
  • The 1891 and 1901 Acts are not imp: know them only as the expansion (50 workers; ages 9 and 14; women 11 hours) and the first Mines Act.
Handout note
  • The handout's closing remark on these Acts (the slide prints it as a box): whether the Moderates were right or wrong to oppose them is a complex question.
  • The Lancashire Lobby motive was real, the British were not philanthropists, but the consequence of Moderate opposition was that Indian workers, including children, continued to labour under harsh conditions for longer than necessary.

3.6 Why Moderate politics failed: the two structural problems

  • The failure came from two structural problems that reinforced each other:
Structural problemDetail
1. They did not represent the massesRoughly 90% Hindu, overwhelmingly drawn from the lawyer and landlord classes: not a cross-section of India, but a sliver
2. They distrusted the massesThey refused to mobilise them, fearing that popular agitation would turn violent and become an uncontrolled mass movement
ConsequenceA movement that neither represented the people nor threatened to unleash them carried no real pressure. The government could easily ignore petitions from a few hundred educated professionals with no mass base. Without the threat of popular disruption, the British could easily ignore the Moderates

4.1 The label: "Strategy of Mendicancy"

Teacher's board

Critics, and later the Extremists, called the Moderate methods the "Strategy of Mendicancy," that is, begging: the methods created little pressure on the British.

4.2 Defence of the Moderates (Gokhale, 1913)

  • To be fair to them, Gokhale noted in 1913 that had the Moderates not followed these methods, the British would not have allowed the formation of the INC at all.
  • And the culture of direct confrontation with the colonial government had not yet developed.
  • A movement that begins with petition and prayer is not necessarily weak: it is building the institutions and culture of politics for a country that had never had a national political organisation.
  • The INC of 1885 was the beginning of something, not the end.

Factories Act 1891 provisions?Factories with 50 or more workers; minimum age 7 to 9; child limit 12 to 14; women's hours capped at 11 a day with a 1.5-hour rest.
What methods did the Moderates use?Prayers and petitions, resolutions at annual sessions, the press and debates; no mass action, no street agitation; gradualism. The teacher: 3P = Prayer, Petitions, Protests in press.
What is the "Strategy of Mendicancy"?The critics' (and later Extremists') label, meaning begging, for Moderate methods that created little pressure on the British.
Why did Moderate methods fail (two structural problems)?(1) They did not represent the masses (90% Hindu, lawyers and landlords); (2) they distrusted the masses and refused to mobilise them; so no pressure and the British could ignore their petitions.
What was Gokhale's defence in 1913?Without these methods the British would not have allowed the INC at all; the culture of direct confrontation had not yet developed; a petition-and-prayer movement was building the institutions and culture of politics.
Mains PYQ: why did the Moderates fail to carry conviction by the end of the 19th century?Intro: Limited SG by petitions, none of 11 demands met. Body: weak claim (UC-WEMC from M, B, C; Muslims 6.5%), no trust in masses, weak (class-coloured) demands, weak methods (3P, gradual, part-time, 3-day drama). Closing: neither represented nor threatened the people, so they could be ignored; yet Gokhale 1913: they were laying the foundation.
Prelims 2000 A-R on the narrow social base: answer and reason?(c): A true (uneven representation, exclusion of non-elites); R false (members were elites but the demands: land revenue, salt tax, Arms Act, jury, were for all). The 1886 rule: Indian-versus-Indian issues not picked up.
