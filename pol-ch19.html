1 · Unitary against federal systems#

Governments are classed by how power is shared between the national and regional levels.

Unitary

  • The central authority holds supreme power
  • all administrative and legislative powers sit with the national government
  • regional or local bodies are subordinate agencies, do delegated duties, and cannot act independently or claim constitutional status
  • examples: United Kingdom, France, Japan, China, Italy

Federal

  • Constitutional division of powers between two distinct levels, national (central or union) and regional (state or provincial)
  • both derive authority directly from the Constitution and act autonomously in their spheres
  • neither is subordinate to the other in its own field
  • examples: United States, Canada, Switzerland, Australia, Russia, Brazil, Argentina

In a federation the national authority is the Federal or Union Government; the sub-national units are called States, Provinces or Republics.

The nine-point comparison
#BasisFederal governmentUnitary government
1Structure of authorityTwo distinct levels, national and regional; both derive power from the ConstitutionOne central authority with supreme control; regional bodies, if created, are subordinate to the centre
2Source of powerThe Constitution divides powers, ensuring autonomy for both within their jurisdictionsThe national government is the sole source of authority and may delegate or withdraw powers at its discretion
3Nature of ConstitutionWritten and codified, with functions and powers of each level clearly specifiedWritten or unwritten: written in France, unwritten in the UK
4Distribution of powersConstitutionally distributed by clear demarcation of subjectsNo constitutional division; national legislature has comprehensive authority
5SupremacyThe Constitution is supreme; both levels act within its frameworkParliament or national legislature is supreme; the Constitution may or may not have higher authority (UK)
6AmendmentsRigid; cannot be amended easily without participation of both national and regional governmentsGenerally flexible; the centre can amend without consulting regional entities
7Role of judiciaryIndependent; safeguards the Constitution, settles Centre-State disputes, upholds rule of lawMay or may not be independent; in some unitary states it works under the legislature or executive
8Legislative structureUsually bicameral: one house represents the people, the other the constituent unitsUnicameral or bicameral, depending on tradition and design
9ExamplesUnited States, Canada, Australia, India, SwitzerlandUnited Kingdom, France, Japan, China, Italy
Quick contrast on dependence
FederalUnitary
RelationIndependent of each other; coordinate with each otherStates dependent on the Union; subordinate to the Union

Origin of the word

  • Federation comes from the Latin foedus: a treaty or covenant.
  • Historically, several units or provinces agreed to unite under a common constitution while keeping some self-rule: a new arrangement by mutual consent, balancing unity with regional autonomy.
  • Units are named States (USA), Cantons (Switzerland), Provinces (Canada), Republics (Russia): all share the principle of constitutionally guaranteed powers.
Federal traits
SDWSRJB

Structure two-tier, Distribution of powers, Written constitution, Supremacy of Constitution, Rigidity, Judiciary independent, Bicameral: "Some Dancers Wear Silver Red Jackets, Bravo!"

Unitary versus federal

Federal = two levels, both from the Constitution, rigid, written. Unitary = one centre, delegates downward, flexible.

2 · India's model: a Union with a federal structure#

India is a "Union of States" with a federal structure and strong unitary features. The Constitution creates a dual polity (Union and States) but adds single citizenship, a strong Centre in some matters and emergency powers, so Indian federalism is flexible rather than rigid. This hybrid helps keep national unity while allowing regional autonomy.

How federations arise

Integration (coming together)

  • Several independent or semi-independent states, often smaller, join to form a larger entity
  • USA: thirteen colonies came together in 1787 after the American Revolution (1775-1783), now fifty states

Disintegration (holding together)

  • A previously unitary state becomes a federation by granting constitutional powers to its provinces
  • Canada: four provinces in 1867, now ten

Why India chose federalism

  • Vast geographical expanse and socio-cultural diversity (many languages, religions, traditions) make decentralised governance essential.
  • Aim: efficient governance and harmony of national unity with regional autonomy.

"Union of States"

  • The Constitution does not use the word "federation"; Art. 1 says "Union of States".
  • Ambedkar said the wording was intentional: India's federation is not an agreement among sovereign states as in the USA, and the units cannot secede.
  • So the Indian Union is indestructible: a Centre strong enough to protect integrity, with States autonomous in their constitutional spheres.

Canada, not the USA

  • India is compared to Canada: formed by the disintegration of a larger unitary state (the British colonial administration), then powers were granted to provinces.
  • The Constitution has a centralising tendency: residuary powers, state reorganisation, emergency provisions.
  • Both India and Canada use the term Union to stress an indissoluble federation.
  • The US model is a treaty among independent states with stronger safeguards for state sovereignty.

3 · Seven federal features#

Federal features of the Constitution
#FeatureContent
1Dual polityTwo spheres: Union at the centre, States at the periphery, each independent in its domain. Union: defence, foreign affairs, currency, communications, inter-state trade (Art. 246, Union List). States: public order, agriculture, health, local governance, police (State List)
2Written ConstitutionOriginally Preamble, 395 Articles, 8 Schedules; now (2025) Preamble, about 470 Articles in 25 Parts, 12 Schedules. Defines structure, powers, limits of each level and reduces conflict
3Division of powersSeventh Schedule: Union List (Parliament only, currently 98), State List (State legislatures, currently 59), Concurrent List (both, currently 52). Concurrent conflict: Union law prevails (Art. 254). Residuary powers with the Union (Art. 248)
4Supremacy of the ConstitutionBinds Union and States; laws or acts that violate it may be struck down by the Supreme Court or High Courts through judicial review (Arts. 13, 32, 226)
5RigidityAmendments affecting federal features (Centre-State relations, powers of the judiciary, legislative competencies) need a special majority in Parliament and ratification by at least half of the State legislatures (Art. 368)
6Independent judiciaryHeaded by the Supreme Court; dual role: protects supremacy of the Constitution by judicial review, and decides disputes between Union and States or among States (Art. 131); security of tenure, fixed service conditions, protection against arbitrary removal
7Bicameral legislatureRajya Sabha represents the States, Lok Sabha the people; Rajya Sabha has less power but protects State interests, mainly when Union legislation touches State subjects (Arts. 80, 249)
Seven federal features
DWDSRIB

Dual polity, Written constitution, Division of powers, Supremacy, Rigidity, Independent judiciary, Bicameralism: "Dear Writers, Don't Skip Reading Indian Books".

Article anchors for federal features

Powers: 246 (Union List), 248 (residuary), 254 (conflict). Judiciary: 13, 32, 226, 131. Amendment: 368. House of States: 80, 249.

4 · Fourteen unitary features#

These make the Union far stronger than the States and give the label "quasi-federal" or "federation with a strong Centre".

Unitary features of the Constitution
#FeatureContent
1Strong central authorityUnion List has more subjects than State List; defence, foreign affairs, currency are with the Union (Art. 246); Union prevails in Concurrent conflicts (Art. 254); residuary powers with the Union (Art. 248)
2States not indestructibleParliament can alter area, boundaries or name of a State by simple majority (Art. 3); in Ambedkar's words the States are "destructible", the Union indestructible
3Single ConstitutionUnion and States work under one Constitution (Arts. 245, 246); only exception: Jammu and Kashmir, with its own Constitution until 2019
4Flexible amendmentMost provisions can be amended by Parliament alone (Art. 368); only amendments touching the Centre-State balance need ratification by at least half the States
5Unequal representationRajya Sabha seats are by population (Art. 80); larger States get more. The US Senate gives equal representation
6Emergency provisionsNational, State, Financial (Arts. 352-360); the Union gets legislative and executive authority over the States; federal structure becomes effectively unitary
7Single citizenshipArt. 5; unlike the US, Switzerland or Australia with national and state citizenship; same rights everywhere
8Integrated judiciarySupreme Court at the top, High Courts in States (Arts. 124-237); applies Union and State laws; unlike separate US federal and state courts
9All-India ServicesIAS, IPS, IFS (Arts. 312-323); recruited and controlled by the Union, deployed in States
10Centralised auditCAG audits Union and State accounts (Arts. 148-151); appointed by the President without State consultation
11Parliament over State ListParliament may legislate on a State List subject if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution declaring it in the national interest (Art. 249); no amendment needed
12Governor as Central agentAppointed by the President (Art. 155), holds office at his pleasure; may reserve bills for the President; US States elect their heads
13Integrated election machineryElection Commission conducts Union and State elections (Art. 324), constituted by the President
14Presidential veto over State billsThe Governor can reserve certain State bills for the President, who may withhold assent even upon re-submission (Art. 200): an absolute veto

5 · Forms of federalism#

Seven forms
FormMeaning
CooperativeUnion and States share functions and cooperate through coordination, joint programmes and institutions
CollaborativeUnion and States jointly design, implement and monitor policies through formal consultation
CompetitiveStates (and sometimes local bodies) compete for investment, talent and resources inside a national framework
FiscalDivision of taxing powers, expenditure responsibilities and transfers between levels
AsymmetricDifferent units have unequal powers, status or autonomy under one federal Constitution
Holding-togetherA large unitary state decentralises downward to keep unity (e.g. India)
Coming-togetherPreviously independent units voluntarily join and share sovereignty (e.g. USA)

6 · Asymmetric federalism#

India shows asymmetry on two axes: vertical (Centre against States) and horizontal (among States). Causes: historical compulsion, socio-cultural diversity and the need for unity within pluralism.

8 · Evaluating the federal character#

India's Constitution blends federal and unitary elements, so experts call it "quasi-federal".

Scholars and verdicts
ScholarView
K.C. WheareQuasi-federal: "a unitary state with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal state with subsidiary unitary features"
K. SanthanamFinancial dependence of States on Union grants and central planning (the erstwhile Planning Commission) centralised power; "India has practically functioned as a unitary state, though Union and States have tried to function legally as a federation"
Paul Appleby"Extremely federal"
Morris Jones"Bargaining federalism": neither Centre nor States can impose their will absolutely
Ivor Jennings"A federation with a strong centralising tendency"; also quoted as "mainly federal, with unique safeguards for enforcing national unity and growth"
Granville AustinCooperative federalism: Centre is strong but States are not weak or subordinate; "designed to meet India's peculiar needs"; federation built for diversity with strength at the Centre
AlexandrowiczIndia is sui generis: a unique case not fitting classical models
AmbedkarDual polity: Union and States co-created by the Constitution, deriving power directly from it (Arts. 245-255); neither mere administrative extensions nor sovereign units; the Constitution can work as unitary or federal depending on circumstances
