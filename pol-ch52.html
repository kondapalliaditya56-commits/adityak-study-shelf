In simple words, key terms and memory words#

In simple words

  • India has no national language. Part XVII (Arts. 343-351) deals with the Official Language. The Munshi-Ayyangar Formula was the compromise that avoided a 'national' language.
  • Hindi in Devanagari script is the official language of the Union (Art. 343) but international numerals are used. English continued for 15 years (till 1965) and Parliament may extend it.
  • The Official Languages Act, 1963 let English continue in addition to Hindi. The 1967 amendment says English cannot be dropped for Union-state and state-state talks until all non-Hindi states pass resolutions: a permanent veto.
  • The articles fall into four heads: Union (343-344), Regional (345-347), Judiciary and texts of laws (348-349) and Special directives (350-351).
  • English is the language of the Supreme Court and High Courts and of authoritative law texts until Parliament decides otherwise. A Governor, with the President's consent, can allow Hindi or the state language in High Court proceedings, but judgments and decrees stay in English.
  • The Eighth Schedule lists 22 languages (14 at first). There are now 11 classical languages after the October 2024 additions.

Key terms

1 · Why "Official" and not "National"#

  • Language was one of the most divisive issues after Independence. The "Munshi-Ayyangar Formula" was the compromise to avoid a national language and adopt an "Official Language". The Constitution-makers did not designate any "National Language" to preserve the federal fabric. Part XVII (Articles 343 to 351) deals exclusively with Official Languages.

Rationale and objectives ("Pragmatic Federalism")

GoalWhat it means
Administrative efficiencyA standard language between Union and States so governance does not stall in a "Babel of tongues"
National integrationA common identity through Hindi (Article 351) while avoiding the "majoritarian trap" by retaining English as an associate language
Preservation of diversityThe Eighth Schedule shows "Indian-ness" is not monolithic; a platform for regional literatures
Socio-political stabilityEnglish acts as a "safety valve" against linguistic sub-nationalism in non-Hindi regions, for example the anti-Hindi agitations of the 1960s
  • Memory aid (Four goals): Administrative efficiency, National integration, Preservation of diversity, Stability: "All Need Peaceful Speech."

2 · Historical context and the Munshi-Ayyangar Formula#

  • During drafting (1947-1949) the Assembly was deeply divided over the "National Language".

Three camps in the Assembly

  • Pro-Hindi group: Hindi in Devanagari script as the sole national language immediately
  • Regional or non-Hindi group:
    • Feared "imposition" of Hindi
    • Preferred English for stability or regional languages
  • Compromise seekers: Suggested "Hindustani" (a blend of Hindi and Urdu) as middle ground
  • To prevent a deadlock, K.M. Munshi and N. Gopalaswamy Ayyangar proposed a "political formula". It avoided the term "National Language" and focused on the "Official Language"
  • Its core pillars became Article 343

Features of the formula

FeatureDecision
Official LanguageHindi in the Devanagari script, official language of the Union
Transition periodEnglish continues for all official purposes for 15 years (until 1965)
NumeralsInternational form of Indian numerals (1, 2, 3...) rather than Devanagari numerals
Parliamentary powerAfter 15 years Parliament could legislate to allow continued use of English (which led to the Official Languages Act, 1963)
Munshi-Ayyangar Formula: pillars of Article 343
OPEN
  • OOfficial language: Hindi in the Devanagari script
  • PParliament may allow English even after 15 years
  • EEnglish continues for official purposes for 15 years (until 1965)
  • NNumerals: international form of Indian numerals

OPEN: the formula kept the door OPEN for English. Four pillars of Art 343; order is rearranged for memory. (Memory aid, not from the handout.)

Significance and legacy

  • Unity in diversity: a 15-year "cooling-off" period prevented a linguistic split.
  • Linguistic secularism: "Official" rather than "National" theoretically puts all Indian languages on an equal footing.
  • The 1963 extension: as the 15-year deadline neared, massive anti-Hindi protests (especially in Tamil Nadu) made the government use the formula's powers so English remained an "associate" official language indefinitely.
  • Today:
    • Hindi Diwas (14 September) commemorates the day in 1949 when the Constituent Assembly adopted this formula
    • The reason for a bilingual (Hindi and English) system in Central administration

How the compromise unfolded

  • 1947-1949: Assembly divided: Hindi, regional languages, Hindustani
  • Formula: Munshi and Ayyangar: Official Language, not National
  • Article 343:
    • Hindi official
    • English for 15 years
    • International numerals
  • 1963: Official Languages Act keeps English alongside Hindi
  • 1967: Amendment adds the non-Hindi states' veto
How the compromise unfolded

3 · Constitutional provisions (Part XVII)#

  • Four heads, A to D.

A. Language of the Union (Articles 343-344)

  • Article 343:
    • Hindi in Devanagari is the official language of the Union
    • Numerals are the international form of Indian numerals
  • Initial transition: English continues for all official purposes for 15 years (until 1965).
  • Article 343(3): Parliament may provide for continued use of English even after 15 years.
  • Article 344: appointment of a Commission and a Committee of Parliament to recommend progressive use of Hindi and restrictions on English.

B. Regional languages (Articles 345-347)

  • Article 345: a State Legislature may adopt any one or more languages in use in the state, or Hindi, as its official language (example: Andhra Pradesh chose Telugu).
  • Article 346: language for communication between States and between a State and the Union is the Union's official language (English or Hindi).
  • Article 347: on demand, the President may recognise a language spoken by a substantial section of a state's population for official purposes in that state.

C. Judiciary and texts of laws (Articles 348-349)

  • Article 348: until Parliament provides otherwise, English is the language for all proceedings in the Supreme Court and every High Court, and for authoritative texts of all Bills, Acts, Ordinances and Rules.
  • High Court exception: with the President's consent, a Governor can authorise Hindi or the state's official language in High Court proceedings, but judgments and decrees must still be in English.

D. Special directives (Articles 350-351)

  • Article 350: redress of grievances in any language used in the Union or State.
  • Article 350A: duty of the State to give facilities for instruction in mother tongue at the primary stage.
  • Article 350B: Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities, appointed by the President.
  • Article 351: directive to the Union to promote the spread and development of Hindi so it can serve as a medium for India's composite culture.

5 · Official Languages Act, 1963#

  • The Act ensures continued use of English alongside Hindi as the 15-year deadline (1965) approached
  • It addressed the anxiety of non-Hindi speaking states, particularly in South India. It fundamentally changed the "sunset clause" of English in the original Constitution

Key provisions

SectionProvision
Section 3English "may" continue in addition to Hindi for all official purposes of the Union and for business in Parliament after 26 January 1965
Section 3(3)Mandatory bilingualism: both Hindi and English for resolutions, general orders, rules and notifications; administrative and other reports; contracts, agreements, licences, permits and tender forms
Section 4Committee on Official Language: a Parliamentary Committee of 30 members to review progress of Hindi and report to the President
Section 5Authorised Hindi translation of Central Acts and Ordinances

The 1967 amendment: the "indefinite" extension

  • The 1963 Act used "may", and many feared it was too vague and Hindi could still be imposed. After violent anti-Hindi protests in 1965, the Act was amended in 1967.
  • The "Veto" provision: use of English for communication between the Union and states (or between states) cannot be discontinued until a resolution is passed by the Legislatures of all the states which have not adopted Hindi as their official language.
  • Effect: a permanent veto for non-Hindi speaking states over removing English.

Impact on administration (the "diglot" system)

AreaLanguage used
ParliamentBoth Hindi and English (with simultaneous translation)
Central Government officesFiles noted in either language; translations where necessary
Inter-State communicationEnglish is the link language for states that have not adopted Hindi
JudiciaryBasis for using Hindi or regional languages in High Courts (with Presidential consent)
  • Shortcut: OLA sections. 3 = English may continue, 3(3) = bilingual documents, 4 = Committee of 30, 5 = Hindi legal texts. Climb the numbers: permission, papers, committee, translation.

The veto

  • 1965: Violent anti-Hindi protests
  • 1967 amendment: Act amended
  • Condition: English use between Union and states, or between states, continues until every non-Hindi state's Legislature passes a resolution to end it
  • Result: Permanent veto for non-Hindi speaking states

6 · Significance and Mains perspective#

  • The Official Languages Act is described as the "statutory soul" of language policy: it moved India from a "Hindi-only" goal to a permanent bilingual reality, so linguistic diversity does not become a barrier to administration or careers for non-Hindi speakers.
  • Official versus national: India has no national language, a deliberate "Status Quo" so no linguistic group feels marginalised.
  • Classical language status: criteria are high antiquity (1500-2000 years), a body of ancient literature and an original literary tradition.
  • Federalism:
    • Language is a "fault line"
    • The Three-Language Formula (Regional, Hindi, English) aims to bridge it but faces implementation challenges

7 · Recent trends (2024-25) and way forward#

  • Linguistic policy has shifted from "translation" to "technological empowerment".

Recent developments

ItemDetail
Classical languages (Oct 2024)Union Cabinet added Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali; total 11; honours heritages beyond Sanskrit and Tamil
Project BhashiniUnder the National Language Translation Mission; AI real-time translation in all 22 scheduled languages; bridges the digital divide
Judiciary in vernacularSupreme Court (through the e-SCR portal) and High Courts translate judgments into regional languages; former CJI DY Chandrachud: "justice must be accessible in the language of the common man"
Educational reform (NEP 2020)Bharatiya Bhasha Pustak Scheme (2025): textbooks for technical and higher education in Indian languages, challenging the hegemony of English

Way forward

  • Harmonious implementation:
    • No "imposing" Hindi
    • Promote Hindi as a link language by enrichment from other Indian languages (Article 351)
  • Technological leverage: scale AI like Bhashini so language never blocks access to schemes like Direct Benefit Transfers.
  • Strengthen the Three-Language Formula:
    • Implement in spirit
    • North Indians learn South Indian languages and vice versa
  • Empower the Eighth Schedule: substantial funding for scheduled languages to develop modern scientific and legal terminology.
  • Conclusion: Language policy is a "delicate social contract", balancing the functional need for a link language with the constitutional duty to protect linguistic minorities. Success depends on making multilingualism an asset for governance rather than a liability.
  • Memory aid (Way forward four): Harmonious Hindi, Technology (Bhashini), Strengthen three-language formula, Empower Eighth Schedule: "Help Them Speak Easily."

    • In 1955 the President appointed the Official Language Commission under B.G. Kher (report 1956)

Traps#

Trap 1 · Official versus national language The Constitution-makers deliberately gave no 'National Language' to protect the federal fabric. Hindi is only the Union's official language. Rule: India has an official language, not a national language.
Trap 2 · How the Eighth Schedule grew to 22 14 original languages, then Sindhi (21st Amendment, 1967), Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali (71st, 1992), and Bodo, Dogri, Maithili and Santhali (92nd, 2003). Rule: 14 + 1 + 3 + 4 = 22.
Trap 3 · High Court language With the President's consent a Governor can authorise Hindi or the state language for High Court proceedings, but judgments and decrees must still be in English. Rule: proceedings can change; judgments and decrees stay in English.
Trap 4 · Six or eleven classical languages The original six are Tamil, Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam and Odia. In October 2024 Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali were added. Rule: 6 + 5 = 11 classical languages.
