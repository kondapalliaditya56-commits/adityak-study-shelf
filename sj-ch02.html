1 · Children AS A Vulnerable Section · Status · National Policy 2013 · Juvenile Justice · Pocso#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2016 (National Child Policy provisions and implementation) · Social Justice handout pp. 12-15. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Children are 39% of the population and the largest vulnerable group because they depend entirely on adults. The framework is layered: Articles 21A, 24, 39(e)-(f), 45, 51A(k); National Policy on Children 2013 (below 18, four priorities); JJ Act 2015; POCSO 2012 with the 2019 and 2020 additions; and the 2024 Supreme Court ruling that possessing CSEAM is itself an offence.

Status AND Challenges OF Children IN India

Key point

Children form 39% of India's population, making them the largest vulnerable group. Their vulnerability is amplified by complete dependence on adults and inability to advocate for themselves.

Key challenges

  • Malnutrition: 29.3% of children under 5 are stunted (NFHS 6).
  • Out of school children: estimated at over 60 lakh (UDISE).
  • Child labour: 1.01 crore children aged 5 to 14 are working (Census 2011).
  • Child marriage continues despite legal prohibition.
  • Sexual abuse: POCSO cases keep rising.
  • Mental health crisis: 23% of Indian school children face mental health issues (NIMHANS).
  • Digital harms: 60% of children at risk of digital addiction.
  • Climate vulnerability: 24.1 million children affected annually by climate disasters (UNICEF).

Constitutional Safeguards FOR Children

Key point

India has a layered framework of constitutional, statutory and policy instruments for child protection.

National Policy ON Children, 2013 PYQ 2016

Quote

PYQ 2016 (12.5 marks): Examine the main provisions of the National Child Policy and throw light on the status of its implementation.

Key point

The 2013 policy replaced the 1974 policy and recognises every person below 18 as a child.

Key provisions

  • Defines children as assets and citizens with rights, not as property.
  • Lists survival, health, nutrition, education, development, protection and participation as the key priorities (the handout says four key priorities and lists these).
  • Mandates a National Plan of Action for Children.
  • Establishes a National Coordination and Action Group for monitoring.
  • Calls for inter-sectoral coordination across health, education and social welfare.

Status of implementation

  • National Plan of Action for Children 2016 operationalised the policy.
  • ICDS, POSHAN Abhiyaan, Samagra Shiksha, Mission Vatsalya, Mission Saksham Anganwadi form the delivery network.
  • Implementation gaps remain in convergence between line departments.
  • Budgetary allocation for children remains below the recommended 5% of Union Budget.
  • NCPCR and SCPCRs are statutory bodies for monitoring but suffer from understaffing.
Value addition

Handout inconsistency: it says the policy lists "four key priorities" but names seven items. Reproduce the seven named items in an answer.

Juvenile Justice System

Key point

The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 governs children in conflict with law and children in need of care and protection.

Key provisions

  • Defines child as anyone below 18 years.
  • Allows trial of 16 to 18 year olds as adults in heinous offences after Juvenile Justice Board assessment (post-Nirbhaya amendment).
  • Establishes JJBs, Child Welfare Committees, Special Juvenile Police Units.
  • Provides for adoption through CARA.
  • 2021 Amendment gave CWCs stronger oversight on Child Care Institutions.

Concerns

  • Adult-like trial of 16 to 18 year olds raises ethical questions.
  • Many CCIs are unregistered and unsafe.
  • Shortage of trained personnel and infrastructure.

Pocso ACT AND Rules 2020

Key point

The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012 is India's primary law against child sexual abuse.

Key features

  • Gender-neutral definition of child as anyone below 18.
  • Three categories of offences: sexual assault, sexual harassment, child pornography.
  • Special Courts for speedy trial.
  • 2019 Amendment introduced the death penalty for aggravated penetrative sexual assault.
  • Mandates child-friendly procedures for recording statements.

POCSO Rules 2020

  • Mandatory police verification of school and care home staff.
  • Procedures for reporting child sexual abuse material.
  • State governments to formulate a child protection policy of zero tolerance.
  • Periodic training and sensitisation for all persons in contact with children.
  • Age-appropriate educational material on personal safety.
In the news (Saarthi IAS CA; verify dates)

Supreme Court 2024 on CSEAM: overturned a Madras HC ruling and held that possession and storage of Child Sexual Exploitative and Abuse Material (CSEAM) is itself an offence under POCSO. The Court recommended changing terminology from "child pornography" to CSEAM.

Contemporary Issues Concerning Children

Child labour

  • 1.01 crore working children aged 5 to 14 (Census 2011) and approximately 10.1 million as per ILO-UNICEF estimates.
  • India ratified ILO Conventions 138 and 182 in 2017.
  • Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016 bans all child labour below 14 and hazardous work for 14 to 18.
  • PENCIL portal for online complaints and rehabilitation.
  • National Child Labour Project for rehabilitation through bridge schools.

Child marriage

  • 23.3% of women aged 20 to 24 married before 18 (NFHS 5).
  • Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006 declares such marriages voidable.
  • Prohibition of Child Marriage (Amendment) Bill, 2021 proposes raising women's marriage age from 18 to 21.
  • Districts in Bihar, Jharkhand, West Bengal and Rajasthan show highest prevalence.
Note

Cross-check: NFHS 6 (Health chapter) shows the share fell to 20.1% from 23.3% (NFHS 5), which is the figure the Women chapter uses. Always quote the survey round with the number.

Child Sexual Exploitative and Abuse Material (CSEAM)

  • The Supreme Court's 2024 verdict criminalising mere possession of CSEAM is a major step.
  • SC recommendations:
    • Comprehensive sex education in schools
    • Expert committee for POCSO awareness
    • Victim rehabilitation and offender treatment
    • Jharkhand's Udaan initiative cited as a model
  • Other legal measures:
    • IT Act 2000 with stringent penalties
    • Intermediary Guidelines 2021 mandating tech platforms to detect CSEAM
    • Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023

Children in street situations

  • NCPCR's CiSS Application under the Baal Swaraj Portal helps in identification and rehabilitation.
  • Bal Mitra initiatives in cities like Bhubaneswar and Indore.
  • Need for shelter, education and family reunification.

Children with disabilities in anganwadis

  • New programme to integrate children with disabilities in anganwadis launched in 2024.
  • Aims for early identification and intervention.
  • Requires special training of anganwadi workers and accessibility upgrades.

Mental health of students and adolescents

  • National Mental Health Survey 2015-16 found 7.3% prevalence of psychiatric disorders among 13 to 17 year olds.
  • NIMHANS study found 23% Indian school children with mental health problems.
  • 27.8% undergraduate and 31.3% postgraduate medical students suffer from mental health issues.
  • UMMEED Draft Guidelines for schools to prevent student suicides.
  • Manodarpan initiative for psychological support.
  • National Task Force on Mental Health and Wellbeing of Medical Students submitted its report in 2024.

3 · Youth AND Adolescents · National Youth Policy 2014 · Challenges · Schemes#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Social Justice handout p. 16. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • Youth are those aged 15 to 29, nearly 27% of the population. The demographic gift is also a policy challenge: unemployment above 17%, a skill gap (2-4% formal training against 52% in the USA), mental health, substance abuse and migration stress. Schemes: RYSK, NYKS, NSS, MY Bharat, Skill India, Khelo India, Nasha Mukt Bharat, Yuva Sangam.

Challenges Faced BY Young Adults

Key point

The National Youth Policy 2014 defines youth as those between 15 and 29 years. Youth comprise nearly 27% of India's population, making the country one of the youngest in the world. This demographic gift is also a policy challenge.

Key challenges

  • Unemployment and underemployment: youth unemployment is over 17% (PLFS 2023-24).
  • Skill gap: only around 2 to 4% of workforce has formal skill training against 52% in the USA.
  • Mental health crisis with rising anxiety, depression and suicides.
  • Substance abuse: National Drug Survey found 3.1 crore Indians use cannabis and 2.26 crore opioids.
  • Digital addiction and social media harms.
  • Identity and radicalisation risks.
  • Marriage and family pressures, especially on young women.
  • Migration stress as youth move to cities for jobs without social support.

4 · Elderly AND Senior Citizens · Status · Inter-generational GAP · Maintenance ACT 2007 · Schemes#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: Medium. Mains PYQs quoted: None quoted in the handout · Social Justice handout pp. 16-18. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • India is ageing fast: 10.4% are 60 plus and will exceed 20% by 2050. The elderly face economic dependence, 78% with no pension, health gaps, digital illiteracy and isolation as nuclear families replace joint ones. The framework: Articles 41 and 47, the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007, policies of 1999 and 2011; Budget 2026-27 expands PM-JAY to 70 plus.

Status AND KEY Challenges OF THE Elderly

Key point

India is ageing fast. Senior citizens (60 plus) comprise around 10.4% of population and will exceed 20% by 2050 (UNFPA 2023). The 70 plus population has doubled from 2.8% in 2001 to 4.3% in 2021 and is expected to double again by 2031.

Key challenges

  • Economic dependency: 70% of elderly depend on family or relatives (NITI Aayog).
  • Pension exclusion: nearly 78% remain without any pension coverage.
  • Health vulnerabilities including non-communicable diseases, dementia and depression.
  • Lack of geriatric care infrastructure with very few trained geriatricians.
  • Digital illiteracy: only 1 in 5 elderly are digitally literate.
  • Rural-urban disparity in care access.
  • Gender inequality: elderly women face higher widowhood, poverty and isolation.
  • Health insurance gap: only one-fifth of 60 plus covered by insurance before PM-JAY expansion.

Inter-generational Communication GAP

Modern life has created a widening communication gap between elderly and younger generations

  • Nuclear families replace joint families leaving the elderly isolated.
  • Digital divide: elderly cannot use smartphones to connect.
  • Different value systems lead to mutual frustration.
  • Migration of working-age children to cities and abroad.
  • Cultural shift from elder reverence to individualism.
Way forward / key point

Intergenerational programmes such as adopt-a-grandparent initiatives, school visits to old age homes and digital literacy camps by youth volunteers help bridge this gap.

Senior CARE Reforms AND Schemes

Constitutional and legal framework

  • Article 41 directs the State to provide public assistance in old age.
  • Article 47 for raising standard of living and health.
  • Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 mandates children to maintain parents.
  • National Policy on Older Persons 1999 and National Policy for Senior Citizens 2011.

Geriatric care ecosystem and caregiver training (Budget 2026-27)

  • Building a Care Ecosystem covering geriatric and allied care services.
  • Training of 1.5 lakh multi-skilled caregivers under NSQF.
  • Expansion of PM-JAY for 70 plus senior citizens, offering Rs 5 lakh cover.
  • New NIMHANS 2 and upgrading mental health institutes at Ranchi and Tezpur.
  • Emergency and trauma care centres at district hospitals.

5 · Persons WITH Disabilities · RPWD ACT 2016 · Empowerment AND Sensitisation GAPS · Supreme Court · Schemes#

Handout only · Saarthi IAS
Exam lens

Priority: High. Mains PYQs quoted: Mains 2017 (RPwD Act and empowerment), 2022 (Act as only a legal document without sensitisation) · Social Justice handout pp. 18-21. Prelims PYQs quoted: none quoted in the material.

In simple words
  • PwDs are 2.21% (2.68 crore) per Census 2011, though advocates say 5-8%. The RPwD Act 2016 (UNCRPD-aligned) raised recognised disabilities from 7 to 21, set 4% job and 5% higher-education reservation and made accessibility and reasonable accommodation legal duties. It is strong on rights but weak on implementation and sensitisation; the Supreme Court has pushed accessibility rules and the social model.

Persons WITH Disabilities: Status

Key point

PwDs comprise around 2.21% of population, approximately 2.68 crore (Census 2011). Disability rights advocates argue the real figure is far higher and may reach 5 to 8% if global standards of measurement are used.

Profile

  • 69% of PwDs live in rural areas.
  • 55% are illiterate.
  • 44% are women.
  • Only 34% are workers.
  • Employment in the private sector remains less than 1% despite quotas.
  • WHO estimates 1 billion people globally experience some form of disability.

Rights OF Persons WITH Disabilities ACT, 2016

Key point

The RPwD Act 2016 replaced the older 1995 Act and brought Indian law in line with the UN Convention on Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD), ratified by India in 2007.

Key provisions

  • Expanded recognised disabilities from 7 to 21 types, including acid attack survivors, mental illness, learning disabilities, dwarfism and Parkinson's disease.
  • Defines Person with Benchmark Disability as those with 40% or more disability.
  • Reservation of 4% in government jobs and 5% in higher education.
  • Right to free education for every child with benchmark disability aged 6 to 18.
  • Mandates accessibility in public buildings, transport and ICT.
  • Chief Commissioner for PwDs at the Centre and State Commissioners as monitoring bodies.
  • National Fund and State Funds for financial support.
  • Special Courts and Special Public Prosecutors for offences.

Number card

  • 7 to 21 disabilities
  • 40% benchmark
  • 4% jobs
  • 5% higher education
  • ages 6-18 free education
  • UNCRPD ratified 2007
  • replaced 1995 Act.

Empowerment AND Inclusion Mechanisms PYQ 2017

Quote

PYQ 2017 (10 marks): Does the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 ensure effective mechanism for empowerment and inclusion of the intended beneficiaries in the society. Discuss.

Strengths

  • Rights-based approach moving from charity to entitlement.
  • Wider recognition of disabilities.
  • Reasonable accommodation as a legal duty.
  • Reservation in jobs and education.
  • Inclusive education mandate.
  • Accessibility standards for buildings and ICT.

Implementation gaps

  • Sugamya Bharat Abhiyan missed several deadlines for accessible buildings.
  • Backlog of vacancies in PwD-reserved posts in central ministries.
  • Lack of trained special educators in schools.
  • Most courts and police stations remain inaccessible.
  • Slow pace in framing accessibility standards under Section 40.

Sensitisation GAPS PYQ 2022

Quote

PYQ 2022 (10 marks): The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 remains only a legal document without intense sensitisation of government functionaries and citizens regarding disability. Comment.

Where sensitisation fails

  • Government functionaries including police, judges and frontline staff lack disability awareness training.
  • Citizens continue to use derogatory terms like "handicapped" and view PwDs through pity.
  • Schools and teachers are not equipped for inclusive education.
  • Employers see PwDs as liabilities rather than talent.
  • Media portrays PwDs through stereotypes.

Supreme Court interventions

  • Rajive Raturi v. Union of India (2024): directed the Centre to frame mandatory rules under Section 40 for accessibility.
  • Madhya Pradesh Judicial Services case: struck down Rule 6A disqualifying visually impaired candidates.
  • Nipun Malhotra v. Sony Pictures (Aankh Micholi case): issued a seven-point media framework against derogatory portrayal of PwDs.
  • The SC emphasised the principle of "Nothing About Us Without Us".
Way forward / key point

The Court has affirmed the social model of disability, which places the burden of change on society, not on the individual with disability.

Schemes AND Initiatives FOR Divyangjan

Key point

The term Divyangjan itself is a sensitivity reform aimed at removing stigma and recognising the divine abilities within.

Budget 2026-27: two flagship schemes

  • Divyangjan Kaushal Yojana:
    • Dignified livelihood opportunities through industry-relevant and customised training specific to disability groups
    • Skills aligned with each disability type to ensure employability
  • Divyang Sahara Yojana:
    • Timely access to high-quality assistive devices for all eligible PwDs
    • Supports the Artificial Limbs Manufacturing Corporation of India (ALIMCO) to scale up production
    • Integration of AI and R&D in assistive technology

PM Divyasha Kendras and assistive devices

  • PM Divyasha Kendras to be strengthened as modern retail-style centres for assistive devices.
  • ADIP Scheme (Assistance to Disabled Persons for Purchase of Aids and Appliances) provides financial support.
  • Sugamya Bharat App allows users to report inaccessible spaces.

Recent amendments (RPwD Rules 2024)

  • Colour-coded UDID cards with white (below 40%), yellow (40 to 80%) and blue (above 80%) bands.
  • Processing time of 3 months for disability certificates.
  • Online application through the UDID portal with Aadhaar.
  • Accessibility standards extended to police stations, prisons and disaster mitigation centres.
Way forward / key point

The path forward requires moving from legal recognition to lived dignity through sensitisation, accessibility audits, mainstreaming PwDs in workplaces and respecting the Nothing About Us Without Us principle: no policy, law or programme should be created or decided for a marginalised group without the full and direct participation of its members.
